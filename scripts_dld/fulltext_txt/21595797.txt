                  VIM-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae (VPKP) has been identified as a source of hospital outbreaks and is prevalent particularly in the Mediterranean region. In this study we have characterized eight VPKP isolates identified in Scandinavia during 2005–2008. With the exception of one isolate, all were from patients with recent history of hospitalization abroad (Greece, n = 6; Turkey, n = 1). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) resulted in five sequence types (STs), ST36 (n = 1), ST147 (n = 4), ST272 (n = 1), ST273 (n = 1) and ST383 (n = 1), which except for ST272 were part of putative international clonal complexes. All were multidrug resistant due to the presence of other resistance determinants, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (CTX-M-3, SHV-5 and SHV-12), 16S rRNA methylases (ArmA) and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinants (QnrS). One isolate harboured a novel VIM-variant (VIM-26) while VIM-1 and VIM-19 were detected in six and one isolate, respectively. Two different genetic structures surrounding the bla
                     VIM gene were identified in four isolates. In two isolates bla
                     VIM-1 and bla
                     VIM-26 were located in an integron similar to In-e541 (intI1;bla
                     VIM-1/-26;aacA7; dhfrI;aadA1;3’CS) while in the other two isolates bla
                     VIM-1 was located in an integron lacking 3’CS but with an IS26 element in the 3’end (intI1;bla
                     VIM-1;aac(6’)-Ib;IS26), as identified in the IncN plasmid pKOX105. The bla
                     VIM-genes were located on transferable plasmids ranging from -40 to -240 kb and associated with Tn21 in four isolates. PCR-based replicon typing indicated association of bla
                     VIM with IncN (n = 3) and A/C (n = 1) broad-host-range plasmids but also with unknown replicons (n = 4). In conclusion, Scandinavian VPKP is associated with importation and genetically related to international clones encoding transferable plasmid-mediated multidrug resistance.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara50" view="all">VIM-producing <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> (VPKP) has been identified as a source of hospital outbreaks and is prevalent particularly in the Mediterranean region. In this study we have characterized eight VPKP isolates identified in Scandinavia during 2005–2008. With the exception of one isolate, all were from patients with recent history of hospitalization abroad (Greece, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 6; Turkey, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) resulted in five sequence types (STs), ST36 (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1), ST147 (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 4), ST272 (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1), ST273 (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1) and ST383 (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1), which except for ST272 were part of putative international clonal complexes. All were multidrug resistant due to the presence of other resistance determinants, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (CTX-M-3, SHV-5 and SHV-12), 16S rRNA methylases (ArmA) and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinants (QnrS). One isolate harboured a novel VIM-variant (VIM-26) while VIM-1 and VIM-19 were detected in six and one isolate, respectively. Two different genetic structures surrounding the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> gene were identified in four isolates. In two isolates <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-26</ce:inf> were located in an integron similar to In-e541 (<ce:italic>int</ce:italic>I1;<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1/-26</ce:inf>;<ce:italic>aacA7</ce:italic>; <ce:italic>dhfrI</ce:italic>;<ce:italic>aadA1</ce:italic>;3’CS) while in the other two isolates <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf> was located in an integron lacking 3’CS but with an IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic> element in the 3’end (<ce:italic>int</ce:italic>I1;<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf>;<ce:italic>aac(6’)-Ib</ce:italic>;IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic>), as identified in the IncN plasmid pKOX105. The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf>-genes were located on transferable plasmids ranging from -40 to -240 kb and associated with Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic> in four isolates. PCR-based replicon typing indicated association of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> with IncN (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 3) and A/C (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1) broad-host-range plasmids but also with unknown replicons (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 4). In conclusion, Scandinavian VPKP is associated with importation and genetically related to international clones encoding transferable plasmid-mediated multidrug resistance.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">The increased prevalence of acquired carbapenem-hydrolysing β-lactamases in Gram-negative bacteria is of great concern due to their ability to hydrolyse virtually all β-lactam antibiotics, as well as their genetic association with transferable multidrug resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref110" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. As a consequence, infections with carbapenemase-producing bacteria have become a serious challenge for infection control and antibiotic therapy. Acquired metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) are a diverse family of carbapenemases [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref120" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. Among <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> the VIM-group and particularly VIM-1 are dominating [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref130" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], although NDM-1 has been on the rise among this species recently [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>]. VIM-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> (VPKP) has been associated with hospital outbreaks in several countries [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5 bib6">3–6</ce:cross-refs>], and has become endemic in hospitals in certain countries such as Greece [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. Worryingly, community-onset infections by VPKP have recently been described [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref160" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>], as well as isolates producing both VIM and KPC-type carbapenemases [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf>-genes are located in class 1 integrons as gene cassettes and have been identified on plasmids with different replicon types [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>], increasing the possibility of dissemination and linkage to other antibiotic resistance genes. As a consequence, VPKP isolates often harbour other acquired resistance genes (i.e. genes encoding aminoglycoside modifying enzymes, 16S rRNA methylases, plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance, trimethoprim resistance and other βlactamases). Molecular typing of VPKP isolates has shown that in general the dissemination is multiclonal with some clonal outbreaks. This is in contrast to what is observed for KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumonia</ce:italic>, where the majority of isolates belong to multilocus sequence type (ST) 258 or related STs [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">In Scandinavia the emergence of carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria has been mainly associated with the importation of isolates [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib11 bib12">11,12</ce:cross-refs>]. Here we report the characterization of VPKP isolates identified in Scandinavia from 2005 to 2008. (Part of this study was presented at the 20th European Congress of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases, Vienna, Austria.)</ce:para>
               </ce:section>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Results and Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Molecular epidemiology</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">With one exception, all isolates were derived from patients who had undergone recent medical treatment abroad either in Greece (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 6) or Turkey (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 1), and were thus likely to be imported cases (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Isolate K46-62 was recovered from an 83-year-old female with no history of international travel. A strong association with importation has also been observed with the emergence of other carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative clinical isolates in Scandinavia [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib11 bib12">11,12</ce:cross-refs>]. PFGE typing indicated a possible clonal relationship between four isolates (Ö N-2211, T14789, AO-15200 and HY-1491/7) with a Dice coefficient between 72% and 86% (data not shown). All four isolates were identified as MLST sequence type (ST) 147 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Isolate K46-62 was typed to ST273, a single locus variant of ST147. K45-67, B-22365 and U-60687 were typed to ST272, ST383 and ST36, respectively. eBurst and minimum spanning tree analysis showed that with the exception of ST272, which is a singleton in the MLST database, the other STs are part of putative international clonal complexes (data not shown). Both ST147 and ST36 have previously been associated with CTX-M-15-producing isolates from Hungary, [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>] Spain, [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib20 bib21">20,21</ce:cross-refs>] and Tunisia, [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>] while ST383 has been associated with an isolate co-producing VIM-4, KPC-2 and CMY-4 from Greece [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Antimicrobial susceptibility</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">All isolates expressed high-level resistance to penicillins, cephalosporins and aztreonam, with the exception of one isolate that was susceptible to aztreonam (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). Carbapenem MICs were variable, with two isolates categorized as susceptible to meropenem. All isolates were intermediate susceptible or resistant to ciprofloxacin and at least one aminoglycoside. Two isolates were resistant to tigecycline and one was intermediate susceptible. Colistin was the only antibiotic that retained susceptibility in all the isolates. Several of the transformants and transconjugants in this study were fully susceptible to both meropenem and ertapenem (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>), which have recently been suggested as the two most sensitive antibiotics for detecting carbapenemases in <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>]. Low carbapenem MIC values for VIM-producing clinical <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> isolates have also been documented [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>], although such isolates have so far been described very infrequently and in low numbers compared with VPKP [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>]. According to these data, one explanation could be that the MIC levels in MBL-positive <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> are very low for the carbapenems, making it very difficult to identify these carbapenemase producers unless epidemiological cut-off (ECOFF) values from EUCAST are used [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">Resistance determinants</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf> was identified in six isolates, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-19</ce:inf> and a novel variant, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-26</ce:inf>, were detected in one isolate each (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). The novel variant, VIM-26 (isolate U-60687) harbours one amino acid mutation at position 224 (His224Leu, BBL numbering scheme), compared with VIM-1. Residue 224 is located close to the active site of VIM enzymes and has been shown to play a role in inhibitor interactions [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>] and suggested to affect the affinity of substrates [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. Leu224 has previously been found in VIM-5 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>] and VIM-13 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>]. Both VIM-5 and VIM-13 show reduced catalytic efficiency towards cephalosporins with a bulky positively charged substituent (e.g. ceftazidime and cefepime) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib28 bib29">28,29</ce:cross-refs>]. However, mutagenesis studies on VIM-13 did not reveal a significant effect of Leu224 with respect to the susceptibility to ceftazidime and cefepime [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>]. Further, the VIM-26 transformant (U-60687-TF, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1302" refid="tbl2">Tables 2</ce:cross-ref> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1303" refid="tbl3">3</ce:cross-ref>) showed an elevated MIC of aztreonam although it was negative for CTX-M, SHV and TEM ESBLs. However, the presence of Leu224 in VIM-5 and VIM-13 did not seem to have an effect on aztreonam [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib28 bib29">28,29</ce:cross-refs>]. Thus, the elevated MIC is likely to be due to the presence of other ESBLs. However, the significance of the His224Leu mutation in VIM-26 needs to be determined by kinetic experiments. <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-19</ce:inf>, identified in isolate B-22365, has previously only been identified in Algeria [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>], France (patient transferred from Algeria to France) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>] and Greece [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>]. Characterization of VIM-19 has suggested increased carbapenemase activity compared with VIM-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>]. However, comparison of B-22365 and the transformant with the other VPKP isolates in this study did not reveal any increased MIC towards the carbapenems.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Screening for other resistance genes in the isolates revealed the presence of genes encoding extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) in five isolates (<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-3</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-group 1</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-5</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-12</ce:inf>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>Bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-1</ce:inf> (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 2), <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-11</ce:inf> (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 3) and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-1</ce:inf> (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 3) were also detected. The 16S rRNA methylase gene <ce:italic>armA</ce:italic> was identified in one isolate that showed high-level aminoglycoside resistance and the plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinant <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> was detected in two isolates. All isolates were negative for <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">Genetic context of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> and plasmid analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> gene was successfully transferred from all isolates either by conjugation or transformation into recipient <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>, suggesting plasmid localization (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). S1 nuclease PFGE and in-gel hybridization of seven isolates (data not shown) showed that <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> was located on plasmids ranging from -40 to -240 kb (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). Co-transfer of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> with other resistance determinants such as <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-3</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-gr.1</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-12</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-1</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>armA</ce:italic> was observed. The co-transfer of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-3</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>armA</ce:italic> as observed in isolate Ö N-2211, resulting in resistance to all β-lactams and aminoglycosides, is worrying as dissemination of such multidrug resistance plasmids will have serious consequences for treatment options. Linkage of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> to Tn<ce:italic>402</ce:italic> has been previously reported in Scandinavia for <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]; however, none of the <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> integrons possessed the <ce:italic>tni</ce:italic>A-C gene locus to the right-hand side of the integron. Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic>-like transposons have been linked with MBLs previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>], and in the case of these isolates all demonstrated Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic> to the left-hand side of the class 1 integron, apart from isolates K45-67 and K46-62. These data suggest that whilst the transposons are carried on different plasmids that vary in size and type, the genetic structure of Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic> linked to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> is largely conserved. Interestingly, although we believe Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic> to be functional in each of these cases, chromosomal location of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> could not be demonstrated; however, the fact that this structure has appeared on different plasmid scaffolds suggests recent mobility.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">Two different integron structures containing <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> were identified in four isolates. In isolates U-60687 and HY-1491/7, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-26</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf>, respectively were located in an integron similar to In-e541 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>], where <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> is located as the first gene cassette followed by <ce:italic>aacA7, dhfrI, aadA1</ce:italic>, and the 3’conserved segment (3’CS) [<ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>;<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1/-26</ce:inf>; <ce:italic>aacA7</ce:italic>;<ce:italic>dhfrI</ce:italic>;<ce:italic>aadA1</ce:italic>;3’CS]. However, the <ce:italic>aadA1</ce:italic> gene was truncated in HY-1491/7, as observed in VIM-1-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> identified in France [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>]. In-e541 has previously been identified in different species, including <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>], <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>] and <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic>. PCR-based replicon typing (PBRT) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>] suggested that In-e541 was located on an A/C plasmid in isolate U-60687 and on an unknown replicon type in isolate HY-1491/7. This is in contrast to previous reports where In-e541 has been associated with IncN plasmids [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para180" view="all">In isolates K45-67 and K46-62, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf> along with <ce:italic>aac(6’)- Ib</ce:italic> was located in an integron lacking the 3’CS but instead having an IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic> element in the 3’end (<ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>;<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf>;<ce:italic>aac(6’)- Ib</ce:italic>;IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic>). An identical structure has been identified in the IncN plasmid pKOX105, shown to harbour <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-1</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-12</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>]. Interestingly, K45-67, K46-62 and the respective transconjugants were positive for IncN, <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-12</ce:inf>, suggesting a similar plasmid. In the other isolates PCR amplification of the genetic context of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> was unsuccessful despite repeated attempts and the use of different primer combinations.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">In conclusion, the study shows that the emergence of VPKP in Scandinavia is associated with importation of international clones harbouring multiple resistance determinants on plasmids, including plasmids with a broad-host range, supporting further spread.</ce:para>
