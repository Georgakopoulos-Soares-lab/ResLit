                  The fosfomycin resistance protein FomA inactivates fosfomycin by phosphorylation of the phosphonate group of the antibiotic in the presence of ATP and Mg(II). We report the crystal structure of FomA from the fosfomycin biosynthetic gene cluster of Streptomyces wedmorensis in complex with diphosphate and in ternary complex with the nonhydrolyzable ATP analog adenosine 5′-(β,γ-imido)-triphosphate (AMPPNP), Mg(II), and fosfomycin, at 1.53 and 2.2Å resolution, respectively. The polypeptide exhibits an open αβα sandwich fold characteristic for the amino acid kinase family of enzymes. The diphosphate complex shows significant disorder in loops surrounding the active site. As a result, the nucleotide-binding site is wide open. Binding of the substrates is followed by the partial closure of the active site and ordering of the α2-helix. Structural comparison with N-acetyl-l-glutamate kinase shows several similarities in the site of phosphoryl transfer: 1) preservation of architecture of the catalytical amino acids of N-acetyl-l-glutamate kinase (Lys9, Lys216, and Asp150 in FomA); 2) good superposition of the phosphate acceptor groups of the substrates, and 3) good superposition of the diphosphate molecule with the β- and γ-phosphates of AMPPNP, suggesting that the reaction could proceed by an associative in-line mechanism. However, differences in conformations of the triphosphate moiety of AMPPNP molecules, the long distance (5.1Å) between the phosphate acceptor and donor groups in FomA, and involvement of Lys18 instead of Lys9 in binding with the γ-phosphate may indicate a different reaction mechanism. The present work identifies the active site residues of FomA responsible for substrate binding and specificity and proposes their roles in catalysis.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara80" view="all">The fosfomycin resistance protein FomA inactivates fosfomycin by phosphorylation of the phosphonate group of the antibiotic in the presence of ATP and Mg(II). We report the crystal structure of FomA from the fosfomycin biosynthetic gene cluster of <ce:italic>Streptomyces wedmorensis</ce:italic> in complex with diphosphate and in ternary complex with the nonhydrolyzable ATP analog adenosine 5′-(β,γ-imido)-triphosphate (AMPPNP), Mg(II), and fosfomycin, at 1.53 and 2.2Å resolution, respectively. The polypeptide exhibits an open αβα sandwich fold characteristic for the amino acid kinase family of enzymes. The diphosphate complex shows significant disorder in loops surrounding the active site. As a result, the nucleotide-binding site is wide open. Binding of the substrates is followed by the partial closure of the active site and ordering of the α2-helix. Structural comparison with <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-glutamate kinase shows several similarities in the site of phosphoryl transfer: 1) preservation of architecture of the catalytical amino acids of <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-glutamate kinase (Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup>, and Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup> in FomA); 2) good superposition of the phosphate acceptor groups of the substrates, and 3) good superposition of the diphosphate molecule with the β- and γ-phosphates of AMPPNP, suggesting that the reaction could proceed by an associative in-line mechanism. However, differences in conformations of the triphosphate moiety of AMPPNP molecules, the long distance (5.1Å) between the phosphate acceptor and donor groups in FomA, and involvement of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> instead of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> in binding with the γ-phosphate may indicate a different reaction mechanism. The present work identifies the active site residues of FomA responsible for substrate binding and specificity and proposes their roles in catalysis.</ce:simple-para>

            <ce:sections>
               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Phosphorus-carbon bond-containing natural products (phosphonates and phosphinates) are of great medical importance because they exhibit a wide spectrum of antibacterial, antiviral, and antiparasitic activities in addition to high chemical stability. Fosfomycin ((1<ce:italic>R</ce:italic>,2<ce:italic>S</ce:italic>)-1,2-epoxy-propylphosphonic acid) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>), a natural product of several species of <ce:italic>Pseudomonas</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>Streptomyces</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>), is one of the best known representatives of the class. Since its introduction by Merck in 1969 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>), the antibiotic has successfully been used for treatment of lower urinary tract infections (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). It is also very effective against methicillin- and vancomycin-resistant strains of <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>). In addition fosfomycin has the capacity to favor phagocystosis, act as an immunomodulator, and protect human cells from cisplatin, cyclosporine, aminoglycoside, vancomycin, amphotericin B, and polyximin toxicity (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>). Although new, and in some instances better, urinary tract infection drugs have become available, fosfomycin remains a very useful drug. Studies of antibiotic prescribing patterns for cystitis during the period 1999–2002 show that fosfomycin trometamol (Monurol®) use increased dramatically, becoming the first choice for any type of cystitis (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>). Fosfomycin trometamol is still the only drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for treatment of acute cystitis during pregnancy (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Bactericidal activity of fosfomycin is based on inhibition of UDP-<ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-glucosamine-3-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-enolpyruvyl-transferase, MurA, the enzyme responsible for the first stage of peptidoglycan biosynthesis (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>). Fosfomycin irreversibly inactivates MurA by alkylating an active site cysteine (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>). Upon introduction into the clinic, resistance to fosfomycin emerged rapidly and was mainly attributed to mutations affecting antibiotic transport (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>) or to mutations in the MurA target itself (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). Later, a plasmid-related mechanism of resistance was identified in clinical isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Three different fosfomycin resistance genes, <ce:italic>fosA, fosB</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>fosX</ce:italic>, have been extensively described in the literature (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib16 bib17 bib18 bib19">16–19</ce:cross-refs>). All of the corresponding proteins open the epoxide ring of the antibiotic during the catalysis, but each in a distinct way. FosA utilizes the tripeptide glutathione as a thiol donor, Mn<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> and K<ce:sup loc="post">+</ce:sup>; FosB subgroup members perform a similar reaction using <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-cysteine and Mg<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup>, whereas FosX produces a diol product, 1,2-dihydroxypropylphosphonic acid, with the addition of water, which is assisted by Mn<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup>. Despite the fact that FosA, FosB, and FosX proteins share modest (23–40%) sequence identity between the subgroups and perform different chemical reactions, they all belong to the vicinal oxygen chelate superfamily of metalloenzymes and share a common molecular fold. Two additional fosfomycin resistance genes, named <ce:italic>fomA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>fomB</ce:italic>, that confer high level fosfomycin resistance on <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> have recently been discovered in the fosfomycin biosynthetic clusters of <ce:italic>Streptomyces wedmorensis</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>Streptomyces fradiae</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>). The <ce:italic>fomA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>fomB</ce:italic> genes encode 29- and 37-kDa proteins, respectively. In contrast to previously known types of fosfomycin resistance proteins, these novel proteins have partial homology to the Mg-ATP-binding domains of some eukaryotic kinases, and therefore they represent a novel mechanism of resistance to the antibiotic. FomA catalyzes phosphorylation of fosfomycin to fosfomycin monophosphate, and FomB, phosphorylation of fosfomycin monophosphate to fosfomycin diphosphate in the presence of ATP and magnesium ions (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="fig2">Scheme 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). Fosfomycin monophosphate and fosfomycin diphosphate have not been shown to alkylate an active site cysteine of MurA.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para40" view="all">Fosfomycin resistance kinases FomA and FomB are encoded in the fosfomycin biosynthetic gene clusters of different species of <ce:italic>Streptomyces</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Pseudomonas</ce:italic>. The <ce:italic>fomA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>fomB</ce:italic> genes are required for the production of fosfomycin (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>), although their particular role in the biosynthesis still remains unclear. As is true for other antibiotic-producing organisms, fosfomycin producing bacteria must protect themselves from the lethal effect of the antibiotic. It is suggested that FomA and FomB proteins are responsible for the self-resistance of bacteria (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Both proteins were also proposed to participate in the transport of the antibiotic (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para50" view="all">The growing threat of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms accentuates the importance of understanding the mechanism of resistance to design effective inhibitors to combat the antibiotic resistance and reduce the further spread of resistant bacteria. ATP-dependent fosfomycin resistance has already been detected in <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> clinical isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>). However, it is not clear whether it is connected to the <ce:italic>fomA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>fomB</ce:italic> genes, because an enzyme characterization was not performed. The understanding of the precise mechanism of the fosfomycin phosphorylation by FomA and FomB proteins will lead to the development of inhibitors specifically targeting FomA-dependent antibiotic resistance. In this paper we report high resolution crystal structure of the fosfomycin resistance kinase FomA from <ce:italic>S. wedmorensis</ce:italic> in complex with diphosphate and in ternary complex with the nonhydrolyzable ATP analog AMPPNP,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="FN4">

                     <ce:italic>Gene Amplification and Cloning</ce:italic>—The open reading frames for FomA were amplified by PCR using pFBG1204 as a template.                      <ce:italic>Protein Expression and Purification</ce:italic>—FomA was expressed in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> BL21(DE3) cells by the autoinduction method (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>). An overnight culture grown in MDG medium containing 100 μg/ml of kanamycin was diluted 1:1000 (v/v) into ZYM-5052 medium and incubated at 37 °C for 5 h, and then the temperature was reduced to 22 °C. The cells were pelleted after reaching saturation, frozen at -80 °C, subsequently resuspended in the binding buffer (50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 20 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> imidazole, 10% glycerol), and sonicated for 5 min. After removal of debris by centrifugation (at 46,000 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 20 min at 4 °C), the supernatant was applied to a TALON metal affinity resin (Co<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> affinity, Clontech Inc.) preequilibrated with the binding buffer. Protein was extensively washed with the binding buffer and eluted with the elution buffer (50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 200 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> imidazole, 10% glycerol). The final purification step was performed by size exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 column; GE Healthcare). The column was preequilibrated with 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, supplemented with 150 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> NaCl. A typical flow rate of 0.5 ml/min was maintained during elution. Absorbance at 280 nm was measured to monitor elution of the protein from the column. Purified protein was concentrated to 10 μg/μl.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle20">RESULTS AND DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para120" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Overall Fold and Quaternary Structure</ce:italic>—According to the classification based on sequence similarity, FomA belongs to the amino acid kinase family (AAKF) of enzymes (PFAM group PF00696) that includes <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-glutamate kinase (NAGK), glutamate-5 kinase (GK), carbamate kinase, the N-terminal domain of aspartokinase, and uridylate kinase (UMPK). The best amino acid identity, however, is only about 24% for the NAGKs. The representatives from the first four classes of the AAKF transfer the γ-phosphate of ATP to a carboxylate or carbamate group of the substrates making acylphosphate bonds, whereas the UMPK enzymes phosphorylate a phosphate group of uridilate. The crystal structure of FomA protein confirmed the assignment of FomA to the AAKF because the enzyme exhibits the polypeptide fold found in all members of the AAKF for which crystal structures have been determined: <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-glutamate kinases (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib32 bib33 bib34">32–34</ce:cross-refs>), glutamate-5-kinase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>), carbamate kinase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>), aspartokinases (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib37 bib38 bib39">37–39</ce:cross-refs>), and uridylate kinases (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib40 bib41 bib42 bib43">40–43</ce:cross-refs>). The search was made by the secondary structure matching data base (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>) using FomA coordinates from the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin complex, because that structure is more ordered and complete in comparison to the FomA·DPO one. Root mean square deviations between the equivalent CA atoms of FomA and all these structures lie in the range of 2.21–2.92 Å (over the range of 185–214 CA atoms).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para130" view="all">The molecule of FomA consists of an open αβα sandwich fold with seven α-helices and 14 β-strands (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="fig4">Fig. 3, <ce:italic>A</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The central core is comprised by eight β-strands (β1, β2, β3, β6, β9, β12, β13, and β14) that are sandwiched between two layers of α-helices (α1 and α3 flank one side of the central β-sheet core, and the helices α4, α5, α6, and α7 lie on the opposite side). Similar to the members of the AAKF, the monomer of FomA could be divided into two lobes: the N-terminal lobe (residues 1–160) and the C-terminal lobe (residues 161–263) with a large crevice between them where the active site of the enzyme is located (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="fig4">Fig. 3<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The N-terminal lobe residues participate in binding fosfomycin. The C-terminal lobe binds the nucleotide molecule.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para140" view="all">As evidenced by size exclusion chromatography, FomA is a homodimer in solution. The asymmetric unit of FomA in the crystal, however, is represented by a monomer. The homodimer is generated by the crystallographic 2-fold axis. The dimer interface is solely mediated by the interactions between the N-lobes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="fig4">Fig. 3<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). It buries a total of 2875 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> of solvent-accessible area, as calculated using the PISA interface server (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>) or 1437.5 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>/monomer (11.8% of the surface) and renders a complexation significance score of 0.407, which accounts for a medium-tight complex. The interdimer interface, which is predominantly hydrophobic, mainly involves: 1) antiparallel interactions between the symmetry related α3-helixes (13 participating residues; most of the dimer interface), 2) interactions of the α4 helix with the symmetry related β7-β8 hairpin, and 3) antiparallel interactions between the 3<ce:inf loc="post">10</ce:inf> helix η<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf> and the connection loop β5-α4 with the same elements of the other dimer subunit. In addition to numerous hydrophobic interactions, primarily provided by Phe, Thr, Ala, and Val, the interface is stabilized by 18 hydrogen bonds and 18 salt bridges. It should be noted that although roughly the same surface of the N-lobe is used for the dimer formation in FomA, NAGK, GK, and UMPK, the mode of dimerization is significantly different in every case (see Ref. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref> for the detailed description of the NAGK, GK, and UMPK dimers). The list of dimer interface interactions is provided in the supplemental Table S1.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Initially we attempted to obtain a crystal structure of the FomA·ATP complex. However, cocrystallization of FomA with ATP was unsuccessful because the resulting structure (FomA·DPO complex) contains only the diphosphate moiety of the ATP molecule, presumably because of hydrolysis of ATP during crystallization. Soaking of the FomA·DPO crystals in modified mother liquor with the addition of AMPPNP, MgCl<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>, and fosfomycin (see “Experimental Procedures” for details) prior to data collection proved to be sufficient to obtain the ternary complex (FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin). Thus, the current paper reports structures of two different reaction states: the FomA·DPO complex, where the active site of the enzyme does not contain any of the required substrates, and the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin complex, the approximation of the early stage of the phosphorylation reaction, when all the substrates are bound, and the enzyme is ready to proceed with the catalytical reaction.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para160" view="all">The protein in both complexes exhibits the same polypeptide fold and dimeric architecture. However, in contrast to the FomA·DPO structure, which has several disordered parts, the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin structure is mainly ordered. The main differences between the two complexes lie in conformations of several structural elements surrounding the active site: 1) a loop stretching from residues 18 to 23 has slightly different conformation; 2) the amino acid residues 57–68 are completely disordered in the FomA·DPO complex, whereas they are ordered in the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin structure. Moreover, residues 57–63 are a part of the α2-helix, and 3) residues 207–211 are not visible in the FomA·DPO structure, the helix α6 (residues 195–199) is four residues shorter in the ternary complex (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="fig5">Fig. 4<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>). As a result, residues 201–205 are no longer part of an α-helix, and they move closer to the active site so that the side chain of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">202</ce:sup> participates in stacking interactions with the adenine ring of AMPPNP. Such interactions limit access to the active site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="fig5">Fig. 4<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The conformational change observed in the ternary complex is obviously connected to the movements of loops during the binding of both substrates</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para170" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>The Nucleotide-binding Site</ce:italic>—The nucleotide-binding site is located in the groove between the N- and C-lobes. The triphosphate part of AMPPNP binds deeply in the catalytic cleft on the interface between the two lobes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="fig4">Fig. 3<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>) and is surrounded by the α6-α7 and β9-β10 connection loops and the η<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> helix. The other side of the nucleotide-binding site (the adenosine moiety) is formed by the β9-β10 and α6-α7 connection loops, the β10 sheet, and the N-terminal part of the α7 helix. Two polypeptide segments containing glycine-rich sequence motifs are deeply buried in the catalytic cleft: a segment of the β1-α1 junction (residues 9–15; participates in binding with the γ-phosphate of the nucleotide) and the β2-α2 connection loop (residues 51–55; binds the phosphonate group of fosfomycin). These motifs are well conserved throughout the AAKF and are considered to be the some of main factors for neutralization of the accumulated negative charge during the transfer of a phosphoryl group.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para180" view="all">The superposition of the active sites in both structures shows that the DPO molecule is positioned closer to the fosfomycin-binding site than the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="fig5">Fig. 4<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Moreover, the overall position of the DPO molecule more resembles the position of the β- and γ-phosphate groups of AMPPNP in the NAGK structure (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>). A different orientation of the γ-phosphate is observed in the case of the ternary complex, where the γ-phosphate group is positioned farther away from the fosfomycin-binding site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="fig5">Fig. 4<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). As a result of the different orientations of the phosphate groups, the modes of interaction of the β- and γ-phosphates differ in both FomA complexes. The diphosphate in the FomA·DPO complex is bound to protein via H-bonds from the nonbridging oxygen atoms of the γ-phosphate to the main chain NH atoms of Gly<ce:sup loc="post">12</ce:sup> and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">53</ce:sup>, to the side chain NZ of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> (from nonbridging oxygen atoms of both phosphates), and indirectly via a water molecule from the γ-phosphate to OG of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig6"/>) (Thus we label the phosphate groups of diphosphate as β and γ). In the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin complex, a water molecule occupies the position approximately equivalent to the γ-phosphate of DPO or the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP in the NAGK.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para190" view="all">In the ternary complex, the nonbridging oxygen atom O-2 of the α-phosphoryl group of AMPPNP is indirectly hydrogen-bonded via water molecules to the carbonyl oxygen of Ala<ce:sup loc="post">222</ce:sup> and via the bridging oxygen atom to OG of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">170</ce:sup>. The nonbridging oxygens of the β-phosphoryl group make hydrogen bonds to NZ of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> and OG of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">170</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The bridging N is hydrogen-bonded to OG of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">13</ce:sup>. The γ-phosphoryl group participates in hydrogen bonding with NH of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">13</ce:sup> and NZ of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> correspond to fully conserved lysine residues in NAGKs and GKs. It was proposed for NAGK that these residues are centrally involved in catalysis of phosphoryl transfer by stabilizing the transition state (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>). In the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin complex, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> does not participate in hydrogen bonding with theγ-phosphoryl group. Instead, NZ of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> is hydrogen-bonded to the nonbridging O-3 of the γ-phosphoryl group. All three phosphoryl groups are complexed with the Mg<ce:sup loc="post">+2</ce:sup> cation via nonbridging oxygen atoms. Three water molecules complete the hexacoordination sphere of magnesium. One of them is H-bonded to the phosphonate oxygen O-3 of fosfomycin.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para200" view="all">The AMPPNP molecule is bound in the extended conformation. An eight-residue insertion loop (residues 203–210) with respect to that in NAGK makes the adenine-binding site less solvent-accessible. The adenine ring sits in the hydrophobic pocket formed by Met<ce:sup loc="post">213</ce:sup>, Val<ce:sup loc="post">172</ce:sup>, Val<ce:sup loc="post">176</ce:sup>, Leu<ce:sup loc="post">20</ce:sup>, Ile<ce:sup loc="post">175</ce:sup>, and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">202</ce:sup>. In the absence of nucleotide (FomA·DPO complex), the pocket is more open to the solvent because of flexibility of the loop 203–211 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="fig5">Fig. 4<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The adenine ring is in the <ce:italic>syn</ce:italic> configuration and closely resembles the conformation of AMPPNP in the NAGK structure (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>). Interactions with the protein involve hydrogen bonds between N-1 and the main chain NH of Val<ce:sup loc="post">176</ce:sup>, N-6 and the carbonyl oxygens of Val<ce:sup loc="post">176</ce:sup> and Ala<ce:sup loc="post">200</ce:sup>, N-7 and the main chain NH of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">202</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>), and stacking interactions in quite a spectacular way. First, the adenine ring makes classical π-π stacking interactions with the side chain of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">202</ce:sup>. Trp<ce:sup loc="post">202</ce:sup> in turn participates in the additional stacking interaction with the side chain of His<ce:sup loc="post">254</ce:sup> of a symmetry related subunit. Met<ce:sup loc="post">213</ce:sup>, which is highly conserved throughout the entire AAKF, completes the hydrophobic interactions from the other side of the adenine ring (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The only hydrophilic interaction between the ribose ring and the protein includes hydrogen bonding between the 3′-hydroxyl group of the ribose ring and the carboxyl oxygen of Asp<ce:sup loc="post">171</ce:sup>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>The Fosfomycin-binding Site</ce:italic>—A fosfomycin molecule binds in the N-terminal lobe. The binding pocket is comprised of the N-terminal parts of the helices α2 and α3, the β7-β8 hairpin, the β2-α2 glycine-rich connection loop, the β8-α5 connection, and the α6-α7 junction, which makes a “lid” over the fosfomycin-binding site just as the β3-β4 hairpin does in the NAGK over the NAG-binding site. The α2 helix and the α6-α7 junction (extra residues in comparison to other members of the AAKF) are not visible in the electron density map in the FomA·DPO complex, suggesting that the conformational change and ordering of those structural elements should occur during the binding of fosfomycin.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">The phosphonate group of fosfomycin superimposes well on the γ-carboxylate group of NAG in the NAGK and points toward the triphosphate tail of AMPPNP (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The interactions of fosfomycin with protein are provided by the phosphonate group of the antibiotic via hydrogen bonds. The O-1 phosphonate atom makes H-bonds with the main chain NH and the side chain OG atoms of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">149</ce:sup>. The O-3 phosphonate oxygen participates in hydrogen bonding with OG of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">210</ce:sup> and OG of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup> and with a water of the Mg<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> coordination sphere. The O-2 phosphonate atom makes a hydrogen bond with NH of Gly<ce:sup loc="post">53</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Additional stabilization factors include hydrophobic interactions between C-1 and C-2 of fosfomycin and hydrophobic parts of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">210</ce:sup>, Ile<ce:sup loc="post">61</ce:sup>, and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">57</ce:sup>. The primary role of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup>, Ser<ce:sup loc="post">149</ce:sup>, and Thr<ce:sup loc="post">210</ce:sup> in catalysis could be in proper positioning and orientation of fosfomycin for the enzymatic reaction. There are no direct interactions between fosfomycin and the nucleotide molecule. However, water-mediated hydrogen bonds between the phosphonate group of fosfomycin and the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP are observed (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Insights into Phosphoryl Transfer</ce:italic>—The best studied enzyme of the AAKF is NAGK, for which kinetic data as well as crystal structures of different complexes and reaction state intermediates are available. The enzymatic reaction proceeds via associative in-line phosphoryl transfer with the assistance of the catalytical residues Lys<ce:sup loc="post">8</ce:sup>, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">217</ce:sup>, and Asp<ce:sup loc="post">162</ce:sup> and with no need for general acid-base catalysis (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>). The crystal structures of two FomA complexes presented here reveal a great degree of similarity between FomA and NAGK in the overall molecular fold as well as in the conservation of amino acid residues participating in nucleotide binding (Figs. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="fig4">3<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="fig6">5<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The residues coordinating the adenine moiety of ATP appear to be less well conserved. However, several key residues coordinating the phosphate groups are conserved in the enzymes. These include Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>, Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup>, and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> corresponding to Lys<ce:sup loc="post">8</ce:sup>, Asp<ce:sup loc="post">162</ce:sup>, and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">217</ce:sup> in NAGK, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup> makes hydrogen bonds with Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> in both FomA complexes, and its likely role is in the proper orientation of Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> for catalysis, similarly as in case of NAGK. Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup>, which hydrogen bonds to a nonbridging oxygen of the β-phosphate, should activate the departure of the leaving phosphate group. Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> in the FomA·DPO complex, which is H-bonded to the γ-phosphate group, is likely to participate in the stabilization of the transition state intermediate, in a similar manner as observed in the NAGK. On the other hand, the conformation of the triphosphate moiety of the AMPPNP molecule in the ternary complex of FomA does not allow hydrogen bond interactions between Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup> and the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). However, in this case Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> (there is no homologous residue in NAGK) is H-bonded to a nonbridging oxygen of the γ-phosphate (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="fig6">Fig. 5, <ce:italic>B</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Such a “switch” from one lysine to another allows Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> to be considered the candidate for a catalytic residue, which neutralizes the charge and polarizes the leaving γ-phosphoryl group. Magnesium is coordinated with the α-, β-, and γ-phosphate groups of the nucleotide and together with Lys<ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup> and Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup> is presumably involved in orientation of the nucleotide and charge neutralization and polarization of the γ-phosphate group.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">As expected, the biggest differences between FomA and NAGK are observed in the substrate-binding site. The amino acid residues Ser<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup>, Ser<ce:sup loc="post">149</ce:sup>, and Thr<ce:sup loc="post">210</ce:sup> participate in binding with phosphonate oxygens of fosfomycin via H-bonds. Their likely role in the enzymatic reaction is in proper positioning and orientation of the antibiotic for the catalysis.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">The shortest distance between oxygen atoms of the phosphonate group of fosfomycin and phosphorus atom of AMPPNP is 5.19 Å, a value that is long for the optimal in-line phosphoryl transfer reaction. In addition, the triphosphate tail of AMPPNP is not aligned toward the phosphonate group of fosfomycin. Such observations could suggest that the catalytic conformation of ATP may be significantly different from the one observed for AMPPNP in the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin crystal structure. The nucleotide molecule could undergo a conformational change that places the γ-phosphate closer to the fosfomycin phosphonate group to allow the in-line phosphoryl transfer to proceed similar to what is seen for NAGK. Indeed, there is plenty of room to accommodate such a conformational change of the AMPPNP molecule without the need for any restructuring of the active site of the enzyme. The preservation of architecture of the catalytical residues of NAGK (Lys<ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup>, Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup> in FomA) in the nucleotide-binding site, as well as the position of the diphosphate molecule in the FomA·DPO complex that superimposes well to the β- and γ-phosphates of AMPPNP in NAGK ternary complex with MgAMPPNP and NAG, suggests that the mechanisms of the enzymatic reaction could be very similar and adds additional ground for a possible conformational change of the nucleotide molecule in the FomA catalyzed reaction. On the other hand, water-mediated catalysis is another possibility that cannot yet be ruled out because interactions between the nucleotide molecule and fosfomycin are mediated by two water molecules. One of them is coordinated to the Mg<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> ion and is hydrogen-bonded to Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup> (equivalent to the key organizing residue Asp<ce:sup loc="post">162</ce:sup> in NAGK) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). Such coordination could suggest general base catalysis. Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup> is positioned to deprotonate the water and could function as a general base. There is also another water molecule that mediates interactions between AMPPNP and the antibiotic via hydrogen bonds of 2.82 Å to the phosphonate group of fosfomycin and 2.73 Åtothe γ-phosphate of AMPPNP. The water molecule is 3.05 Å from His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="fig6">Fig. 5<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> is in close vicinity to both the fosfomycin and AMPPNP molecules (3.83 and 3.45 Å to fosfomycin and AMPPNP, respectively) and is positioned approximately at the midpoint of the phosphoryl transfer site. It should be noted that His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> belongs to the completely disordered part in the fosfomycin-free FomA·DPO structure. In contrast, in the FomA·MgAMPPNP·fosfomycin complex His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> is a part of the ordered α2-helix. The ordering of the helix clearly depends on the binding of fosfomycin in the active site of the enzyme. Hence, His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> could also play an important role in the enzymatic reaction. Mutational and kinetic studies will be necessary to confirm the possible catalytical role of His<ce:sup loc="post">58</ce:sup> and Asp<ce:sup loc="post">150</ce:sup>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">It is not clear from the present structures whether the FomA-catalyzed phosphoryl transfer proceeds via the associative or the dissociative mechanism. The later mechanism, however, is generally not favorable for enzymatic reactions assisted by ATP (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>). Crystal structures of additional complexes, such as complexes with ATP (FomA·ATP and FomA·MgATP), the ternary complex with ADP, Mg(II), and the product of the reaction fosfomycin monophosphate, and a reaction intermediate or a transition state analog will be needed to determine the precise mechanism of the enzymatic reaction.</ce:para>
