## ABSTRACT
Abstract Objectives To elucidate the role of a novel carbapenem-hydrolysing class D β-lactamase (RAD-1) from Riemerella anatipestifer . Methods We applied WGS and bioinformatic analysis to screen putative β-lactamase genes in R. anatipestifer SCVM0004. A putative class D β-lactamase gene was cloned into pET24a and transferred into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) for antibiotic susceptibility determination and protein purification. Meanwhile, the purified native protein was used to determine the enzymatic activities. Results A class D β-lactamase, RAD-1, was identified in the genome of R. anatipestifer SCVM0004. It was distinct from all characterized class D β-lactamases (≤42% amino acid sequence identity). Searching in GenBank showed that bla RAD-1 was widely disseminated among R. anatipestifer . Genomic environment analysis indicated that the chromosomal structures of bla RAD-1 -located regions were relatively conserved. Expression of RAD-1 in E. coli results in elevated MICs for various β-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, extended-spectrum cephalosporins, a monobactam and carbapenems. Moreover, kinetic analysis of purified RAD-1 revealed: (i) high-level activity against penicillins; (ii) highest affinity for carbapenems; (iii) moderate hydrolysis of extended-spectrum cephalosporins and a monobactam; and (iv) no activity for oxacillin and cefoxitin. Conclusions This study identified a novel chromosomally located class D carbapenemase RAD-1 (Bush–Jacoby functional group 2def) in R. anatipestifer SCVM0004. Moreover, bioinformatic analysis confirmed that the RAD-1 was widely prevalent and conserved in R. anatipestifer .

## INTRODUCTION
Riemerella anatipestifer belongs to the family Weeksellaceae (formerly classified as Flavobacteriaceae) and is an important Gram-negative pathogen causing domestic and wild waterfowl diseases. 1 Cephalosporins, especially ceftiofur and cefotaxime, are commonly used to control R. anatipestifer infection in ducks. 2 Nowadays, the emergence of β-lactam resistance in R. anatipestifer brings new challenges to the antimicrobial agent treatment. 3 Unfortunately, the β-lactam resistance mechanisms of R. anatipestifer have not been fully elucidated. The most common resistance mechanism to β-lactams, especially for Gram-negative bacteria, is the production of β-lactamases, which can be divided into Ambler classes A to D. 4 Classes A, C and D are serine-dependent β-lactamases, while class B is formed by zinc-requiring metalloenzymes. 5 Amongst the various β-lactamases, carbapenemases found in classes A, B and D are one of the greatest threats to clinical therapy as they can confer resistance by hydrolysing carbapenems and other β-lactam antimicrobials. 6 Of particular importance is the recent dissemination of carbapenem-hydrolysing class D β-lactamases (CHDLs). 7 More than 1200 class D enzymes are divided into 21 families according to the most recent Beta-Lactamase DataBase (BLDB, 10 October 2022; http://bldb.eu/BLDB.php?prot=D ). 8 Several β-lactamases have been reported in R. anatipestifer , including chromosomally encoded class A β-lactamases TEM-1, RAA-1 and RASA-1, and plasmid-mediated class D β-lactamases OXA-209 and OXA-347. 9–13 In this study, we identified a novel chromosome-encoded class D carbapenemase RAD-1 from R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 and investigated its genetics and biochemical properties.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
Bacterial strains and plasmids The R. anatipestifer clinical strain SCVM0004 was isolated from the liver of a sick duck in Rongchang, Chongqing, China, in 2019. It was identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and plasmid pET24a were used for cloning experiments. R. anatipestifer strains were cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA; Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) or in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Oxoid). When E. coli strains harbouring recombinant plasmids were cultured, 50 mg/L kanamycin (Aladdin) was supplemented in LB agar or broth (Oxoid). Details of the bacteria, plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). Antibiotic susceptibility testing The MICs of various β-lactam antibiotics were determined by a modified CLSI-based method. 14 Briefly, the β-lactams were prepared by broth microdilution using Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB). The cultured bacteria were also diluted to approximately 10 6 cfu/mL with MHB. One hundred microlitres of serially diluted antibiotic and 100 μL of bacterial solution were added to the 96-well sterile culture plates. The inoculated microplates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. 13 Due to no CLSI-approved breakpoints being available for R. anatipestifer , the bla RAD-1 -negative R. anatipestifer ATCC 11845 was used as a control. 10 Meanwhile, E. coli strain ATCC 25922 was used for the quality control. All MIC experiments were carried out at least in triplicate. Identification of β-lactamases in R. anatipestifer The high-quality genomic DNA of R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 was extracted with a TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen). Libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy DNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and ONT 1D ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK109) and subsequently sequenced by using the MGISEQ-2000 (BGI, Shenzhen, China) and Oxford Nanopore MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) platforms according to the manufacturer protocol. We then assembled these high-quality data using Canu (version 1.5, recommended parameters), and two rounds of polishing were applied to the assembled contigs using Pilon (version 1.24, recommended parameters) with the Illumina short reads. 15 , 16 The putative β-lactamase genes in SCVM0004 were analysed by NCBI’s AMRFinderPlus. 17 For protein sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction, 81 representative class D β-lactamases from different subfamilies were obtained from BLDB (updated 30 September 2022). Multiple sequence alignment was performed by Clustal Omega ( https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/ ). A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE web server. 18 Distribution characteristics and genetic environment analysis of bla RAD-1 For characterizing the distribution of bla RAD-1 in R. anatipestifer , we first analysed the population structure of R. anatipestifer based on publicly available genomic data from the NCBI database ( n = 126, data as of 12 October 2022). Briefly, a genome-scale tree was constructed by PopPUNK (version 2.4.0), and the lineages of the phylogenetic tree were defined by FastBAPS (version 1.0.8) with a Bayesian analysis of the population structure (BAPS) algorithm. 19 , 20 FigTree (version 1.4.3, http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/ ) and Ggtree (version 3.2.1) with R (version 4.1.2, https://www.r-project.org/ ) was used for tree visualization and manipulation. 21 To elucidate the genetic environment of the bla RAD-1 gene, we studied 10 kb regions upstream and downstream (if available) of the bla RAD-1 gene in all available R. anatipestifer genome data. The sequences were clustered by cd-hit-est (CD-HIT version 4.8.1) at the 95% level to remove redundancy. 22 Finally, the genetic similarity of bla RAD-1 -located regions was visualized using Easyfig (version 2.2.3) with BLASTn (BLAST version 2.6.0+). 23 Natural transformation To study the transferability of the bla RAD-1 gene, the genomic DNA of R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 was transferred to R. anatipestifer ATCC 11845 by natural transformation, as described previously. 24 , 25 Briefly, different concentrations of SCVM0004 genomic DNA (1, 10, 20 and 40 μg) were served as donor DNA, and incubated with recipient ATCC 11845. The transformants were screened by amoxicillin (1 mg/L) and identified by PCR. Nitrocefin test To assess whether R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 produced active β-lactamases, the chromogenic cephalosporin nitrocefin (Macklin) was used for testing as previously described with a few modifications. 26 SCVM0004 was cultured overnight at 37°C in 100 mL of TSB. Pellets were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) with 50 mM sodium bicarbonate (Aladdin). The cleared lysate acquired by sonication and centrifugation was used to hydrolyse nitrocefin (100 μM). Cloning experiments For the RAD-1 β-lactamase expression in E. coli , the bla RAD-1 gene with or without its N-terminal signal peptide was seamlessly cloned into pET24a by the circular polymerase extension cloning method. 27 The recombinant plasmids pET24a- bla RAD-1-sp and pET24a- bla RAD-1-no-sp were verified by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). The transformants with pET24a or pET24a- bla RAD-1-sp were used to evaluate the impact of the bla RAD-1 gene on MICs of β-lactams. Meanwhile, the transformant with pET24a- bla RAD-1-no-sp was used for RAD-1 purification. Protein purification E. coli BL21 (DE3) harbouring pET24a- bla RAD-1-no-sp was grown to an OD 600 of 0.6 at 37°C in 1 L of LB broth. Induction, crude protein extract preparation, and ion-exchange purification of RAD-1 were performed as described previously with a few modifications. 28 Briefly, the growing culture was induced by 0.2 mM IPTG (Sigma–Aldrich) overnight at 25°C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in buffer A (50 mM Tris-H 2 SO 4 , pH 7.0). After disruption by sonication, the supernatant was separated by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 40 min at 4°C and filtrated by 0.22 μm membranes. Subsequently, the cleared lysate was loaded onto a pre-equilibrated Q-Sepharose column (Cytiva) in buffer A. The enzyme recovered in the flowthrough was dialysed overnight at 4°C against buffer B (50 mM Tris-H 2 SO 4 , pH 8.0). The enzyme fraction was then loaded onto a CM cation-exchange column (Cytiva), pre-equilibrated with the same buffer, and eluted with a linear K 2 SO 4 gradient (0 to 500 mM) to prevent any inhibition by NaCl. In purification processes, the enzyme activity was assessed by nitrocefin. Fractions were analysed by SDS-PAGE. The RAD-1 was collected and dialysed against buffer A. Finally, the protein was concentrated using a 10 kDa Amicon ® Ultra-15 centrifugal filter device (Millipore), and the concentration was determined using the BCA kit (Tiangen). Enzyme kinetics Purified RAD-1 enzyme was used for kinetic measurements under the same reaction conditions reported previously. 28 The hydrolysis rates were determined continuously with a SpectraMax iD3 plate reader (Molecular Devices) at 30°C. The wavelengths and absorption coefficients of β-lactams were as described previously. 11 , 29 The kinetic parameters ( K m and k cat ) were calculated from the Michaelis–Menten equation employing GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software). The IC 50 s of inhibitors, including clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam and avibactam, were determined as described previously. 11 Nucleotide sequence accession number The complete genome sequence of SCVM0004 and protein sequence of RAD-1 reported in this paper have been submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers CP104076 and UWS41116 , respectively.

Tazobactam was fixed at a concentration of 4 mg/L. To reveal possible β-lactamases in SCVM0004, we sequenced its whole genome. WGS analysis based on AMRFinderPlus identified a 777 bp ORF annotated as a putative β-lactamase gene, encoding a 258 amino acid protein (protein_id: UWS41116 ). Through the protein sequence analysis, we found four conserved amino acid sequence motifs characterized by class D β-lactamases: SXXK (positions 70 to 73, containing the catalytic serine), YGN (positions 144 to 146), WXXG (positions 153 to 156) and KTG (positions 216 to 218). 30 Consequently, we named the enzyme RAD-1 (an abbreviation from R iemerella a natipestifer , carbapenem-hydrolysing class D β-lactamase), which was recognized by NCBI ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/submit-beta-lactamase/ ). Multiple sequence alignment showed that RAD-1 was weakly related to other characterized class D β-lactamases (sharing at most 42% amino acid identity). Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis revealed that RAD-1 was located adjacent to the clade containing OXA-209 and OXA-347 (Figure 1 ). 12 , 13 Therefore, RAD-1 was identified as a novel class D β-lactamase and showed a close evolutionary relationship with OXA subfamily β-lactamases. Evolutionary and distribution of RAD β-lactamase The distribution of the bla RAD-1 gene was explored in the R. anatipestifer population by analysing 33 complete and 93 draft genome sequences from the NCBI database. The results showed that more than half of them (75 out of 126; 59.52%) harboured a single-copy chromosomal bla RAD-1 gene. Notably, the bla RAD-1 gene had a high prevalence in clade 6 (67/70; 96%, Figure 2a ). In contrast, bla RAD-1 was barely present in clade 1, clade 2, clade 3 and clade 4. Interestingly, clade 5 appeared to be in an intermediate state, where half of the strains (4/8) harboured the bla RAD-1 gene. The non-uniform distribution of bla RAD-1 in the lineage was statistically significant (chi-squared test, χ 2 = 82.27, P value < 2.2 × 10 −16 ). Genome database search results showed that the bla RAD-1 -positive R. anatipestifer strains were mainly collected from China (70/75). The earliest bla RAD-1 -positive R. anatipestifer strain was RCAD0179, collected from Sichuan Province, China in 1995 (Table S2 ). Apart from that, bla RAD-1 -positive strains RA1 (2011), 17CS0503 (2017), and 1879 (2018) were collected from India, Germany and Russia, respectively. To appreciate the diversity of RAD β-lactamase on the amino acid sequence level, we utilized BLAST software from the NCBI to search the homologous proteins using the sequence of the mature RAD-1 as the template. The results showed that 75 RAD β-lactamases of R. anatipestifer strains isolated from different parts of the world were identified as two variants. RAD-1 and RAD-2 exhibited only one amino acid difference (position 249, Asn→Ser). Additionally, the prevalent variant was RAD-1 (65/75; 86.7%, Figure 2a ). Furthermore, the genomic context illustrated that bla RAD-1 was constantly surrounded by two conservative loci (Figure 2b ), one of which referred to a permease gene, and the other denoted the dgt , encoding d eoxy g uanosinetriphosphate t riphosphohydrolase. The coexistence of the permease gene and dgt was stable in R. anatipestifer , even in bla RAD-1 -negative strains (Figure 2b and Figure S1 ). It was worth noting that as long as there were any coding fragments inserted into the permease gene and dgt , a highly c onserved h ypothetical p rotein (CHP) coding gene chp could be found (Figure 2b and Figure S1 , cyan arrow). The organization of these gene pairs was reminiscent of IS mobile elements. However, CHP cannot be retrieved from the bacterial mobile genetic elements database (mobileOG and VRprofile2) by domain or sequence similarity. 31 , 32 For class D β-lactamases, most were identified in the chromosome as an intrinsic resistance determinant, while only a few were found in mobile genetic elements. 26 , 30 The bla RAD-1 gene reported in this study was located chromosomally and distributed widely in R. anatipestifer. However, despite several attempts, we failed to obtain any positive transformants by natural transformation. The result indicated that bla RAD-1 was not mobilized from the genome of SCVM0004. We speculated that the bla RAD-1 gene was disseminated among R. anatipestifer through unidentified mobile genetic elements. Impact of RAD-1 on antibiotic susceptibility to β-lactams In order to determine whether RAD-1 of R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 possesses β-lactamase activity and could confer resistance to β-lactams, the bla RAD-1 gene was cloned into pET24a and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). The MIC results showed that RAD-1 increased resistance to all tested β-lactams except cefoxitin. This enzyme elevated the MICs of the penicillins amoxicillin and ampicillin, the cephalosporin ceftazidime, the monobactam aztreonam, and the carbapenem meropenem by 16- to 128-fold. The MICs of other various β-lactams are elevated by only 2- to 8-fold in the presence of this enzyme. Moreover, RAD-1 is susceptible to inhibition by classical class A β-lactamase inhibitors including clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam and avibactam. These inhibitors decreased the β-lactam resistance (up to 32-fold). According to the ability of the enzyme to produce resistance to penicillins, expanded-spectrum cephalosporins, a monobactam and carbapenems, RAD-1 was classified as CHDL. 30 The RAD-1 β-lactamase displays broad-spectrum activity To evaluate the substrate profile of the RAD-1, we purified the native RAD-1 and performed kinetic studies. RAD-1 exhibited high catalytic efficiency for the penicillins ampicillin and benzylpenicillin, and the cephalosporin nitrocefin (Table 2 ). Comparatively, the catalytic efficiency determined for other cephalosporins, cefepime, cefuroxime, cefotaxime and cefalotin were two to three orders of magnitude lower ( k cat / K m , 2.8 × 10 3 to 3.3 × 10 4 s −1 M −1 ). Moreover, the hydrolysis spectrum of RAD-1 encompassed the cephalosporin ceftazidime and the monobactam aztreonam, exhibiting relatively low catalytic efficiencies ( k cat / K m , 57 and 2.1 × 10 2 s −1 M −1 , respectively) and high-micromolar affinities ( K m , 729 μM and 2565 μM, respectively). However, hydrolysis of cefoxitin and oxacillin was not detected. It was worth noting that RAD-1 could hydrolyse all tested carbapenems, exhibiting high-affinity levels ( K m , 15 to 20 μM) and relatively low turnover numbers ( k cat , 0.04 to 0.12 s −1 ). Catalytic efficiency values of RAD-1 for carbapenems were similar to that determined for the extended-spectrum cephalosporins. ## EVOLUTIONARY AND DISTRIBUTION OF RAD Β-LACTAMASE
The distribution of the bla RAD-1 gene was explored in the R. anatipestifer population by analysing 33 complete and 93 draft genome sequences from the NCBI database. The results showed that more than half of them (75 out of 126; 59.52%) harboured a single-copy chromosomal bla RAD-1 gene. Notably, the bla RAD-1 gene had a high prevalence in clade 6 (67/70; 96%, Figure 2a ). In contrast, bla RAD-1 was barely present in clade 1, clade 2, clade 3 and clade 4. Interestingly, clade 5 appeared to be in an intermediate state, where half of the strains (4/8) harboured the bla RAD-1 gene. The non-uniform distribution of bla RAD-1 in the lineage was statistically significant (chi-squared test, χ 2 = 82.27, P value < 2.2 × 10 −16 ). Genome database search results showed that the bla RAD-1 -positive R. anatipestifer strains were mainly collected from China (70/75). The earliest bla RAD-1 -positive R. anatipestifer strain was RCAD0179, collected from Sichuan Province, China in 1995 (Table S2 ). Apart from that, bla RAD-1 -positive strains RA1 (2011), 17CS0503 (2017), and 1879 (2018) were collected from India, Germany and Russia, respectively. To appreciate the diversity of RAD β-lactamase on the amino acid sequence level, we utilized BLAST software from the NCBI to search the homologous proteins using the sequence of the mature RAD-1 as the template. The results showed that 75 RAD β-lactamases of R. anatipestifer strains isolated from different parts of the world were identified as two variants. RAD-1 and RAD-2 exhibited only one amino acid difference (position 249, Asn→Ser). Additionally, the prevalent variant was RAD-1 (65/75; 86.7%, Figure 2a ). The organization of these gene pairs was reminiscent of IS mobile elements. However, CHP cannot be retrieved from the bacterial mobile genetic elements database (mobileOG and VRprofile2) by domain or sequence similarity. 31 , 32 For class D β-lactamases, most were identified in the chromosome as an intrinsic resistance determinant, while only a few were found in mobile genetic elements. 26 , 30 The bla RAD-1 gene reported in this study was located chromosomally and distributed widely in R. anatipestifer. However, despite several attempts, we failed to obtain any positive transformants by natural transformation. The result indicated that bla RAD-1 was not mobilized from the genome of SCVM0004. We speculated that the bla RAD-1 gene was disseminated among R. anatipestifer through unidentified mobile genetic elements.

## IMPACT OF RAD-1 ON ANTIBIOTIC SUSCEPTIBILITY TO Β-LACTAMS
In order to determine whether RAD-1 of R. anatipestifer SCVM0004 possesses β-lactamase activity and could confer resistance to β-lactams, the bla RAD-1 gene was cloned into pET24a and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). The MIC results showed that RAD-1 increased resistance to all tested β-lactams except cefoxitin. This enzyme elevated the MICs of the penicillins amoxicillin and ampicillin, the cephalosporin ceftazidime, the monobactam aztreonam, and the carbapenem meropenem by 16- to 128-fold. The MICs of other various β-lactams are elevated by only 2- to 8-fold in the presence of this enzyme. Moreover, RAD-1 is susceptible to inhibition by classical class A β-lactamase inhibitors including clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam and avibactam. These inhibitors decreased the β-lactam resistance (up to 32-fold). According to the ability of the enzyme to produce resistance to penicillins, expanded-spectrum cephalosporins, a monobactam and carbapenems, RAD-1 was classified as CHDL. 30

## THE RAD-1 Β-LACTAMASE DISPLAYS BROAD-SPECTRUM ACTIVITY
To evaluate the substrate profile of the RAD-1, we purified the native RAD-1 and performed kinetic studies. RAD-1 exhibited high catalytic efficiency for the penicillins ampicillin and benzylpenicillin, and the cephalosporin nitrocefin (Table 2 ). Comparatively, the catalytic efficiency determined for other cephalosporins, cefepime, cefuroxime, cefotaxime and cefalotin were two to three orders of magnitude lower ( k cat / K m , 2.8 × 10 3 to 3.3 × 10 4 s −1 M −1 ). Moreover, the hydrolysis spectrum of RAD-1 encompassed the cephalosporin ceftazidime and the monobactam aztreonam, exhibiting relatively low catalytic efficiencies ( k cat / K m , 57 and 2.1 × 10 2 s −1 M −1 , respectively) and high-micromolar affinities ( K m , 729 μM and 2565 μM, respectively). However, hydrolysis of cefoxitin and oxacillin was not detected. It was worth noting that RAD-1 could hydrolyse all tested carbapenems, exhibiting high-affinity levels ( K m , 15 to 20 μM) and relatively low turnover numbers ( k cat , 0.04 to 0.12 s −1 ).
