                  <ce:simple-para id="spara40" view="all">From 2002 to 2006, 35 of 73 multidrug-resistant <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates from different wards at Charles Nicolle hospital of Tunis were positive for class B carbapenemase (using the imipenem-EDTA test), owing to a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>

Despite belonging to the same chromosomal type, as shown by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), the VIM-2 producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates prevalent at Charles Nicolle hospital displayed a diversity of VIM-2-carrying integrons.</ce:simple-para>

            <ce:sections>
               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Carbapenems are among the drugs of choice for the treatment of infections due to multidrug-resistant (MDR) <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa.</ce:italic> However, their efficacy is being increasingly compromised by the emergence of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> strains producing metallo-<ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamases (MBLs) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref80" refid="bib1">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>], which are implicated in large outbreaks, as described in Greece, Italy, Canada, Korea and Kenya [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib2 bib3 bib4 bib5 bib6">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">2–6</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>]. We report the first molecular characterization of Tunisian MBL-producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates, some of which were also extended-spectrum <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamase producers.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">A total of 73 MDR <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates (defined as being resistant to at least three different families of antibiotics) were collected at Charles Nicolle hospital of Tunis. Among these isolates, 35 were positive according to the EDTA disk synergy test (AB Biodisk, Solna, Sweden), suggesting the presence of a class B enzyme. The frequency of MBL-producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> among imipenem-resistant isolates was stable between 2002 and 2004, but increased dramatically from 2005: 1% (1/99) in 2002, 1% (1/111) in 2003, 1% (1/85) in 2004, 25% (16/65) in 2005, and 28% (16/57) in 2006. This is consistent with the worldwide increase in the frequency of MBL producers among <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> clinical isolates, particularly in Europe [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib1 bib2 bib3 bib4 bib7 bib8">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">1–4,7,8</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>]. MICs of ticarcillin, ticarcillin–clavulanic acid (2 mg/L), aztreonam, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem and rifampin were determined using the dilution method on Mueller–Hinton agar, according to CLSI guidelines [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref90" refid="bib9">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. The MIC determinations showed high levels of resistance to all <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactams (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref100" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">In order to determine whether the MBL-producing isolates were clonally related, pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) of <ce:italic>Spe</ce:italic>I-digested genomic DNA samples was performed with a CHEF-DRIII apparatus (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). PFGE patterns were interpreted according to the criteria of van Belkum <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref110" refid="bib10">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">10</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. The distribution of the PFGE patterns is shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref120" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>. Only one PFGE type was found, suggesting a clonal outbreak, including three subtypes differing from each other by one to four bands.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para40" view="all">PCR amplification with primers for <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">TEM</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">IMP</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> (Table S1) was performed to further investigate the presence of <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamase genes. Sequencing of the amplified fragments confirmed the presence of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> in all isolates. Many studies have reported that VIM-2 is the most prevalent type of MBL in various Mediterranean countries, i.e. Italy, Greece and France [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib1 bib2 bib3 bib7">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">1–3,7</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>], but this is the first report of the VIM-2 MBL in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> from Tunisia and the second from the African continent [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref130" refid="bib6">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">6</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para50" view="all">PCR and sequencing revealed that SHV-2a was the only class A enzyme present in our set of isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). SHV-2a has been previously described in a clinical isolate of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> recovered from a French patient hospitalized in Tunisia [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="bib11">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">11</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. The coexistence of class A and class B enzymes in the same isolate has been described in <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolates from Tunisia [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref160" refid="bib12">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">12</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. Many studies have described the association between members of these two classes of enzymes: VIM-1 and SHV-5 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="bib13">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">13</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>] and VIM-4 and SHV-12 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib14">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">14</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>] in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic>, and PER-1 and VIM-2 in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib15 bib16">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">15,16</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para60" view="all">To analyse the genetic support of these <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamase genes, conjugational transfers were performed with <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> PAO38 rifR or <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> J53–2 rifR as the recipient strains, with selection on aztreonam (4 mg/L), or ticarcillin (125 mg/L), or imipenem (2 mg/L) and rifampin (250 mg/L). Isolates 8 and 11 (with rifampin MICs &lt;32 mg/L), which produced both VIM-2 and SHV-2a, were tested as donor strains. Transfer experiments were unsuccessful for both genes.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para70" view="all">DNA fragments obtained from partially <ce:italic>Sau</ce:italic>3A-digested genomic DNA of two representative isolates (1 and 4) were ligated into the vector pACYC184 digested with <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI. <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> DH10B transformants were selected on Mueller–Hinton agar supplemented with chloramphenicol (50 mg/L) and ticarcillin (125 mg/L). The inserted DNA fragments and the genetic organization of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf> were investigated by PCR and sequencing, using the primers listed in Table S1.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para80" view="all">First, the cloned 5599-bp fragment from isolate 1 (D1463a) was found to carry a class 1 integron. The cassette region contained <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>aacA7</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aacA4</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="fig1">Fig. 1a</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>). By PCR mapping and sequencing, the same genetic organization was found for ten additional isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). In the upstream part of this integron, an insertion sequence element, IS<ce:italic>Pa7</ce:italic>, was bracketed by two 17-bp inverted repeats, as described previously in In70 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib3">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para90" view="all">On the basis of PCR mapping, all other isolates also harboured IS<ce:italic>Pa7, intll</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf>. This cassette was found in the first position of the integron, which indicates that it was the most recently acquired resistance gene. PCR with reverse primers specific to the 3'-CS of class 1 integrons gave PCR products of various sizes. In six isolates, the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> gene cassette was found to be part of an integron containing two additional cassettes: <ce:italic>aadB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>arr6</ce:italic>, a novel rifampin ADP ribosyl transferase gene (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="fig1">Fig. 1a</ce:cross-ref>). Indeed, ARR-6 shares 75–80% amino acid identity with ARR-2, ARR-3, ARR-4 and ARR-5. The remaining 18 isolates displayed a novel structure for class 1 integrons involving two pairs of cassettes: either <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>aacA7</ce:italic>, or <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>arr6</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>a).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para100" view="all">These combinations of gene cassettes are different from those reported in Kenya [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib6">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">6</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. Analysis of integron structures yielded identical profiles in all isolates of PFGE subtype A1 and in some isolates of subtype A3, suggesting a conserved structure of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf>-containing integrons. However, isolates of subtype A2 were shown to contain two new integron structures.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para110" view="all">All isolates producing ARR-6 showed high levels of resistance to rifampin (MIC range 256–1024 mg/L), whereas the MIC of rifampin was &lt;32 mg/L for all others isolates except one (128 mg/L) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Using the Intll-Vim-2-for and the 3'CS primers and the Topo XL PCR cloning kit (Invitrogen, Cergy-Pontoise, France), the 2-kb PCR product (obtained from isolate 10; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) was cloned into electrocompetent <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10. After selection on kanamycin (50 mg/L) and ticarcillin (125 mg/L), the recombinant clone harboured <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">VIM-2</ce:inf> (PCR-positive) and showed a high level of resistance to rifampin (MIC &gt;256 mg/L).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Second, the cloned 3373-bp fragment from isolate 4 (B2781) contained an IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic> insertion element, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf>, and Tn<ce:italic>1721</ce:italic>; the sequences immediately upstream and downstream from <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf> showed 100% DNA identity with parts of the plasmid pMPA2a from <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KpZU-3 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib17">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">17</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>] and with the plasmid pPL20 (pPa-1) from <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> RP-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib11">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">11</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>] (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="fig1">Fig. 1b</ce:cross-ref>). The more distantly related sequences downstream from <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf> had 100% identity with Tn<ce:italic>1721</ce:italic>, a transposon often found in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib18">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">18</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. On the basis of PCR mapping, all isolates harbouring <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf> had this same structure.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para130" view="all">This article describes a high frequency of VIM-2 among MDR <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates from Tunisia, as well as the presence of new integrons encoding a VIM-2-type MBL and the detection of a novel gene cassette containing a rifampin resistance gene in some of these isolates. The nucleotide sequences have been submitted to the EMBL/GenBank under accession numbers AM774408, AM988778, AM988779, FM897214, and FM955585.</ce:para>
