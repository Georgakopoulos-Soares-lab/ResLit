                  Objectives
                  Ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA) and cefiderocol are recently commercialized molecules active against highly drug-resistant bacteria, including carbapenem-resistant members of the Enterobacteriaceae. Mutants resistant to CZA have been described, notably in Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) producers. Considering the structural similarities between ceftazidime and cefiderocol, we hypothesized that resistance to CZA in KPC-producing members of the Enterobacterales may lead to cross-resistance to cefiderocol.
               
                  Methods
                  CZA-resistant mutants from three clinical isolates of the Enterobacterales carrying either bla
                     KPC-2 or bla
                     KPC-3 were selected in vitro. Mutants with increased MIC to CZA compared to the ancestral allele were cloned in a pBR322 plasmid and expressed in Escherichia coli TOP10. We evaluated the impact of these mutations on cefiderocol MICs and minimal bactericidal concentrations (MBCs), and we assessed the impact of bacterial inoculum size on cefiderocol MICs.
               
                  Results
                  We used 37 KPC mutants with increased CZA MICs. Of these, six have been described previously in clinical isolates. Compared to the wild-type alleles, increases in the cefiderocol MICs of 4- to 32-fold were observed for 75.6% of tested mutants (28/37), MICs reaching up to 4 mg/L in E. coli TOP10 for KPC-31 (D179Y–H274Y mutations). MBCs and MICs of cefiderocol were similar, confirming the bactericidal activity of this drug. Finally, when using higher inocula (107 CFU/mL), a large increase in cefiderocol MIC was observed, and all isolates were categorized as resistant.
               
                  Conclusion
                  We observed that most of the CZA-resistant KPC variants have a possible impact on cefiderocol by increasing the cefiderocol MICs. In addition, cefiderocol is greatly impacted by the inoculum effect, suggesting that precautions should be taken when treating infections with a suspected high inoculum.

            <ce:abstract id="abs0010" xml:lang="en" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0010" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0015">Objectives</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">Ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA) and cefiderocol are recently commercialized molecules active against highly drug-resistant bacteria, including carbapenem-resistant members of the Enterobacteriaceae. Mutants resistant to CZA have been described, notably in <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> carbapenemase (KPC) producers. Considering the structural similarities between ceftazidime and cefiderocol, we hypothesized that resistance to CZA in KPC-producing members of the Enterobacterales may lead to cross-resistance to cefiderocol.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0015" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0020">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0015" view="all">CZA-resistant mutants from three clinical isolates of the Enterobacterales carrying either <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> or <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> were selected <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic>. Mutants with increased MIC to CZA compared to the ancestral allele were cloned in a pBR322 plasmid and expressed in <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> TOP10. We evaluated the impact of these mutations on cefiderocol MICs and minimal bactericidal concentrations (MBCs), and we assessed the impact of bacterial inoculum size on cefiderocol MICs.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0020" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0025">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0020" view="all">We used 37 KPC mutants with increased CZA MICs. Of these, six have been described previously in clinical isolates. Compared to the wild-type alleles, increases in the cefiderocol MICs of 4- to 32-fold were observed for 75.6% of tested mutants (28/37), MICs reaching up to 4 mg/L in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10 for KPC-31 (D179Y–H274Y mutations). MBCs and MICs of cefiderocol were similar, confirming the bactericidal activity of this drug. Finally, when using higher inocula (10<ce:sup loc="post">7</ce:sup> CFU/mL), a large increase in cefiderocol MIC was observed, and all isolates were categorized as resistant.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0025" role="conclusion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0030">Conclusion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0025" view="all">We observed that most of the CZA-resistant KPC variants have a possible impact on cefiderocol by increasing the cefiderocol MICs. In addition, cefiderocol is greatly impacted by the inoculum effect, suggesting that precautions should be taken when treating infections with a suspected high inoculum.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:section id="sec1" role="introduction" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0040">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">Antimicrobial resistance in Gram-negative bacteria is a major public health concern, with predictions of catastrophic developments in the coming years [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0080" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. Ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA) and cefiderocol are two of the most recently commercialized β-lactam molecules. They are active against highly drug-resistant bacteria, including carbapenem-resistant members of the Enterobacteriaceae. Avibactam inhibits Ambler class A, class C and some class D β-lactamases. Cefiderocol is a siderophore cephalosporin sharing side chains similar to those of both cefepime and ceftazidime. It also has a chlorocatechol group which confers a siderophore-like structure acting as a decoy for the bacteria. The cefiderocol structure allows rapid penetration into bacteria and resistance to hydrolysis by most β-lactamases regardless of their class [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0085" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">Soon after the commercialization of CZA in 2015, resistant mutants were described both <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic>, notably <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> carbapenemase (KPC) producers [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0090" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0095" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>]. KPC β-lactamase has spread worldwide, challenging the therapeutic management of infected patients. Cefiderocol shows excellent <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> activity against most Gram-negative bacteria, notably those producing KPC, and promising results are also observed <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0100" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. However, although rare, some resistant mutants have been described, and the mechanisms underlying this resistance remain poorly understood. Mutations in iron transporters (<ce:italic>cirA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>fiu</ce:italic> of <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic>) have been suggested, and some β-lactamases have been implicated [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0105" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Because of the structural similarities between cefiderocol and ceftazidime, we hypothesized that resistance to CZA in KPC-producing members of the Enterobacterales could lead to cross-resistance to cefiderocol. We used <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> KPC β-lactamase CZA-resistant mutants and evaluated the impact of these mutations on cefiderocol MICs. We also evaluated minimal bactericidal concentrations (MBCs) and the impact of the inoculum size on cefiderocol MICs.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0045">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.1" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="sectitle0050">Isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">We used the previously described CZA-resistant mutants containing insertions or deletions in the coding region of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> as well as new mutants obtained from <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> selection [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0110" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Briefly, three isolates of KPC-producing Enterobacteriaceae were used for <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> selection of CZA-resistant mutants as previously described. These were strains of <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> and of <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> harbouring both <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> (RD26 and DD35), and an <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> strain harbouring <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> (RD29); <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> differ by one mutation, the H274Y substitution. Mutants of these isolates were selected by plating overnight cultures on Mueller–Hinton agar containing a concentration of CZA (4:1 ratio) four times higher than the MIC. Growing colonies were screened by PCR/sequencing of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene. The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene of the mutants with increased MICs to CZA was then cloned in a pBR322 plasmid (Gibson assembly) and expressed in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10 [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0115" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.2" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="sectitle0055">Minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC)</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">CZA, ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem and cefiderocol MICs were determined in triplicate on ancestral alleles and their mutants expressed in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10 using either the Etest method (bioMérieux, France) or by broth microdilution using the Sensititre plates and cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth as specified by the manufacturer (Thermo Scientific). Results were interpreted using EUCAST breakpoints: 8 mg/L for CZA and 2 mg/L for cefiderocol [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0120" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. The reference strain <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> ATCC25922 was used as a control.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">To evaluate the impact of the bacterial inoculum, cefiderocol MICs were also performed with an inoculum increased from 10<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup> to 10<ce:sup loc="post">7</ce:sup> CFU/mL.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.3" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="sectitle0060">Minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC)</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">Cefiderocol MBCs were determined by plating the dilutions corresponding to the MIC, and two-, four- and eight-fold MIC, on Mueller–Hinton agar. After 18 hours of growth, we enumerated the number of viable CFU/mL. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration that demonstrates a predetermined reduction (such as 99.9%) in CFU/mL when compared with the MIC dilution.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec3" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0065">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">In total, 37 mutants with increased CZA MIC were used for this study. Of these, 17 containing insertions or deletions in the KPC gene have been described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0125" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>], including three in clinical isolates (KPC-25, KPC-29 and KPC-44). Twenty different non-synonymous mutations were also investigated, including three previously described in clinical isolates (KPC-31, KPC-33 and KPC-39), six in <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> experiments, and 11 that are, to our knowledge, new from this study [<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0010" refid="bib8 bib9 bib10 bib11">8–11</ce:cross-refs>] (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0130" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">MICs are summarized in <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0135" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>. Cefiderocol MICs were 0.25 and 0.12 μg/mL <ce:italic>for E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10-KPC-2 and -KPC-3, respectively. Taking into account the expected variability in the MIC determination of log<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> factor, we considered only ≥4-fold increases in the cefiderocol MIC compared to that of the wild-type alleles. Such an increase (≥4-fold to 32-fold increase) was observed in 75.6 % of the mutants (28/37). We found the highest increase in both D179Y and D179Y associated with H274Y mutants. Only the latter was categorized as resistant to a cefiderocol MIC of 4 mg/L [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0140" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">After a 24-hour culture, MBCs and MICs of cefiderocol were similar, and so for all tested isolates, suggesting bactericidal activity.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">Compared to the usual inoculum (10<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup> CFU/mL), we observed an increase in cefiderocol MICs when large inocula (10<ce:sup loc="post">7</ce:sup> CFU/mL) were used with all tested isolates categorized as resistant according to EUCAST breakpoints (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0145" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0150" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec4" role="discussion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0070">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">Cefiderocol shows a good <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> activity on Gram-negative bacteria and especially on members of the Enterobacterales [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0155" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0160" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]. Resistant strains are rare, and the underlying mechanisms have been little explored.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">Production of KPC β-lactamases is a common carbapenem resistance mechanism among members of the Enterobacterales [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0165" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>]. Cefiderocol shows a good activity on KPC-producing enterobacteria, and to our knowledge no resistant KPC-producing isolates have been described to date [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0170" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. Here, we show that the most widely spread alleles of KPC (i.e. KPC-2 and KPC-3) are associated with low cefiderocol MIC, close to the ECOFF of 0.25 mg/L for <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (<ce:inter-ref id="intref0010" xlink:href="https://www.eucast.org/mic_distributions_and_ecoffs/" xlink:type="simple">https://www.eucast.org/mic_distributions_and_ecoffs/</ce:inter-ref>). However, in 75.6% of the KPC mutants (28/37) studied here the cefiderocol MIC increased (four to 32 times) compared to the ancestral allele, reaching an MIC higher than the clinical breakpoint (defined as ≤2 mg/L in EUCAST) [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0175" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. As all KPC mutants were cloned in pBR322 and expressed in the same genetic background (<ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Top10), we can assume that the mutation in KPC β-lactamase is the only factor responsible for the increased cefiderocol MIC. Although we conducted an <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> study, 16.2% (6/37) of the reported mutants have been described in clinical isolates, notably D179Y (KPC-33) and D179Y–H274Y (KPC-31) [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0180" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>]. Here, we emphasize that some mutations can also extend the KPC β-lactamase spectrum to cefiderocol, and may confer cross-resistance to both of the latest generation of commercialized antibiotics. A better understanding of the underlying resistance mechanisms and a precise epidemiological follow-up of selected <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> mutants will be of paramount importance to avoid the emergence of resistance to this new molecule.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">For all tested KPC mutants, after a 24-hour culture, cefiderocol MBC and MIC were similar, suggesting a strong bactericidal effect of this drug. We also noticed a high impact of the inoculum on the cefiderocol MIC. By increasing the inoculum from 10<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup> to 10<ce:sup loc="post">7</ce:sup> CFU/mL, we observed a large increase in the MIC for all isolates tested (up to &gt;32 mg/L). These <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> data suggest that caution should be exercised when treating potentially high-inoculum infections.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">This study was performed only <ce:italic>in vitro,</ce:italic> and further studies should include <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> experiments to measure the impact of KPC β-lactamase mutants and the inoculum effect on resistance to cefiderocol.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0085" view="all">In summary, we show that cefiderocol MIC can increase in KPC β-lactamase mutants. In our study, the clinical allele KPC-31 showed cross-resistance to both ceftazidime–avibactam and cefiderocol [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0185" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. Considering the large inoculum effect on cefiderocol MICs, this molecule should be used cautiously in the case of infections with a suspected high inoculum.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
