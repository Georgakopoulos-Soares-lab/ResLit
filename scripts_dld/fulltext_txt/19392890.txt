                  The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of the plasmid-borne quinolone resistance genes qnrA, qnrB and qnrS in a collection of Enterobacteriaceae causing bacteraemia. The presence of the three genes was tested for using multiplex PCR in 306 clinical isolates. Plasmid analysis was performed using I-CeuI and S1 nuclease digestion and hybridization with specific probes for the qnr and 23S rRNA genes. Five strains were found to carry a qnr gene, one of which, qnrB 16, a new variant of qnrB, was detected in a Citrobacter freundii isolate. The qnrB6 variant was found in two C. freundii isolates and in one Citrobacter werkmanii isolate. The qnrS2 gene was found in one Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate. The qnrA gene was not found in any of the isolates studied. The qnrS2 gene was located on a plasmid of c. 50 kb, whereas qnrB6 and qnrB16 were inserted in the chromosome between pspF and the orf2, which had previously been found in a complex integron. In the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona, Spain, the prevalence of qnrB was higher than that of qnrA and qnrS. The importance of the description of the new qnrB16 is emphasized.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara80" view="all">The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of the plasmid-borne quinolone resistance genes <ce:italic>qnrA, qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> in a collection of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> causing bacteraemia. The presence of the three genes was tested for using multiplex PCR in 306 clinical isolates. Plasmid analysis was performed using <ce:italic>I-CeuI</ce:italic> and S1 nuclease digestion and hybridization with specific probes for the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> and 23S rRNA genes. Five strains were found to carry a <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> gene, one of which, <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16, a new variant of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>, was detected in a <ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii</ce:italic> isolate. The <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 variant was found in two <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> isolates and in one <ce:italic>Citrobacter werkmanii</ce:italic> isolate. The <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 gene was found in one <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate. The <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene was not found in any of the isolates studied. The <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 gene was located on a plasmid of <ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 50 kb, whereas <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 and <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>16 were inserted in the chromosome between <ce:italic>pspF</ce:italic> and the <ce:italic>orf</ce:italic>2, which had previously been found in a complex integron. In the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona, Spain, the prevalence of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> was higher than that of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS.</ce:italic> The importance of the description of the new <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>16 is emphasized.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Quinolones are a group of antimicrobial agents with good activity against Gram-negative bacteria. Quinolones act by inhibiting the activity of type II topoisomerases (DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib2">1,2</ce:cross-refs>]. The DNA gyrase introduces negative superhelical turns into DNA, whereas topoisomerase IV relaxes DNA. Mutations in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> (encoding the A subunit of the DNA gyrase) augment quinolone resistance, typically four-fold to eight-fold [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib2 bib3">2,3</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Development of fluoroquinolone resistance in Gram-negative bacteria is due to two main factors: (i) mutations in the genes encoding the drug targets; [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib1 bib2 bib4">1,2,4</ce:cross-refs>] and (ii) decreased intracellular accumulation of the antimicrobial agent through decreased cell wall permeability or overexpression of an efflux pump system [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib1 bib2">1,2</ce:cross-refs>]. In 1998, Martínez-Martínez <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref50" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>] reported that a multiresistant isolate of <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> from a urine culture collected in Birmingham, Alabama contained a broad-host-range plasmid (pMG252) that, in <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> transconjugants, increased resistance to nalidixic acid from 4 to 32 mg/L, and resistance to ciprofloxacin from 0.008 to 0.25 mg/L. Because pMG252 did not alter host porin expression and did not reduce quinolone accumulation, the possibility of a novel resistance mechanism was suggested, and the responsible gene was called <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref60" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. Qnr proteins belong to the pentapeptide-repeat family, which is defined by a five amino acid tandem repeat with the recurrent motif (Ser, Thr, Ala or Val)-(Asp or Asn)-(Leu or Phe)-(Ser, Thr or Arg)-(Gly) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref70" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>]. At present, six variants of QnrA, two variants of QnrS and 19 variants of QnrB have been described worldwide [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref80" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Although the action of Qnr results in only low-level quinolone resistance, this reduced susceptibility facilitates the selection of mutants with higher-level resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref90" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. It is thought that this low level of resistance to the antibacterial agent would facilitate the occurrence of secondary mutations and causing an increase in the level of resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref100" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">The geographical distribution of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> is known to be wide. The spread of these and new additional <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes could have a substantial impact on the treatment of Gram-negative bacterial infections with fluoroquinolones [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref110" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para50" view="all">In this study, the prevalence of the plasmid-borne quinolone resistance genes <ce:italic>qnrA, qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> was studied in a collection of 306 isolates of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> causing bacteraemia.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">Materials and Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Bacterial isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">Three hundred and six isolates of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> from blood samples were collected between 2001 and 2002 at the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona, Spain: 111 <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (36.2%), 54 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> (17.6%), 28 <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic> (9.2%), 25 <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> (8.2%), 22 <ce:italic>Serratia</ce:italic> marcescens (7.2%), 21 Salmonella <ce:italic>enteritidis</ce:italic> (6.9%), 16 <ce:italic>Klebsiella oxytoca</ce:italic> (5.2%), eight <ce:italic>Salmonella typhimurium</ce:italic> (2.6%), six <ce:italic>Enterobacter aerogenes</ce:italic> (2%), five <ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii</ce:italic> (1.6%), four <ce:italic>Citrobacter koseri</ce:italic> (1.3%), three <ce:italic>Morganella morganii</ce:italic> (1%), two <ce:italic>Pantoea agglomerans</ce:italic> (0.7%), and one <ce:italic>Citrobacter werkmanii</ce:italic> (0.3%).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">PCR amplification and sequencing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">Screening for <ce:italic>qnrA, qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> was performed by multiplex PCR, using a cocktail of specific primers [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref120" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>]. Bacterial strains positive for each <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> gene were used as positive controls, and were run in each batch of tested samples [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref130" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>]. Positive reactions were confirmed by direct sequencing of the PCR products. To complete the sequences of the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes, an inverse PCR was performed, using DNA digested with BfaI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">The fragments obtained were autoligated overnight at 16°C using T4 Ligase (Promega Biotech Iberica, Madrid, Spain), and used as a template for a PCR with inverse primers designed from the amplified sequences (qnrevI, 5'-CAGAGCCATATTTGTACCTG-3'; and qnrevII, 5'-GTTGG ACAACTACCAGGCAT-3'). The sequences obtained were compared with those deposited in GenBank to determine the corresponding <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> variant. The quinolone resistancedetermining region (QRDR) of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> of the <ce:italic>Citrobacter</ce:italic> spp. isolates was sequenced directly from PCR-amplified DNA, using the specific primers previously described [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>]. To amplify the QRDR of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> of <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, the primers used were: E-1 (5'-AAATCTGCCCGTGTCGTTGGT-3') and E-2 (5'-GCCATACCTACGGCGATACC-3') for <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic>; and PARC1 (5'-AAACCTGTTCAGCGCCGCATT-3') and PARC2 (5'-GTGGTGCCGTTAAGCAAA-3') for <ce:italic>parC.</ce:italic>
                     </ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Antimicrobial susceptibility testing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">MICs were determined using the Etest method, and CLSI breakpoints were used to define susceptibility [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]. The</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">MICs of nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin (IZASA, Barcelona, Spain) were determined.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Conjugation experiments</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">Conjugation was attempted using a liquid mating-out assay with the reference strain <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> J53 Rif<ce:sup loc="post">R</ce:sup> and the <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> 46408 clinical isolates. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller–Hinton agar plates containing rifampicin (50 mg/L) and nalidixic acid (16 mg/L). Additionally, agar plates containing rifampicin (50 mg/L) and ampicillin (50 mg/L) were used to search for co-transfer of quinolone resistance, using the <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 72857, <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> 46408 clinical isolates as donor strains, and <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> J53 Rif<ce:sup loc="post">R</ce:sup> as a recipient strain.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">Plasmid DNA analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Plasmid DNA identification was attempted, after digestion of total DNA with <ce:italic>I-Ceu</ce:italic>I and S1 nuclease, by Southern blot and double hybridization with probes for the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> and the 23S rRNA (<ce:italic>rrl</ce:italic> gene) genes as previously described [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib13 bib14">13,14</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec80" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">Determination of the genomic surrounding of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> genes</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para130" view="all">DNA of the four <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>-carrying <ce:italic>Citrobacter</ce:italic> spp. isolates was digested with SacI (New England Biolabs), which does not have recognition sites in the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para140" view="all">The fragments obtained were autoligated overnight at 16°C using T4 DNA Ligase (Promega Biotech Ibérica) and used as templates for a PCR with inverse primers designed from the sequences obtained with the inverse PCR performed with qnrevI and qnrevII to obtain the whole sequences of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 and <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>16 (qnrevIII, 5'-GACATCGGTTTAGTTTCCGG-3'; and qnrevIV, 5'-TCGTTCCACTTATCAAATAG-3'). DNA from the resulting bands was sequenced using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 cycle sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK).</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">Molecular typing</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Epidemiological analysis was performed for the three <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>-carrying <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> isolates in order to assess the possible clonality of the strains.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para160" view="all">The analysis was performed by digestion of the chromosomal DNA with a restriction enzyme cutting at low frequency (XbaI) followed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) under the conditions previously described [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref160" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Nucleotide sequence accession number</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">The sequence of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16 described here has been assigned GenBank accession number EU136183, as recommended by Jacoby <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para180" view="all">Five of the 306 (1.6%) <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> strains isolated as a cause of bacteraemia carried a <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> gene; four of these corresponded to <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>, and one to <ce:italic>qnrS.</ce:italic> The <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene was not found in any of the strains studied. The <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene corresponded to the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 variant, and was found in a <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> clinical isolate. A new variant of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>, named <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16, was detected in <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The amino acid sequence of QnrB16 differed from that of QnrB1 by four amino acids: S79A, I142M, A144T, and V212I. Furthermore, QnrB6 and QnrB16 differed in one amino acid, A144T [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para190" view="all">The QRDR of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> of the five isolates was amplified and sequenced. <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 showed two mutations, one in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> (T83I) and one in <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> (S80I). The other strains did not show any mutation in the analysed genes. The characteristics related to the presence of mutations in the target genes, the type of <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> determinant, and the MICs of the quinolones determined for the studied isolates are summarized in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para200" view="all">PFGE was performed to test the possibility of a clonal relationship among the three <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> clinical isolates. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/> clearly shows three different patterns of the isolates, indicating that they were not epidemiologically related; the presence and acquisition of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> determinants could therefore not be explained by vertical transmission.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">Plasmids containing the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> genes could not be purified, either with commercial plasmid extraction kits or the Kieser technique [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>]. This was the first indication of a chromosomal location of these genes. Another approach to elucidate the location of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> genes was the conjugation experiment with the <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 62778 clinical isolate. The conjugation assays using both nalidixic acid and ampicillin yielded negative results, also indicating a chromosomal location. Plasmid analysis and hybridization experiments of the <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> 46408 isolate showed that <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 was located on a plasmid of <ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 50 kb (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="fig2">Fig. 2a</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). Analysis of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> determinants in the other isolates by genomic mapping with <ce:italic>I-CeuI</ce:italic> and S1 nuclease followed by Southern blot and double hybridization with probes for the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes and for the 23S rRNA (<ce:italic>rrl</ce:italic> gene) genes (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="fig3 fig4">Figs 3 and 4</ce:cross-refs>) revealed that both <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 and <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16 were chromosomally located. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/> shows the result of a mapping experiment with <ce:italic>I-CeuI</ce:italic> using PFGE with pulse times ramped from 5 to 20 s for 19 h.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">Although we were unable to discriminate the bands to which the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> probes hybridized, the sizes suggested by the hybridization signals indicate that they derived from chromosomal DNA. Modification of the PFGE conditions as previously described by Liu <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>] provided better resolution of high molecular weight DNA bands (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). The resulting Southern blot showed that the probes for <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> hybridized outside the limits of the marker, i.e. above 750 kb, thereby making the presence of a plasmid in that region impossible. Additionally, undigested DNA from the strains <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 and <ce:italic>C. werkmanii</ce:italic> 14.0 yielded the same result when hybridized with the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> probe (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">A region of <ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 2 kb near <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 and <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16 was sequenced following inverse PCR. In both cases, the genomic surrounding was similar to that recently deposited in Genbank (accession number AJ609296), showing a <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>2 gene inserted into a class 1 integron and flanked upstream by <ce:italic>pspF</ce:italic> and downstream by <ce:italic>orf</ce:italic>2 and <ce:italic>sapA.</ce:italic>
                  </ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">QnrA, QnrB and QnrS have been found on all continents and in the clinically most common <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib8 bib17 bib18 bib19 bib20">8,17–20</ce:cross-refs>]. For the first time, a QnrS determinant has recently been reported also in an isolate of <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>]. Several studies carried out worldwide have shown the prevalence of the QnrB determinants in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib9 bib18 bib21 bib22 bib23 bib24 bib25 bib26">9,18,21–26</ce:cross-refs>]. In Spain, several studies have shown the prevalence of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib8 bib27">8,27</ce:cross-refs>]. In a study performed by Cano <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>], among 202 <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Enterobacter aerogenes</ce:italic> clinical isolates, 22 <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> carried the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> determinant. Five different repetitive extragenic palindromic (REP)-PCR profiles were obtained for the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>-positive <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic>, representing 14, four, two, one and one isolate(s), respectively. All of them were resistant to ciprofloxacin, except for four isolates, clonally related according to REPPCR, which were susceptible to ciprofloxacin and to nalidixic acid, and one isolate that was intermediately resistant to ciprofloxacin and resistant to nalidixic acid. Lavilla <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>] recently analysed 305 extended-spectrum <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamaseproducing isolates of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> for the presence of <ce:italic>qnrA, qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS.</ce:italic> Fifteen isolates carried a <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> determinant, among which 14 carried <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>1 and one <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>1. The qnr-positive strains comprised <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> (7), <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> (6), and <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (2). In a recent study, three of 55 ciprofloxacin-resistant clinical <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> isolates causing uncomplicated community-acquired cystitis were found to contain a <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>-like gene. This is the first time that a QnrB determinant has been reported in Spain [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">In this study, we report a new variant of <ce:italic>qnrB, qnrB</ce:italic>16. An interesting feature observed in the present study is the high prevalence rate of the <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> determinant in our hospital as compared with those of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>, and the high prevalence rate of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> in <ce:italic>Citrobacter</ce:italic> spp. (four of five <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic>-positive isolates). The new QnrB16 variant reported here differs from QnrB1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>] by four amino acids, i.e. S79A, I142M, A144T, and V212I. They do not concern position 115, which has been proposed as a potential point of union between Qnr and DNA or type II topoisomerases [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>]. Characterization of the interaction of Qnr with DNA and type II topoisomerases is necessary for a better understanding of the mechanism of action of Qnr and for the design of new antibacterial agents able to counteract its action.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">This is also the first report of a <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 determinant in a <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate. To date, the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 gene has been described only on a transferable IncQ-related plasmid (pGNB2) isolated from a bacterial community in activated sludge of a waste water treatment plant in Germany [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>], from a <ce:italic>Salmonella enterica</ce:italic> subsp. <ce:italic>enterica</ce:italic> serovar Anatum isolate [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>], a <ce:italic>Vibrio parahaemolyticus</ce:italic> isolate, and an <ce:italic>Aeromonas punctata</ce:italic> isolate [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para270" view="all">Only the <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> 46408 isolates were resistant to nalidixic acid. <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> 21112 carried <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 and had one mutation in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> (T83I) and one mutation in <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> (S80I), which would explain the high MIC of nalidixic acid (&gt;256 mg/L) and also the MIC of ciprofloxacin (3 mg/L). <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> 46408 carried <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2, but no mutations were found in any of the studied genes (<ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic>). A <ce:italic>Salmonella</ce:italic> isolate carrying <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 showed MICs of 0.5 and 16 mg/L of ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid, respectively [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>]. Moreover, a <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 determinant found in an <ce:italic>A. punctata</ce:italic> isolate [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>] was transferred to <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10, conferring low levels of resistance to fluoroquinolones, with MICs of nalidixic acid from 1 to 4 mg/mL, and MICs of ciprofloxacin from &lt;0.01 to 0.25 mg/mL. As no mutations in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> were found, the high levels of resistance to nalidixic acid (&gt;256 mg/L) and ciprofloxacin (&gt;32 mg/L) in this <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate could have been achieved by the combination of the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>2 determinant and some other mechanism of quinolone resistance, such as changes in permeability (associated with a decrease in porin expression or overexpression of efflux pump systems) for these antimicrobial agents, or the recently reported mechanisms encoded by <ce:italic>aac</ce:italic>(<ce:italic>6</ce:italic>‘)-<ce:italic>Ib-cr</ce:italic> and/or <ce:italic>qepA</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib31 bib32">31,32</ce:cross-refs>]. In the remaining qnr-positive isolates, and as reported previously, the Qnr determinant alone did not confer resistance to fluoroquinolones [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs110" refid="bib33 bib34 bib35">33–35</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para280" view="all">The origin of Qnr has been identified in several genomes (<ce:italic>Shewanella algae</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Vibrio</ce:italic>-like species) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>]. In some cases, <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic>-like genes have been proposed to be inserted in the chromosome of bacterial species that do not naturally possess <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic>-like genes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs120" refid="bib22 bib37 bib38">22,37,38</ce:cross-refs>]. Repeated plasmid purification and conjugation experiments using <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> J53Rif<ce:sup loc="post">R</ce:sup> yielded negative results concerning the presence of <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic>-containing plasmids. Genomic mapping analysis with <ce:italic>I-CeuI</ce:italic> and SI nuclease followed by Southern blot hybridization studies confirmed these results, which point to the chromosomal location of these genes. Although plasmids larger than 750 kb have been described (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs10" xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/genlist.cgi?taxid=1&amp;type=7&amp;name=Plasmids" xlink:type="simple">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/genlist.cgi?taxid=1&amp;type=7&amp;name=Plasmids</ce:inter-ref>), their occurrence is rare, and they have been found only in bacteria with special requirements and from extreme environments. Therefore, the data presented here are taken to demonstrate the chromosomal location of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>6 in <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>C. werkmanii</ce:italic> and of <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> 16 in <ce:italic>C. freundii.</ce:italic>
                  </ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para290" view="all">On the other hand, variants of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, and in some cases <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>, have been found as part of a complex class of type 1 integrons containing a presumed recombinase, Orf513 [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs130" refid="bib39 bib40">39,40</ce:cross-refs>]. However, in the few <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>-bearing plasmids sequenced, <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> was not part of an integron, although one plasmid was flanked by inverted repeats with an insertion sequence-like structure that could have been responsible for its mobilization [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>]. The <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> determinants described in this study were found to be associated with an integron-like structure similar to that reported for a <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic>2 gene in <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib41">41</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para300" view="all">As new variants of Qnr determinants continuously appear in different genomic locations and also outside the <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> family, further studies are necessary to determine the extent of their distribution and to assess their clinical implications as contributors to quinolone resistance.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
