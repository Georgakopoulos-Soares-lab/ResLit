                  Hospital effluents are involved in dissemination of antibiotic-resistant integrons. We describe here a new class 3 integron, In3-5, detected in an Enterobacter cloacae isolate retrieved from a random French hospital effluent sample collected in 2009. In3-5 carries two gene cassettes: the new bla
                     OXA-256 and an aac(6′)-Ib variant, respectively conferring resistance to β-lactams and aminoglycosides. In3-5 is located on an IncQ-like backbone plasmid. Class 3 integrons could thus be involved in the dissemination of antibiotic resistance in both clinical settings and the environment, and could participate in the exchange of antibiotic-resistance genes between these two ecosystems.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara30" view="all">Hospital effluents are involved in dissemination of antibiotic-resistant integrons. We describe here a new class 3 integron, In3-5, detected in an <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> isolate retrieved from a random French hospital effluent sample collected in 2009. In3-5 carries two gene cassettes: the new <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-256</ce:inf> and an <ce:italic>aac(6′)-Ib</ce:italic> variant, respectively conferring resistance to β-lactams and aminoglycosides. In3-5 is located on an IncQ-like backbone plasmid. Class 3 integrons could thus be involved in the dissemination of antibiotic resistance in both clinical settings and the environment, and could participate in the exchange of antibiotic-resistance genes between these two ecosystems.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

            <ce:sections>
               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Several studies have underlined the environmental resistome as a source of resistance genes, and environmental hot spots of integron dissemination, including hospital effluents, have been identified [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref120" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. Integrons are bacterial genetic elements able to capture and express genes contained within mobile gene cassettes (GCs) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref130" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Integrons are composed of an intI gene encoding an integrase that mediates GC exchange, a primary recombination site, <ce:italic>attI</ce:italic>, and a promoter, Pc. Different classes of antibiotic-resistant integrons have been described, based on the amino acid sequence of the integrase; class 1 and class 2 integrons are the most frequently found [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Only four different class 3 integrons have been fully characterized to date (Table S1): In3-1 and In3-2 in clinical <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> [4–6] and In3-3 and In3-4 from two environmental <ce:italic>Delftia</ce:italic> strains [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref160" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">In 2009, we isolated a class 3 integron-positive <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> strain (LIM73) from an effluent sample of a French Hospital. LIM73 was resistant to β-lactams (amoxicillin and ticarcillin), quinolones (nalidixic acid and pefloxacin), aminoglycosides (tobramycin and netilmicin) and tetracyclin. This class 3 integron was detected with a qPCR [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>]. The GC array was amplified with the attI3L (5′GGTATCCGGTGTTTGGT CAG3′) and class3R (5′CGTCAAACGGGTAAGCAGT3′) primers located, respectively, in <ce:italic>attI3</ce:italic> and in the 3′ end of class 3 integrons. This class 3 integron was designated In3-5, according to the nomenclature used by Xu <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. In3-5 contains 2 GCs: (i) a novel <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> variant named bla<ce:inf loc="post">OXA-256</ce:inf> (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs80f" xlink:href="http://www.lahey.org/Studies/" xlink:type="simple">http://www.lahey.org/Studies/</ce:inter-ref>), and (ii) an <ce:italic>aac(6′)-Ib</ce:italic> variant. The OXA256 protein has an Asn to Asp mutation at amino acid position 73 compared with the OXA10 protein. The <ce:italic>aac (6′)-Ib</ce:italic> variant harbours the mutation Leu119Ser, known to be associated with the loss of amikacin resistance and the acquisition of gentamicin resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">In integrons, GCs are usually promoterless and are expressed from the common Pc promoter [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. In In3-5, the sequence of Pc is identical to that described for In3-1, In3-3 and In3-4 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>], except for one mutation located in the Pc spacer and previously described in In3-2 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>). Moreover, in In3-5, upstream of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-256</ce:inf> GC, we found a 161-bp sequence previously described in the class 1 integron In53 and containing two putative promoters that have been shown to allow <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-10</ce:inf> expression [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para40" view="all">Sequencing of the <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> gene revealed very high identity (&gt;99%) with other known <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> genes. However, we found a G to A mutation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>) leading to a modified presumed start codon (ATA instead of ATG). Such a start codon has occasionally been described in prokaryotes and has always been associated with very low translation levels [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]. Alternatively, the start codon of the <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> gene might be located at a different position, in which case a GTG could act as a start codon (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). However, it was recently shown that in class 1 integrons the <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic> promoter is repressed by the transcriptional repressor LexA [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. The region upstream of the <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> gene also contains a LexA-binding site surrounding a potential integrase promoter (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. The putative translation start site GTG would be compatible with the more distal of the two potential <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> promoters proposed by Collis <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>], but in this case, this <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> promoter would be too far from the LexA binding site to be regulated by the SOS response.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para50" view="all">Plasmid analysis showed that LIM73 harbours only one plasmid of 9 kbp, designated pUL3AT (accession no. HE616889). We sequenced the whole plasmid and found it to be very similar to five plasmids related to the IncQ incompatibility group, pQ7 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>], pWES-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>], pGNB2 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>], pBRST7.6 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>] and pAHH04 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>]; pQ7 also contained a class 3 integron with 2 GCs (Table S1). IncQ plasmids are known to be mobilizable. We thus performed a conjugation assay using MFD<ce:italic>pir</ce:italic>::pUL3AT (Table S2) as the donor strain, and obtained conjugation at a frequency of 3 × 10<ce:sup loc="post">–3</ce:sup>. We also transformed <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> DH5α with the LIM73 plasmidic DNA. Transformants and transconjugants were positive by qPCR for <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> and both expressed resistances encoded by the In3-5 GCs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref60a" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The <ce:italic>aac(6')-Ib</ce:italic> variant with mutation Leu119Ser is known to encode gentamicin resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>]. However, we found only a slight increase of the gentamicin MIC, which remained low (1 mg/L). This moderate reduced susceptibility to gentamicin has been described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>]. Moreover, this low level of resistance can be explained by the fact that <ce:italic>aac(6')-Ib</ce:italic> GC is in second position, the level of GC transcription depending on the position within the integron.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para60" view="all">We failed to obtain transconjugants when using LIM73 and a zeocin-resistant <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> recipient strain, confirming that pUL3AT is not self-conjugative. The pUL3AT plasmid has a backbone structure nearly identical to that of other IncQ-type plasmids found in strains from aquatic environments [15–18]. Class 3 integrons seem to be abundant in aquatic environments [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>]; In3-3 and In3-4 integrons have been described on the chromosome of wastewater <ce:italic>Delftia</ce:italic> species and did not carry antibiotic-resistance genes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. We suggest that class 3 integrons located on the chromosome of aquatic bacteria may have been captured through transposition events and inserted within broad-host-range plasmids. These plasmids would then disseminate among clinical strains, and the class 3 integrons would thus be able to acquire GCs encoding antibiotic resistance from class 1 integrons that are highly prevalent in clinical isolates. Indeed, at the boundaries of In3-5, were Tn<ce:italic>21</ce:italic>-like inverted repeat (IR) sequences flanked by a duplication of a 5-bp sequence. This suggested that In3-5 was inserted in the backbone of the pUL3AT plasmid by a transposition event. In3-1 also contains such an IR sequence beyond the last GC [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>]. Moreover, all the class 3 integrons contain, downstream of the GC array, the proximal 44 bp of the left-end of Tn402-type class 1 integrons [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para70" view="all">The In3-1 and In3-2 integrons harbouring antibiotic-resistance GCs were found in strains from patients with urinary tract infections. LIM73 was isolated from hospital effluent, suggesting that it may be of clinical origin. Class 3 integrons could thus be involved in the dissemination of antibiotic resistance in both clinical settings and the environment, and could participate in the exchange of antibiotic-resistance GCs between these two ecosystems.</ce:para>
