                  Background
                  Antimicrobial resistance in cystic fibrosis (CF) Pseudomonas aeruginosa airway infection is complex and often attributed to chromosomal mutations. How these mutations emerge in specific strains or whether particular gene mutations are clinically informative is unclear. This study focused on oprD, which encodes an outer membrane porin associated with carbapenem resistance when it is downregulated or inactivated.
               
                  Aim
                  Determine how mutations in oprD emerge in two prevalent Australian shared CF strains of P. aeruginosa and their clinical relevance.
               
                  Methods
                  The two most common shared CF strains in Queensland were investigated using whole genome sequencing and their oprD sequences and antimicrobial resistance phenotypes were established. P. aeruginosa mutants with the most common oprD variants were constructed and characterised. Clinical variables were compared between people with or without evidence of infection with strains harbouring these variants.
               
                  Results
                  Frequently found nonsense mutations arising from a 1-base pair substitution in oprD evolved independently in three sub-lineages, and are likely major contributors to the reduced carbapenem susceptibility observed in the clinical isolates. Lower baseline FEV1 %predicted was identified as a risk factor for infection with a sub-lineage (odds ratio=0.97; 95% confidence interval 0.96-0.99; p&lt;0.001). However, acquiring these sub-lineage strains did not confer an accelerated decline in FEV1 nor increase the risk of death/lung transplantation.
               
                  Conclusions
                  Sub-lineages harbouring specific mutations in oprD have emerged and persisted in the shared strain populations. Infection with the sub-lineages was more likely in people with lower lung function, but this was not predictive of a worse clinical trajectory.

            <ce:abstract class="author" id="abs0002" view="all"><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0003" role="background" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0003">Background</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara008" view="all">Antimicrobial resistance in cystic fibrosis (CF) <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> airway infection is complex and often attributed to chromosomal mutations. How these mutations emerge in specific strains or whether particular gene mutations are clinically informative is unclear. This study focused on <ce:italic>oprD,</ce:italic> which encodes an outer membrane porin associated with carbapenem resistance when it is downregulated or inactivated.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0004" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0004">Aim</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara009" view="all">Determine how mutations in <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> emerge in two prevalent Australian shared CF strains of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> and their clinical relevance.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0005" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0005">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara010" view="all">The two most common shared CF strains in Queensland were investigated using whole genome sequencing and their <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> sequences and antimicrobial resistance phenotypes were established. <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> mutants with the most common <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants were constructed and characterised. Clinical variables were compared between people with or without evidence of infection with strains harbouring these variants.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0006" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0006">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara011" view="all">Frequently found nonsense mutations arising from a 1-base pair substitution in <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> evolved independently in three sub-lineages, and are likely major contributors to the reduced carbapenem susceptibility observed in the clinical isolates. Lower baseline FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted was identified as a risk factor for infection with a sub-lineage (odds ratio=0.97; 95% confidence interval 0.96-0.99; <ce:italic>p</ce:italic>&lt;0.001). However, acquiring these sub-lineage strains did not confer an accelerated decline in FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> nor increase the risk of death/lung transplantation.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0007" role="conclusion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0007">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara012" view="all">Sub-lineages harbouring specific mutations in <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> have emerged and persisted in the shared strain populations. Infection with the sub-lineages was more likely in people with lower lung function, but this was not predictive of a worse clinical trajectory.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

                  <ce:label>1</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0008">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para0013" view="all">Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disease resulting in progressive lung damage. <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> lung infection in people with CF (PWCF) is common and increases with age. Established infection is associated with increased antimicrobial resistance (AMR) accompanied by intensified treatment burden, impaired quality of life and an accelerated lung function decline <ce:cross-ref id="crf0030" refid="bib0001">[1]</ce:cross-ref>. Some PWCF are infected with commonly shared strains. Such strains have been identified in PWCF attending Australian CF centres with AUST-02 and AUST-06 being the most prevalent in the state of Queensland <ce:cross-ref id="crf0031" refid="bib0002">[2]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0014" view="all">Adaptation of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> during chronic infection results in intra-strain population diversity; but studies have demonstrated that certain loci appear more prone to mutation than others [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0032" refid="bib0003">3</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0033" refid="bib0004">4</ce:cross-ref>]. Of importance are mutations in loci that facilitate AMR, which might contribute to the persistence of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> in the lungs with multi-resistant isolates associated with a more rapid disease progression <ce:cross-ref id="crf0034" refid="bib0005">[5]</ce:cross-ref>. Currently, there are limited descriptions of whether mutations in AMR-related loci are fixed within independent shared strain genotypes or whether novel variants generally arise within individual patients, suggesting parallel evolution. Furthermore, it is unclear whether specific AMR-gene mutations could be used to inform clinical care by identifying differences in adaptive changes that may help to predict PWCF who are at the greatest risk of deterioration <ce:cross-refs id="crf0035" refid="bib0006 bib0007 bib0008">[6–8]</ce:cross-refs>. One such study reported that <ce:italic>mexY</ce:italic> mRNA expression, associated with aminoglycoside resistance, was inversely correlated with lung function <ce:cross-ref id="crf0037" refid="bib0009">[9]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0015" view="all">The carbapenems are used to treat pulmonary exacerbations and are often reserved for PWCF with advanced disease or where there is a suboptimal clinical response to other ß-lactam antibiotics. Although carbapenem resistance of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates from PWCF is well recognised <ce:cross-ref id="crf0038" refid="bib0010">[10]</ce:cross-ref>, the underlying mechanisms and the potential clinical implications of their identification are less clear. Specific <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> alleles, encoding an outer membrane porin, OprD, have been detected frequently across strains in multiple studies, including in our previous work of the AUST-02 M3L7 strain sub-type which harboured a nonsense mutation [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0039" refid="bib0004">4</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0040" refid="bib0011">11</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0041" refid="bib0012">12</ce:cross-ref>]. This indicates the potential importance of <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> mutations in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> adaptation to the CF lungs. The OprD porin facilitates diffusion of substrates, including carbapenems and basic amino acids, across cell membranes <ce:cross-ref id="crf0042" refid="bib0013">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. The decreased expression or mutational inactivation of <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> is established as an important AMR mechanism [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0043" refid="bib0014">14</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0044" refid="bib0015">15</ce:cross-ref>], while strains lacking OprD can also have enhanced virulence <ce:cross-ref id="crf0045" refid="bib0016">[16]</ce:cross-ref>, indicating that mutations at this locus could potentially have consequences for <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> pathogenicity.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0016" view="all">We performed a detailed <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> analysis in a collection of CF shared strains. The study aim was to determine how mutations in <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> emerge in these strain populations and if the mutations observed are of potential clinical relevance.</ce:para>

                     <ce:label>2.5</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0014">Site-directed mutagenesis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0021" view="all">Site-directed mutagenesis of chromosomally encoded <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> was used to determine the contribution that each mutation of interest had on carbapenem MICs using the PAO1 wild-type strain (herein referred to as PAO1).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0022" view="all">Strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani broth at 37°C with shaking (200rpm) and supplemented with antibiotics when appropriate. Strains and plasmids are described in Table S2. An Applied Biosystems Veriti Thermal cycler with primers listed in Table S3 was used for PCRs.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0023" view="all">PAO1 isogenic mutants, were constructed essentially as described previously; <ce:cross-ref id="crf0050" refid="bib0022">[22]</ce:cross-ref> and specific details and minor variations are outlined in the Supplemental Material File. Plasmid constructs with the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of interest (<ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> c.526G&gt;T, p.E176X; c.748C&gt;T, p.Q250X; c.956C&gt;A, p.S319X) were constructed using pMRS101 <ce:cross-ref id="crf0051" refid="bib0023">[23]</ce:cross-ref>. The pMRS101 constructs were mobilised from <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> CC118 λ pir into PAO1 by triparental mating with <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> DH5α <ce:cross-ref id="crf0052" refid="bib0024">[24]</ce:cross-ref>. A single homologous recombination event was selected using streptomycin and a second homologous recombination event was counter selected with 5% sucrose <ce:cross-ref id="crf0053" refid="bib0022">[22]</ce:cross-ref>. The presence of the SNPs of putative PAO1 mutants was confirmed by PCR and DNA sequencing. The isogenic mutants were named: PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">E176X</ce:sup>, PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">Q250X</ce:sup>, and PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">S319X</ce:sup>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0024" view="all">Complementation of the isogenic mutants was performed using a pMRS101 construct with the wild-type <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> gene encoded and the same methodologies employed to generate the original mutations. Complemented mutants were named: PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">E176C</ce:sup>, PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">Q250C</ce:sup> and PAO1-OprD<ce:sup loc="post">S319C</ce:sup>.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="sec0011" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0018">CF centre, clinical characteristics and infection control policies</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0028" view="all">Patients were recruited between 2007 and 2014 and followed until their time of death, lung transplantation or the study census date of August 31<ce:sup loc="post">st</ce:sup>, 2017, whichever occurred sooner. Recruits attended either the Royal Children's Hospital (RCH; now the Queensland Children's Hospital) (paediatric CF centre) or The Prince Charles Hospital (TPCH) (adult CF centre). When paediatric patients completed their schooling (~17 years of age) their care was transferred to the adult centre.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0029" view="all">Age, gender, body mass index (BMI), spirometry, co-morbidities, long-term treatments and <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> infection status [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0054" refid="bib0025">25</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0055" refid="bib0026">26</ce:cross-ref>], were obtained. Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) function <ce:cross-ref id="crf0056" refid="bib0027">[27]</ce:cross-ref>, data on hospitalisations in the previous calendar year and, where applicable, the date of death/lung transplantation were recorded.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0031" view="all">Patients at RCH were segregated from one another and managed in single room inpatient facilities during this period. This was not always practical at TPCH due to limited availability of single room accommodation and shared care in multi-patient inpatient rooms occurred when single room accommodation was unavailable. In this era, patients with <ce:italic>Burkholderia cepacia</ce:italic> complex and methicillin-resistant <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> infection were managed entirely in single room accommodation in separate inpatients wards. However, segregation (with single room care for all patients) at TPCH was implemented in 2015 with the commissioning of a purpose-built adult CF inpatient facility. Outpatient care at RCH used a program of patients staying in a single room and having the multidisciplinary team rotate through the patient room as needed, but clinics were not cohorted by <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> status. Meanwhile, at the adult centre a segregation policy for outpatient clinic attendance was implemented in the 1990s for patients with <ce:italic>Burkholderia cepacia</ce:italic> complex and methicillin-resistant <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus.</ce:italic> Segregation of patients based on <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> status was first introduced in 2014.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="sec0012" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0019">Statistical analyses</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0032" view="all">MICs were compared using a Mann-Whitney <ce:italic>U</ce:italic> test/Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni adjustment applied. Based on the variant-specific PCR outcome, participants (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=148) were categorised as either cases (tested positive for ≥1 isolate [sub-lineage] with a target <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> SNP) or control subjects (tested negative for isolates with a target <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> SNP). Potential predictors of being a case were identified by univariate analysis as having <ce:italic>p</ce:italic>≤0.15 and examined in a multivariable logistic regression model using backwards stepwise selection. Linear mixed-effects models incorporating random intercept and slope to account for multiple observations/participant were used to assess change over time for forced expiratory volume in one-second (FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>) %predicted (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=348 observations; 104 participants), forced vital capacity (FVC) %predicted (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=348 observations; 104 participants) and BMI (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=352 observations; 105 participants). Each of the models included main effects for group (case/control) and time from baseline in years. The interaction between group and time was examined but not included in the final models. Restricted maximum likelihood estimates are reported. Cox regression was used to assess the association between being a case with time to death/lung transplantation. For patients who had not had a transplant and were still alive, time was censored on the August 31st, 2017. The model included main effects for age, type of shared strain infection and FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted. The interaction between group and FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted was examined but not included in the final models. Analyses were generated using IBM SPSS v22/STATA v15.1. See Supplemental Material File for further details.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>

               <ce:section id="sec0013" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:label>4</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0020">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec0014" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0021">
                        <ce:italic>In silico</ce:italic> analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0033" view="all">The phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationship of <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants is shown in <ce:cross-ref id="crf0057" refid="fig0001">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig0001"/> (panel A and panel B). The AUST-02 and AUST-06 wild-type variant (1326-base pairs [bp]) is denoted as the ones closest to the base of each clade. These are highly conserved with two nucleotide differences between the lineages; their protein sequences are identical (441 amino acids). Overall, 14/114 sequenced isolates had the wild-type variant.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0034" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> mutations caused by a 1-bp substitution or a 1-bp insertion or deletion (indel) occurred multiple times in the strain collection. Notably, three independent nonsense mutations caused by a 1-bp substitution were identified in 79/114 isolates from 32/50 participants; two mutations within AUST-02 (<ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> c.526G&gt;T or c.748C&gt;T) and one within AUST-06 (<ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> c.956C&gt;A). These SNPs were predicted to result in a disrupted protein sequence<ce:italic>,</ce:italic> with <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> c.526G&gt;T (p.E176X), c.748C&gt;T (p.Q250X), and c.956C&gt;A (p.S319X) leading to truncation at amino acids 175, 249 and 318, respectively. All isolates carrying the <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> c.526G&gt;T (p.E176X) variant belonged to the AUST-02 M3L7 strain sub-type [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0058" refid="bib0008">8</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0059" refid="bib0011">11</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0035" view="all">Seven independent 1-bp indels were identified in a smaller number of AUST-06 isolates (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=20/114) from 10/50 participants. Six indels were predicted to result in a truncated protein, and one extended the protein length by 31 amino acids. The <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variant in isolate AUS426 was disrupted by an insertion sequence (IS) of the IS3 family.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0036" view="all">Some isolates had acquired additional missense (AUS925/AUS899) or synonymous (AUS901) mutations.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0037" view="all">Most variants emerged from monophyletic clades (sub-lineages that share a recent common ancestor), including isolates with the nonsense mutations caused by a 1-bp substitution (<ce:cross-ref id="crf0060" refid="fig0001">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>B). Participants with multiple isolates sequenced were mostly infected with a strain that had an identical <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variant (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=14/18). In isolates from Patient-11 and Patient-37, multiple variants had arisen independently from the same lineage.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0015" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0022">AMR of clinical isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0038" view="all">There were no statistical differences in MICs between the AUST-02 and AUST-06 genotypes (Fig. S1A).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0040" view="all">Given the prevalence of isolates (sub-lineages) with an <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> nonsense mutation arising from a 1-bp substitution (target SNPs; <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=79), their AMR patterns were compared to isolates with less common indel mutations (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=21; frameshift/disruption by IS) or the wild-type variant (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=14). Differences were apparent for all antimicrobials, except colistin. Isolates with a target SNP (100%) or an indel (95%) were highly resistant to imipenem; whereas only one wild-type variant isolate was resistant to this agent (<ce:cross-ref id="crf0061" refid="tbl0001">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl0001"/> and Fig. S1B). Relative to isolates with other variants, those with target SNPs demonstrated greater resistance/MICs to meropenem, ceftazidime and ticarcillin-clavulanic acid (<ce:cross-ref id="crf0062" refid="tbl0001">Table 1</ce:cross-ref> and Fig. S1B).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0041" view="all">Isolates with different target SNPs showed significant MIC variations across all antimicrobials tested, excluding the carbapenems (Fig. S1C).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0016" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0023">Site-directed mutagenesis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0042" view="all">Introduction of the commonly observed 1-bp substitutions/SNPs (Table S6) into <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> of PAO1 led to imipenem resistance for all isogenic mutants (24-32mg/L) and an increased meropenem MIC by ~2-3 doubling dilutions (1.5-3mg/L). Conversely, all complemented mutants had MICs at wild-type levels (imipenem, 1mg/L; meropenem, 0.19-0.38mg/L). There were no differences in the susceptibility of each isogenic mutant to the other antimicrobials tested compared to PAO1.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0043" view="all">The proteomic analysis found no evidence of OprD protein in the isogenic mutants. Conversely, peptides spanning both N-/C-terminal regions of OprD were detected in PAO1 and the complemented mutants. The results were confirmed using tryptic peptides that were unique to OprD when compared to the background proteome.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0044" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>In vitro</ce:italic> growth was not affected by the introduction of the target SNPs into the PAO1 chromosome (Fig. S2).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0017" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0024">Clinical course</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0045" view="all">Overall, 83/149 participants (cases) were infected with ≥1 isolate (sub-lineage) carrying a target <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> SNP; whereas the remaining participants (control subjects) were presumed to have an infection with isolates with other variants (<ce:cross-ref id="crf0063" refid="fig0002">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig0002"/>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0046" view="all">Cases (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=82; one excluded because clinical data were lacking) and controls (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=66) were similar with respect to baseline age, sex, CFTR function, chronic/type of shared strain infection and co-morbidities. <ce:cross-ref id="crf0064" refid="tbl0002">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl0002"/> shows that the odds of being a case were reduced with higher lung function and increased with a greater number of hospital days. Although not statistically significant, the odds of being a case were reduced with increasing BMI. Amongst the long-term prescribed treatments, inhaled colistin significantly increased the odds of being a case compared to non-inhalation, as did tobramycin although this was not statistically significant. After controlling for these factors in a multivariable logistic regression, only lung function remained statistically significant. For every unit increase in FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted, the odds of being a case decreased by 3%.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0047" view="all">The rate of change in lung function (cases, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=56; controls, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=48) and BMI (cases, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=57; controls, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=48) was determined for participants who had &gt;1 measurement available. At baseline, cases had on average a FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted value 13.3 units lower than controls (-13.3 [95% confidence interval (CI), -22.1 to - 4.6]) and FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> dropped by 1.2 units/year for both groups (-1.2 [95%CI, -1.8 to -0.6]). Likewise at baseline, cases had on average a FVC %predicted value 12.3 units lower than controls (-12.3 [95%CI, -19.9 to - 4.7]) and FVC remained relatively unchanged as each year passed for both groups (-0.4 [95%CI,-1.0 to 0.2]). Finally at baseline, cases had on average a BMI 1.7 units lower than controls (-1.7 [95%CI, -3.1 to - 0.4]) and the BMI increased by 0.2 units/year (95%CI, 0.1 to 0.3) for both groups.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0048" view="all">In all, 45/148 participants were deceased (cases, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=11; controls, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=4) or had received a lung transplant (cases, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=20; controls, <ce:italic>n</ce:italic>=10) by the censor date (Table S7). Being a case had a higher risk of death/transplant (hazard ratio [HR], 2.16; 95%CI, 1.14-4.10; <ce:italic>p</ce:italic>=0.018) compared to being a control; however, when baseline FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted was accounted for, the risk was no longer statistically significant (HR, 1.30; 95%CI, 0.67-2.55; <ce:italic>p</ce:italic>=0.441).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec0018" role="discussion" view="all">
                  <ce:label>5</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0025">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para0049" view="all">The relationship between <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> AMR and the associated underlying resistance mechanisms are complex in CF lung infections, making it difficult to interpret data clinically. Here we provide an example of a study investigating relationships between mutations in a single AMR-related locus and microbial phenotype and patient outcomes. Our targeted <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> analysis in two major Australian shared strains, firstly, led to the identification of two more independently acquired nonsense mutations in addition to the one we previously described in the AUST-02 M3L7 strain sub-type <ce:cross-ref id="crf0065" refid="bib0011">[11]</ce:cross-ref>. This is consistent with the fixation of advantageous mutations in these strain populations. Next, we confirmed that each of the commonly occurring <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> mutations was an important contributor to carbapenem resistance without affecting bacterial growth rate. We also determined that PWCF with lower baseline lung function were more likely to have a sub-lineage with the specific <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants. This potentially indicated that identification of these gene variants could be a marker of worse clinical status. However, further analysis of patient clinical trajectories showed that infection with the sub-lineages was not associated with a subsequent accelerated rate of decline in FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> nor was there a higher risk of mortality/lung transplantation.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0050" view="all">AUST-02 and AUST-06 frequently carried <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants<ce:italic>,</ce:italic> supporting previous studies that identified this gene as a common target for mutation during persistent infection [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0066" refid="bib0004">4</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0067" refid="bib0012">12</ce:cross-ref>]. The variants identified were unique to each strain, providing no evidence for homologous recombination or homoplasy. There were no <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants that were found exclusively in all AUST-02 or AUST-06 isolates. Rather than identifying many novel variants that were limited to individual patient isolates, we repeatedly identified three variants in isolates from multiple PWCF that were characteristic of monophyletic strain sub-lineages. We hypothesise that these variants became fixed in the sub-lineages following their divergence from all other strains and were then disseminated between patients. Introduction of the various SNPs of interest into <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> had no impact on PAO1 growth in Luria-Bertani broth suggesting that these mutations may be acquired without an excess fitness cost; however, work using a biochemical/biophysical environment more relevant to <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> airway conditions is required to support this finding. Nevertheless, our observations may further substantiate the likelihood of clinical strains carrying these variants being capable of spreading and establishing new infections <ce:cross-ref id="crf0068" refid="bib0028">[28]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0051" view="all">Reduced expression/inactivation of OprD constitutes a major carbapenem resistance mechanism <ce:cross-ref id="crf0069" refid="bib0015">[15]</ce:cross-ref>, and our analysis revealed that 99% of imipenem-resistant isolates harboured <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> with either a nonsense/indel mutation or disruption by an IS. To confirm the effect of the three most common <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> mutations in our strain collection, we used site-directed mutagenesis in PAO1. Our results showed imipenem resistance and an apparent loss of the porin for all three variants. Although 97% of clinical isolates with these mutations also had a meropenem MIC &gt;32mg/L, we found that in our isogenic mutants the meropenem MIC only increased by several fold. Therefore, meropenem resistance is a polygenic trait <ce:cross-ref id="crf0070" refid="bib0014">[14]</ce:cross-ref>, and more diverse collections of clinical isolates are needed to identify the genes that are associated with this phenotype.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0052" view="all">Other mechanisms contributing to carbapenem resistance include the production of antibiotic-inactivating carbapenemases; but studies in CF <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates suggest that this mechanism is uncommon [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0071" refid="bib0010">10</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0072" refid="bib0015">15</ce:cross-ref>]. Upregulation of multi-drug efflux pumps and mutations in a multitude of regulatory genes may also contribute to increased MICs, which highlights the complexity of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> AMR <ce:cross-ref id="crf0073" refid="bib0014">[14]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0053" view="all">AUST-02 and AUST-06 have been associated with increased treatment requirements compared to unique strains <ce:cross-ref id="crf0074" refid="bib0002">[2]</ce:cross-ref>. When we investigated characteristics of PWCF who had acquired the sub-lineages carrying the target <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants, lower FEV<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> %predicted was potentially important. Prior studies identified that adaptive mutations in specific loci of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> were associated with a steeper decline in lung function and accelerated disease progression in CF <ce:cross-refs id="crf0075" refid="bib0006 bib0007 bib0008">[6–8]</ce:cross-refs>. Studies of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> blood stream infections have also reported that loss of OprD is associated with mortality <ce:cross-ref id="crf0077" refid="bib0029">[29]</ce:cross-ref>. Conversely, we did not find a statistically significant relationship between infection with the sub-lineages and an adverse clinical trajectory. A possible explanation is that PWCF with lower lung function may be at greater risk of acquiring the shared strain sub-lineages following increased exposure to healthcare environments, rather than their acquisition leading to an accelerated decline in lung function and poorer survival.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0054" view="all">Study limitations include that the proportion of PWCF infected with strains carrying the target variants may have been underestimated due to the small number of isolates that were available per person. It is possible that the <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> SNPs identified could have a therapeutic value in predicting responses to pulmonary exacerbation treatment regimens containing carbapenems, although determining this was outside the scope of the study. Currently <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> AMR and treatment response are poorly correlated in CF <ce:cross-ref id="crf0078" refid="bib0030">[30]</ce:cross-ref>. Finally, we were unable to exchange the <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variant carrying a nonsense mutation in the clinical isolates with that of the wild-type variant, as they were insensitive to SacB-mediated sucrose toxicity used for counter-selection.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0055" view="all">In conclusion, the study illustrates the importance of investigating genes under strong selection pressure to advance our understanding of how <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> adapts to the CF lung and AMR emerges in strain populations. The findings suggest that shared strain sub-lineages with specific <ce:italic>oprD</ce:italic> variants pre-existed in the population before spreading between patients. We show that people infected with the sub-lineages had a poorer baseline clinical status, but acquiring these strains did not result in a worse prognosis.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
