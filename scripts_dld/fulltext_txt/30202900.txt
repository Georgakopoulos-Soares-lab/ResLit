## ABSTRACT
Background: Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole is a synthetic antibiotic combination recommended for the treatment of complicated non-typhoidal Salmonella infections in humans. Resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole is mediated by the acquisition of mobile genes, requiring both a dfr gene (trimethoprim resistance) and a sul gene (sulfamethoxazole resistance) for a clinical resistance phenotype (MIC ≥4/76 mg/L). In 2017, the CDC investigated a multistate outbreak caused by a Salmonella enterica serotype Heidelberg strain with trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance, in which sul genes but no known dfr genes were detected. Objectives: To characterize and describe the molecular mechanism of trimethoprim resistance in a Salmonella Heidelberg outbreak isolate. Methods: Illumina sequencing data for one outbreak isolate revealed a 588 bp ORF encoding a putative dfr gene. This gene was cloned into Escherichia coli and resistance to trimethoprim was measured by broth dilution and Etest. Phylogenetic analysis of previously reported dfrA genes was performed using MEGA. Long-read sequencing was conducted to determine the context of the novel dfr gene. Results and conclusions: The novel dfr gene, named dfrA34 , conferred trimethoprim resistance (MIC ≥32 mg/L) when cloned into E. coli . Based on predicted amino acid sequences, dfrA34 shares less than 50% identity withother known dfrA genes. The dfrA34 gene is located in a class 1 integron in a multiresistance region of an IncC plasmid, adjacent to a sul gene, thus conferring clinical trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance. Additionally, dfrA34 is associated with IS CR1 , enabling easy transmission between other plasmids and bacterial strains.

## INTRODUCTION
Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) causes an estimated 1.2 million infections annually in the USA 1 and, while most are self-limiting, invasive or severe infections may require antibiotic treatment. Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole is a synthetic antibiotic combination recommended for the treatment of NTS infections in humans if needed. 2 , 3 Resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole in NTS is conferred by mobile resistance genes and acquisition of both a dfr (trimethoprim resistance) and a sul (sulfamethoxazole resistance) gene is required. 3 Mobile dfr genes, encoding dihydrofolate reductases (DHFRs), are typically carried as cassettes in integrons or associated with IS CR1 4 , 5 and over 30 different variants have been identified. 5 The sul genes (encoding dihydropteroate synthases) are usually plasmid-borne or, in the case of sul1 , carried as part of the CS of the widely disseminated class 1 integrons. 3 , 5 , 6 Despite the ubiquity of these genes, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance has remained relatively low (<4%) in NTS over the last 20 years. 7 In 2017, the CDC investigated a multistate Salmonella enterica serotype Heidelberg outbreak displaying a trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance phenotype. 8 Multiple sulfamethoxazole resistance genes were detected in these isolates, but no known trimethoprim resistance genes were found. One such isolate was further characterized to identify the cause of the phenotype–genotype discrepancy. Here, we report a novel trimethoprim resistance gene, dfrA34 , identified during this investigation.

## METHODS
S. enterica Heidelberg isolate 2016K-0796 (BioSample SAMN05825344) was characterized as part of a multistate outbreak linked to contact with dairy calves. 8 Briefly, isolates from ill patients were characterized by public health laboratories in the USA and identified as part of the multistate outbreak through PulseNet, CDC’s molecular subtyping network of state and local public health departments. Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) was performed by broth microdilution (Sensititre ® , USA) at CDC’s National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System (NARMS) laboratory, using CLSI interpretive standards, when available. 9 , 10 Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (QIAGEN Inc., Germany), prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (2×250 bp) (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (v2 chemistry). De novo assemblies generated with CLC Genomics Workbench (QIAGEN, Inc.) were analysed for resistance gene content using ResFinder 3.0 (90% identity, 60% cut-off). 11 Geneious (version 11.0.3) 12 and NCBI BLAST were used to identify a 588 bp ORF encoding a putative DHFR, based on conserved domains. To confirm this gene conferred trimethoprim resistance, a 627 bp region containing only the ORF was amplified with primers TmpNew-F (5′-GGAATTACGGAGACGGTTA-3′) and TmpNew-R (5′-TTCCTATACCCTGGG CGT-3′) and cloned into chemically competent Escherichia coli using the TOPO ™ TA Cloning Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Transformants were selected on blood agar containing ampicillin (32 mg/L) and cultured overnight in LB broth supplemented with doubling trimethoprim concentrations (0.05–32 mg/L). 2016K–0796 and E. coli carrying the empty cloning vector were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Growth or no growth was determined by observed turbidity. Trimethoprim resistance of transformants and controls was also confirmed by Etest ® (bioMérieux, France; concentration range 0.002–32 mg/L), according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Phylogenetic analyses using MEGA (version 7.0.18) 13 were conducted to determine the relatedness of this novel gene to dfrA genes represented in the ResFinder database (accessed 10 April 2018). 11 Amino acid sequences of known dfrA genes were aligned using ClustalW and used to generate a maximum-likelihood tree (JTT model, 1000 bootstraps). 2016K-0796 was also sequenced by Pacific Biosciences SMRT technology, described previously, 14 to identify the genetic location and surrounding context of the putative trimethoprim resistance gene. Annotation was performed using Prokka (version 1.12) 15 and MARA 16 before submission to NCBI (accession number MH760469 ).

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Salmonella Heidelberg isolate 2016K–0796 was resistant to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (MIC ≥4/76 mg/L) by AST, but ResFinder identified only the sulfamethoxazole resistance genes sul1 and sul2 , and no known dfr gene. A 588 bp putative dfr gene was cloned and transformed into E. coli . Resulting transformants were resistant to trimethoprim (MIC ≥2 mg/L), compared to control strains carrying the empty cloning vector (MIC=0.5mg/L), thus confirming this gene as a novel trimethoprim resistance gene, named dfrA34 . Based on predicted amino acid sequences, dfrA34 shares <50% identity with other reported dfrA variants. dfrA34 does not fall within well-established dfrA1 and dfrA12 subgroups in which genes are invariably cassette-borne, instead clustering more closely with IS CR1 -associated genes ( Figure 1 ). SMRT sequencing revealed that dfrA34 is indeed associated with IS CR1 , located in a class 1 integron/transposon (In/Tn) ( Figure 1 ), within antibiotic resistance island A of an IncC (A/C type) plasmid, named p2016K-0796 ( Figure 2 ). 17 Based on the surrounding genetic context, dfrA34 was likely mobilized into this In/Tn via IS CR1 transposition and subsequent homologous recombination, 18 although no clues as to its original genetic location were identified in the adjoining sequence. The association of dfrA34 with IS CR1 essentially guarantees trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance, as this element is almost always located adjacent to the class 1 integron 3′-CS containing sul1 . 18 , 19 While the resistance gene content is ‘fixed’ in this integron due to an IS 26 -mediated inversion that interrupted the integrase gene intI1 ( Figure 2 ), IS CR1 continues to provide a platform for accumulation of resistance genes downstream of the integron by homologous recombination. Nucleotide BLAST returned only one partial match (47% coverage) for dfrA34 in an E. coli isolate from a dairy calf, annotated as a putative trimethoprim resistance gene, but it was not reported as a new dfrA variant and the genetic context is not available for comparison. 20 Translated nucleotide BLAST searches detected dfrA34 in assemblies from six Salmonella isolates and one E. coli isolate. These include two Salmonella Heidelberg isolates (SAMN02911957, human clinical, 2011; SAMN03360085, source unknown) that likely carry the same IncC plasmid as in 2016K-0796 (contig mapping using p2016K-0796 as a reference), with dfrA34 in the same IS CR1 -associated context, although without the IS 26 -mediated inversion. Four Salmonella Braenderup isolates from 2008 (SAMN08514226, SAMN08514244, SAMN08514271 and SAMN08514273) and a single E. coli (SAMN05736413) carry dfrA34 in related but unique In/Tn contexts, thus likely acquired the gene in separate homologous recombination events and not through clonal spread of a p2016K-0796-like plasmid. Notably, an identical 5533 bp region containing dfrA34 , IS CR1 and the 3′-CS ( sul1 ) was found in all contexts ( Figure 2 ), indicating that this gene is invariably found alongside sul1 , thus conferring clinical trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance. While trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance rates in NTS are low in the USA, 7 the potential for rates to increase is demonstrated by the relatively high rate of sulfamethoxazole resistance (11.8% in 2015, trending upwards), 7 coupled with the tendency for dfrA genes such as dfrA34 to be mobilized into the same genetic locations as sulfamethoxazole resistance genes. Additionally, these genes are linked to highly mobile genetic elements, primed for easy transmission between different plasmids and bacterial strains.
