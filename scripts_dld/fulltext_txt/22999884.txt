                  Bongkrekic acid (BA), an infamous respiratory toxin of the pathogenic bacterium Burkholderia gladioli, causes lethal intoxications when tempe bongkrek is produced with contaminated Rhizopus oligosporus cultures. Genome sequencing of B. gladioli pathovar cocovenenans unveiled the genetic basis for BA biosynthesis, and pointed to a homologous bon gene cluster in a B. gladioli strain from an infected rice plant. For functional genetics in B. gladioli λ Red recombination was established. Dissection of the modular type I polyketide synthase (a trans-AT PKS) provided insights into complex polyketide assembly. Isoprenoid-like β-branching events and a six-electron oxidation of a methyl group to a carboxylic acid give rise to the unique branched tricarboxylic fatty acid. The role of the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, BonL, was proven by structural elucidation of deoxybongkrekic acid from a mutant.

            <ce:abstract class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">Bongkrekic acid (BA), an infamous respiratory toxin of the pathogenic bacterium <ce:italic>Burkholderia gladioli</ce:italic>, causes lethal intoxications when tempe bongkrek is produced with contaminated <ce:italic>Rhizopus oligosporus</ce:italic> cultures. Genome sequencing of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pathovar <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic> unveiled the genetic basis for BA biosynthesis, and pointed to a homologous <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene cluster in a <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> strain from an infected rice plant. For functional genetics in <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> λ Red recombination was established. Dissection of the modular type I polyketide synthase (a <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT PKS) provided insights into complex polyketide assembly. Isoprenoid-like β-branching events and a six-electron oxidation of a methyl group to a carboxylic acid give rise to the unique branched tricarboxylic fatty acid. The role of the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, BonL, was proven by structural elucidation of deoxybongkrekic acid from a mutant.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="sec1" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">It is certainly a rare scenario when a government decides to prohibit a national specialty by law. However, in 1988 the Indonesian government responded to thousands of reported food intoxications, many of which were lethal (≈10%), and attempted to officially ban tempe bongkrek from the market. Yet, with little success—as tempe bongkrek has remained a popular dish (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib1">Adams and Moss, 2008</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib78">Wood, 1998</ce:cross-ref>). This indigenous Southeast-Asian fermented food is based on de-oiled coconut flakes that are incubated and modified with the mold fungus <ce:italic>Rhizopus oligosporus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib71">Shurtleff and Aoyagi, 1979</ce:cross-ref>). The resulting press cake proved to be occasionally poisonous, leading to hyperglycemia followed by pronounced hypoglycemia and death in severe instances (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib50">Nugteren and Berends, 1957</ce:cross-ref>). Interestingly, in cases of intoxications, all investigated fungal cultures were contaminated with bacteria. Eventually, <ce:italic>Burkholderia gladioli</ce:italic> pathovar <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic> (formerly <ce:italic>Pseudomonas cocovenenans</ce:italic>) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib10">Coenye et al., 1999</ce:cross-ref>) was identified as the producer of a poison, bongkrekic acid (BA) (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Figure 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>), which accounts for the lethal incidents. Structurally BA represents a highly unsaturated and unusually branched tricarboxylic fatty acid (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib13">de Bruijn et al., 1973</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib80">Zylber et al., 1973</ce:cross-ref>). Toxicological analyses have revealed that BA represents one of the most potent respiratory poisons known. BA efficiently blocks the mitochondrial adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib35">Klingenberg, 2008</ce:cross-ref>), thus inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation, (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib23">Henderson and Lardy, 1970</ce:cross-ref>) with an acute toxicity of 1.41 mg kg<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup> (LD<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> by intravenous injection)(<ce:cross-ref refid="bib43">Lijmbach et al., 1970a</ce:cross-ref>). From a pharmacological point of view, however, it is interesting to note that selective blockage of the ANT could prevent unspecific pore formation in mitochondrial membranes, which may result from oxidative stress and subsequent apoptotic events. Thus, the use of the ANT as a therapeutic target with bongkrekic acid as a potent inhibitor is the matter of ongoing investigations (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib2">Belzacq and Brenner, 2003</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib21">Halestrap and Brenner, 2003</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Javadov et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">In light of this, knowledge of the molecular basis of BA biosynthesis would have a 2-fold benefit. First, it could aid in understanding the regulation of toxin production and detecting potential health threats through bacterial contaminations, and second, BA biosynthesis genes could be employed for engineering BA analogs that could be developed into selective ANT inhibitors. Whereas the chemical structure of BA has been long known, only recently we succeeded in gaining insights into the biosynthetic pathway. Stable isotope labeling studies using synthetic [1,2-<ce:sup loc="pre">13</ce:sup>C<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>]acetyl SNAC and simultaneous inhibition of β-oxidation showed that the BA backbone is composed of acetate units (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Figure 1</ce:cross-ref>B), and the chain branches suggested the involvement of a polyketide synthase (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib60">Rohm et al., 2010a</ce:cross-ref>). Thus far, however, the genes and mechanisms involved in bacterial BA synthesis have remained elusive.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Here, we report the genetic basis of bongkrekic acid biosynthesis in the pathogenic bacterium <ce:italic>B. gladioli.</ce:italic> For functional studies we adapted a targeted gene deletion method for <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. Besides regulatory factors for toxin biosynthesis, bioinformatic and functional analyses shed light on complex polyketide assembly, branching events, and stereochemical issues. Our data also indicate the involvement of a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase in a rare six-electron oxidation of a terminal methyl group, giving rise to the tricarboxylic acid.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.1" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Identification and Analysis of the Bongkrekic Acid Biosynthesis Gene Cluster</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">To unravel the genetic basis for the biosynthetic assembly of BA we have subjected genomic DNA (gDNA) of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pathovar <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic> (DSMZ 11318) to 454 shotgun sequencing, and remaining gaps in the putative <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene locus were filled by PCR and Sanger sequencing (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib38">Lackner et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>). Mining of the assembled contiguous regions for polyketide synthase (PKS) genes revealed an ∼64 kb gene locus consisting of 15 open reading frames ([ORFs], designated <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic>A-M, R1, and R2) (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>A) flanked by housekeeping and transposase genes. All tentative functions of the deduced gene products were assigned by sequence comparisons using the NCBI BLAST and the polyketide prediction server SMART (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib66">Schultz et al., 1998</ce:cross-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The deduced gene products of four ORFs (<ce:italic>bon</ce:italic>A-D) represent a multimodular type I PKS assembly line, and <ce:italic>bonE-M</ce:italic> code for typical accessory components and enzymes mediating tailoring reactions such as alkylation and oxygenation. Putative regulatory genes (<ce:italic>bonR1-R2</ce:italic>) are located upstream of the <ce:italic>bonA-M</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">Interestingly, a BLAST search showed that a related gene cluster is also present in the recently sequenced, yet functionally uncharacterized, <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> strain BSR3 (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib68">Seo et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>). This strain has been isolated from a diseased rice sheath and its genome shows very high overall similarity with the draft genome of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pv. <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic> (DSMZ 11318) in a synteny plot (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>B). A BlastP analysis for all protein sequences encoded on the two gene loci indicated an identity as high as 98%–99%. Nevertheless, the PKS assembly line is encoded on only three ORFs in <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> BSR3 (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>). The differences in the amino acid sequences found are primarily due to single nucleotide substitutions that are more prominent in the PKS part of the gene cluster.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">To prove the identity of the BA biosynthesis gene cluster in <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pv. <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic>, we aimed at correlating toxin production with gene expression and gene inactivation experiments, respectively. Using real-time PCR we first investigated <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene expression under BA-producing and nonproducing conditions and in parallel analyzed the culture extracts by HPLC/MS and noted a clear correlation between the production of BA and expression of the putative <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene cluster (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>C). However, to unequivocally prove the involvement of <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> genes in toxin biosynthesis a targeted deletion of biosynthetic genes was required.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.2" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Targeted PKS Gene Disruption in <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> Using the λ Red Recombination System</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">The targeted disruption of a predicted <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene proved to be challenging because genetic tools that proved to be functional for related analyses in other <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib25">Ishida et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib40">Leone et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib53">Partida-Martinez and Hertweck, 2007</ce:cross-ref>) were not applicable to <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic>. After many fruitless attempts using described methods and vectors for <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. we explored the λ Red recombination system (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib12">Datsenko and Wanner, 2000</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib14">Doublet et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>). By overlap PCR and subcloning we generated a pCR4Blunt-TOPO-based vector containing an apramycin resistance cassette (aac(3)IV) and two fragments of the <ce:italic>bonA</ce:italic> gene, which was introduced into <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> by electroporation with the helper plasmid pKD46 (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib14">Doublet et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>) carrying the λ Red recombinase apparatus and a gentamicin resistance (<ce:cross-ref refid="app3">Figure S1</ce:cross-ref> available online). In this way we were able to adapt λ Red recombination for <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> and succeeded in inactivating <ce:italic>bonA</ce:italic> by scarless double crossover. The successful deletion was verified by PCR. Finally, the mutant was also confirmed by an altered phenotype. Metabolic profiling of the Δ<ce:italic>bonA</ce:italic> mutant showed that the lack of the PKS unit completely abolished the production of BA and its congener iso-bongkrekic acid (IBA) (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>, lane b). In addition, a plate assay using <ce:italic>Aspergillus nidulans</ce:italic> as reporter strain revealed that antifungal activity of the <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> extract fully relates with the presence of BA.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.3" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Dissection of Complex Bongkrekic Acid Assembly on a Multimodular <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT PKS</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">The clear connection of bongkrekic acid biosynthesis with the sequenced <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene locus now allowed decoding the programmed biosynthetic pathway. Through detection of conserved motifs in BonA-D we inferred the PKS domains and the overall architecture of the megasynthase (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Figure 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>A; <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Our bioinformatic analysis revealed that the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS is a representative of the growing family of <ce:italic>trans-</ce:italic>AT PKSs (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib55">Piel, 2010</ce:cross-ref>). In contrast to <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>-AT PKS, modules of the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT subgroup of type I PKS lack AT domains, and the ACP domains are loaded with malonyl units in <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> by freestanding, iteratively acting AT domains (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib9">Cheng et al., 2003</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib48">Musiol et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib55">Piel, 2010</ce:cross-ref>). Typically, <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-ATs occur in pairs, one functioning as a genuine AT, whereas the other has proof-reading function (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">Jensen et al., 2012</ce:cross-ref>). Such <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT domains are encoded by <ce:italic>bonJ</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bonK</ce:italic>, respectively. As to the modular architecture of the PKS, the deduced processing line shows a high degree of colinearity with the chemical structure of BA. We identified ten functional PKS modules for the ten Claisen condensations required for the assembly of the polyketide backbone. Except for few irregularities, such as split modules and tandem ACP domains, the module compositions and the presence or absence of ketoreductase (KR) and dehydratase (DH) domains correspond well with the degree of β-keto processing of the proposed intermediates. According to the structure of the polyketide backbone two full reductive cycles should take place in modules 7 and 9, but in none of the modules any ER domains could be located. Instead, a gene putatively coding for a freestanding ER domain (BonE) with a 2-nitropropane dioxygenase (NPD)-like motif has been found adjacent to the two ECH genes. BonE shares highest sequence homology with the ER part of ThaF in thailandamide biosynthesis (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib25">Ishida et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>) and belongs to the family of NAD(P)H-dependent flavin oxidoreductases. Freestanding ERs have been implicated with enoyl reduction in PUFA synthases (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib33">Kaulmann and Hertweck, 2002</ce:cross-ref>), highly reducing fungal PKSs (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib45">Ma et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>), and bacterial <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT PKSs (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib4">Bumpus et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>), and in analogy BonE likely acts in <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> in modules 7 and 9.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">According to the deduced module architecture, the two methionine-derived methyl residues at C-6 and C-16 would be incorporated into the nascent chain by methyl transferase (MT) domains in PKS modules 2 and 8. In contrast, the alkyl branches at C-21 and C-3 are acetate-derived, suggesting that these alkyl side chains are introduced by β-branching, that is alkylation at the position that corresponds to the former β-keto position of a polyketide intermediate (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib6">Calderone, 2008</ce:cross-ref>). Indeed, the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> cluster codes for several <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-acting elements that have been correlated with such isoprenoid-like β-branching events (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib7">Calderone et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>). Specifically, we identified two gene cassettes, <ce:italic>bonFG</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bonHI</ce:italic>, that code for a 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG) synthase (HCS, BonG), as well as two enoyl-CoA hydratases (ECH, BonH, and BonI) and a freestanding KS° domain (BonF) lacking the conserved active site Cys residue. The structure of BA and the acetate incorporation pattern suggest that two β-branching events could take place: one just after the first elongation of the starter unit, whereas the other one appears to coincide with chain termination. To support and verify the colinearity-based prediction and to gain further insights into obscure steps of the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> pathway, in particular the β-branching events, we performed a more detailed bioinformatic analysis of the KS domains. An intriguing feature of KS domains of <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT PKSs is that they form distinct clades according to their substrate specificities, which allows predicting structures from noncolinear PKS architectures (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib49">Nguyen et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>) as well as structure-based gene targeting (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib15">Fisch et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>). A phylogenetic tree was constructed that comprised all <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> KS domains and over 100 KS sequences from different <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT systems (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Figure 3</ce:cross-ref>B). From the cladogram substructures were predicted that match well with the proposed intermediates. KS2, KS3, and KS10 belong to a clade of KS recognizing carbon branches at either the α- or β-position. The finding that KS3 and KS10 are specific for α-methylated precursors is in agreement with the groups incorporated in the previous elongation round. In contrast, KS4 belongs to clade (II) of KS that accept β-hydroxylated intermediates, which is in good agreement to the hydroxyl functionality predicted by the structure introduced in module 3 (KS-KR-ACP). The phylogenetic analysis also revealed that KS6 is indeed a so-called KS<ce:sup loc="post">0</ce:sup> that belongs to clade XIV. These KS lack the conserved HGTGT motif, including the His residue that is essential for decarboxylative condensation and therefore just channel the intermediate to the subsequent module. The unusual sequence (KS-KR-ACP-KS<ce:sup loc="post">0</ce:sup>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">XIV</ce:inf>-DH-ACP) was found to be characteristic for members of this clade (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib55">Piel, 2010</ce:cross-ref>). KS7 downstream the bimodule falls into clade IX of KS accepting α,β-unsaturated substrates. In the same clade KS5 as well as KS9 and KS11 are found. The module-based structure prediction suggested that two enoylreductions of intermediates bound to modules 7 and 9 are likely carried out by the freestanding enoylreductase (ER) BonE. The full reductive cycles mediated by modules 7 and 9 are supported by the finding that the subsequent KSs have specificity for saturated intermediates. Specifically, KS8 belongs to clade V, comprising KSs recognizing saturated intermediates, and KS10 belongs to clade I, composed of KSs selecting primarily saturated substrates with carbon branches; this is also in line with the presence of an MT domain in module 9.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.4" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>PKS-Based Prediction of Relative and Absolute Configuration</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">To predict the stereochemical course of β-keto processing, we investigated the specificity motifs of the KR domains (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). In canonical modular type I PKSs, the presence of a conserved LDD motif generally correlates with a <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-3-hydroxy substitution (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib5">Caffrey, 2003</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib24">Hertweck, 2009</ce:cross-ref>), and these deductions have proven reliable (e.g., for the absolute configuration of thailandamide, another complex <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> polyketide) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib26">Ishida et al., 2012</ce:cross-ref>). The hydroxyl at position C-17 was predicted to result from ketoreduction by the designated domain KR2. Because KR2 lacks the conserved Asp residue (VET instead of LDD motif), the intermediate was predicted to have an <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-configuration, which translates into the observed absolute configuration at C-17 (<ce:italic>R</ce:italic>). As to the double bond geometries, knowledge of the KR specificities is also insightful because dehydration usually occurs in an <ce:italic>anti</ce:italic> fashion, and consequently <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-hydroxyls yield <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-double bonds, whereas <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-double bonds emerge from <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-hydroxyls. This correlation holds true for the predicted <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-hydroxyl intermediates that are morphed into <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-double bonds at C-4/5 and C-8/9, and the <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-double bond at C-18/19 could be predicted from the <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-specificity of KR1. However, module 4 apparently codes for the formation of an <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-double bond, but a <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-configuration has been described for C-14/15. Another inconsistency has been observed for the unusual split bimodule (modules 5 and 6), which would be suited for the introduction of a <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-double bond into the growing polyketide chain. Consequently, a <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-double bond would be expected at the position between C-12 and C-13 in BA (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Figure 3</ce:cross-ref>A), but this is not in accord with the reported structure of BA (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib13">de Bruijn et al., 1973</ce:cross-ref>). As to the stereochemical course of the enoyl reductions, no valid predictions could be made.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.5" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Polyketide Tailoring by a Six-Electron Oxidation of a Methyl Group</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">Finally, after the full-length polyketide has been assembled and released, it likely undergoes two enzyme-mediated tailoring reactions. The C-17 hydroxyl would be methylated by the predicted <ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-methyltransferase BonM, and according to stable isotope labeling experiments (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib60">Rohm et al., 2010a</ce:cross-ref>) we assumed that the terminal carboxyl group is generated by sequential six-electron-oxidation of C-22 (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Figure 3</ce:cross-ref>A). Based on the sequence analyses, the gene product of <ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> could be the best candidate for catalyzing the oxygenation reaction, because BonL shows high similarity to various cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYPs) involved in secondary metabolic pathways. To relate BonL to the family of CYPs, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the BonL amino acid sequence and various functionally characterized orthologs. BonL is closely related to other CYPs that act as polyketide tailoring enzymes. To test the function of BonL in BA biosynthesis, we aimed at constructing a Δ<ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> mutant. Again, using the λ Red system we succeeded in inactivating <ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic>, as verified by PCR analyses (<ce:cross-ref refid="app3">Figure S2</ce:cross-ref>). The growth inhibition assay using the Δ<ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> mutant extract showed a substantially reduced antifungal activity. Furthermore, HPLC-MS analysis of the mutant’s metabolic profile revealed that the loss of <ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> led to an abrogation of BA and IBA production. Instead, we noted a new peak at a higher retention time (25.4 min), suggesting a lower polarity of the metabolite compared to BA (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Figure 2</ce:cross-ref>D, lane c), probably due to the presence of a methyl group in lieu of the carboxyl moiety. Indeed, mass spectrometry revealed that BA and the observed compound share a similar MS/MS fragmentation, and the molecular mass of 456 Da, would also support the assumption. To unequivocally determine its structure, this compound (deoxybongkrekic acid [DOBA]) was isolated from an upscaled culture (5 L). The molecular composition (C<ce:inf loc="post">28</ce:inf>H<ce:inf loc="post">40</ce:inf>O<ce:inf loc="post">5</ce:inf>) was deduced from HRESI-MS analysis and <ce:sup loc="pre">1</ce:sup>H and <ce:sup loc="pre">13</ce:sup>C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra. One-dimensional and 2D NMR data showed high similarity to those of BA confirming a related core structure. In contrast to BA, the carbon NMR of DOBA revealed the presence of only two carboxyl functions and an additional methyl carbon. HMBC coupling of the corresponding methyl protons with C-20, C-21, and C-21a established the connectivity of the methyl function and finally confirmed the proposed structure (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Figures 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/> and <ce:cross-ref refid="app3">S3</ce:cross-ref>). As to the double bond geometry, the proposed structure of DOBA is in agreement with the configuration of BA. In <ce:sup loc="pre">1</ce:sup>H NMR proton H-14 appears as triplet (<ce:italic>J</ce:italic> = 11 Hz), and the other protons at <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-double bonds have a diagnostic coupling constant of <ce:italic>J</ce:italic> = 14–16 Hz. Unfortunately, H-15 appears as a multiplet, which hampers a clear assignment. However, we observed a strong NOE contact of H-14 and H-15, and no correlation between H-15 and H-13 (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Figures 4</ce:cross-ref> and <ce:cross-ref refid="app3">S3</ce:cross-ref>). BA production could be restored by expression of <ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> in the Δ<ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> mutant (see <ce:cross-ref refid="app3">Supplemental Experimental Procedures</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="sec3" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">In this study we have unveiled the molecular basis for the biosynthesis of the infamous toxin bongkrekic acid (BA), which is produced by <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> as a contaminant in tempe bongkrek fermentation using <ce:italic>R. oligosporus</ce:italic>. The <ce:italic>Burkholderia-Rhizopus</ce:italic> interaction in the fermentation broth is reminiscent to a bacterial-fungal symbiosis producing the phytotoxin rhizoxin, the causative agent of rice seedling blight (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib36">Lackner and Hertweck, 2011</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib52">Partida-Martinez and Hertweck, 2005</ce:cross-ref>). It was shown that endofungal bacteria (<ce:italic>Burkholderia rhizoxininca</ce:italic>) of <ce:italic>Rhizopus microsporus</ce:italic> are the true producers of the toxin complex (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib64">Scherlach et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>), and these antimitotic agents are also produced in alarmingly high concentrations in soy-based tempe (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib37">Lackner et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib61">Rohm et al., 2010b</ce:cross-ref>). As to the bacterial-fungal interaction in tempe bongkrek production it should be noted that <ce:italic>Rhizopus</ce:italic> and various other mold fungi are sensitive to the bacterial toxin (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib81">Šubík and Behúň, 1994</ce:cross-ref>), as evidenced by our plate assay, which is quite in contrast to the acquired rhizoxin-resistance of <ce:italic>Rhizopus</ce:italic> bearing <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> symbionts (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib65">Schmitt et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>). Furthermore, BA formation is strictly dependent on various growth conditions and is mainly observed in fatty coconut media (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib1">Adams and Moss, 2008</ce:cross-ref>). To gain insight into the encoded BA assembly line and regulatory factors, we have subjected total gDNA of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> to shotgun sequencing and obtained a draft genome sequence. Through gap filling we have obtained a contiguous sequence harboring a gene cluster that codes for a modular type I PKS. In this work we provided three lines of evidence for the identity of <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> biosynthesis gene cluster. First, through qRT-PCR and HPLC-MS monitoring of the culture we could link <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene expression with BA production; second, the deduced module architecture of the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS is nearly colinear with the structure of the metabolite; third, selected <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene functions were confirmed by targeted gene knock out.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">The latter proved to be challenging because previously employed methods and tools for <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> were not workable. Whereas recently a transposon-based approach has been described for random transposon mutagenesis of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib72">Somprasong et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>), targeting a specific gene remained a challenge for this species. Eventually, we succeeded in deleting several <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> genes using a modified λ Red recombination protocol. λ Red recombination is known as a powerful technique in particular for functional genetics in actinomycetes (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib19">Gust et al., 2004</ce:cross-ref>), and lately it was also proven to be operative in <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib41">Lesic and Rahme, 2008</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib42">Liang and Liu, 2010</ce:cross-ref>). In the course of our studies, the use of λ Red recombination for homologous lipase overexpression in <ce:italic>Burkholderia cepacia</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib30">Jia et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>) and for recombineering in naturally transformable <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib32">Kang et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>) has been reported. To the best of our knowledge, the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene mutants are the proof of principle for the use of the λ Red recombination system for targeted gene deletions in <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic>. This is highlighted as it represents a convenient and efficient method for functional genomics of this important species. Interest in the various pathovars of <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> has grown substantially during the past years as this species has been recognized as an emerging pathogen causing disease in humans, fungi, and plants (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib67">Segonds et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib73">Stoyanova et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> is a risk factor in cystic fibrosis (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib34">Kennedy et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), and numerous infections have been described in the context of lung transplantations (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib20">Hafkin and Blumberg, 2009</ce:cross-ref>). In agriculture, <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> is known as the cause of cavity disease of the mushroom <ce:italic>Agaricus bisporus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib39">Largeteau and Savoie, 2010</ce:cross-ref>). Our finding that a <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene locus is also present in the genome of a <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> strain isolated from a diseased rice sheath suggests that BA may operate as a virulence factor in plants. Considering the high toxicity of BA it would appear plausible that the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> locus is a common trait of pathogenic <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> strains, and we propose that <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> genes may be used as taxonomic marker for classifying toxinogenic <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pathovars (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib31">Jiao et al., 2003</ce:cross-ref>). However, it should also be noted that apart from being a toxin, BA is also considered as a specific ligand of ANT and may be developed as an antiapoptotic agent. Thus, knowledge of BA biosynthesis genes will also set the basis for pathway engineering.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">In fact, the bioinformatic and functional analyses of the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> genes granted some unexpected insights into the assembly of the complex polyketide. On the ground of module architectures and KS-based phylogeny the chain elongation, β-keto processing and timing of chain branching events could be deduced. Accordingly, the molecule is assembled in a nearly colinear fashion, initiated by GNAT-mediated PKS loading (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">Gu et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), followed by ten chain elongations. Two isoprenoid-like β-branching events take place at the beginning and at the end of chain assembly. The first branching seems to yield an isopentenyl moiety after decarboxylation and double bond shift. In contrast, the terminal branching leaves the C2 unit intact and generates an acrylate side chain that is reminiscent of bryostatin substructures (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib74">Sudek et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). Notably, <ce:italic>syn</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>anti</ce:italic> eliminations during β-branching could account for the occurrence of the <ce:italic>E/Z</ce:italic>-isomers BA and IBA. It should be highlighted that no designated off-loading domain could be identified in the deduced PKS assembly line. Because β-branching appears to be the final step in chain assembly, one may conceive that branching and chain termination coincide, a scenario that is currently unparalleled.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">Whereas the constitution of the polyketide backbone is in line with the PKS domain analysis, comparison of predicted and reported configurations of the BA structure posed some riddles. Generally, the absolute configuration of the hydroxyl-substituted C-17 and the geometries of most double bonds are in full accord with the deduced KR specificity. However, we noted peculiar deviations in predicted and observed double bond geometries for the C-14 diene stretch. The deduced KR specificity in conjunction with the typical DH-mediated <ce:italic>anti</ce:italic> elimination would call for the opposite order of <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>- and <ce:italic>Z</ce:italic>-double bonds. Because the NMR-based resolution of double bond geometries in polyunsaturated compounds may be ambiguous, one might question the published structure of BA. It may be noted that the structure of BA was initially wrongly assigned (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib44">Lijmbach et al., 1970b</ce:cross-ref>), but the revised structure (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib13">de Bruijn et al., 1973</ce:cross-ref>) and the relative and absolute configuration (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib80">Zylber et al., 1973</ce:cross-ref>) has been confirmed by numerous total syntheses (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib11">Corey and Tramontano, 1984</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib16">Francais et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref refid="bib17">2011</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib63">Sato et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib70">Shindo et al., 2004</ce:cross-ref>). Even so, <ce:italic>E/Z</ce:italic>- and <ce:italic>Z/E</ce:italic>-isomers are not easily discriminated from each other, and future synthetic and analytical studies may be needed to address this particular issue. In recent years, stereochemical analyses of complex polyketides are more and more often supported or even facilitated using bioinformatics, as demonstrated for etnangien (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib47">Menche et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>), thuggacins (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib3">Bock et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>), chivosazole (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib27">Janssen et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), and thailandamide (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib26">Ishida et al., 2012</ce:cross-ref>). The in silico dissection of the BA pathway and the configurational analysis underline the power of bioinformatics deductions, yet also indicates a potential caveat regarding the double bond geometries.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0085" view="all">In addition to stable isotope labeling and <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS analysis, the proposed polyketide assembly is supported by the isolation and structural elucidation of a key pathway intermediate from an engineered <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> strain. We selected a candidate gene (<ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic>) coding for a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase that could be involved in a polyketide tailoring reaction. We successfully generated a Δ<ce:italic>bonL</ce:italic> mutant producing a bongkrekic acid derivative. The full structure elucidation by MS, 1D, and 2D NMR revealed that this compound bears a methyl group in place of the C-22 carboxyl moiety. Consequently, C-22 is derived from C-2 of the priming acetate, which is in line with the <ce:sup loc="pre">13</ce:sup>C<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-acetate labeling experiments, and the C-1 carboxyl constitutes the COOH terminus of the polyketide chain. It is intriguing to find that BonL is a CYP that catalyzes the oxidation of the polyketide terminus, and it appears that BonL is involved in a six-electron oxidation. CYPs are known to catalyze a variety of oxidation reactions (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib76">Werck-Reichhart and Feyereisen, 2000</ce:cross-ref>), mainly resulting in hydroxyl groups or epoxides. Considering the large number of functionally characterized cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, it is surprising to find that only very few cytochrome P450 enzymes catalyze multiple oxidation steps in biosynthetic pathways. Interestingly, the oxidation of methyl groups to carboxylic acids has been primarily observed in plant and fungal terpene biosynthetic pathways. Rare examples include CYPs involved in gibberellic acid biosynthesis in <ce:italic>Arabidopsis thaliana</ce:italic> (ent-kaurene-oxidase) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib22">Helliwell et al., 1999</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>Gibberella fujikuroi</ce:italic> (P450-1) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib62">Rojas et al., 2001</ce:cross-ref>). Functionally related CYPs mediate the oxidation of amorpha-4,11-diene to artemisinic acid in <ce:italic>Artemisia annua</ce:italic> (CYP71AV1) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib59">Ro et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib75">Teoh et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>), and participate in the biosynthesis of resin acid in conifers (abietadienol/abietadienal oxidase PtAO) (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib58">Ro et al., 2005</ce:cross-ref>). The biotransformation of pentalenene to pentalen-13-al in <ce:italic>Streptomyces avermitilis</ce:italic> by the CYP PtlI is a rare example of a similar process in bacterial terpene biosynthesis (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib56">Quaderer et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>). In contrast, CYPs catalyzing sequential methyl oxidations in complex polyketide pathways are scarce. From deletion of a CYP450-encoding gene, <ce:italic>fscP</ce:italic>, in the candicidin/FR-008 biosynthesis gene locus it was inferred that the pathway involves oxidation of a methyl branch to a carboxylate residue (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib8">Chen et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>). Borrelidin biosynthesis in <ce:italic>Streptomyces parvulus</ce:italic> Tü4055 employs a CYP (BorI) that is capable of oxidizing a methyl group to an aldehyde, which may be further oxidized to the carboxylic acid in a shunt pathway (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib51">Olano et al., 2004</ce:cross-ref>). The multifunctional CYP AurH from <ce:italic>Streptomyces thioluteus</ce:italic>, mediates a rare sequential hydroxylation-heterocyclization sequence (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib57">Richter et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>), and point mutations result in regiodivergent, multiple oxidations of an allylic methyl group to the carboxyl via alcohol and aldehyde intermediates (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib79">Zocher et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>). We assume that BonL oxidizes the C-22 methyl of BA in a similar fashion, yet no partially oxidized intermediates could be detected by MS. Nonetheless, BonL is a fresh entry in the small family of multifunctional CYPs. It is one of the very few carboxylate-forming CYPs in polyketide pathways, and to our knowledge, the first one that is involved in the oxidation of a carbon chain terminus. Thus, it is an important addition to the currently available enzymatic toolbox for polyketide tailoring.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0090" view="all">Finally, from a natural product perspective <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. are rather neglected organisms (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib77">Winter et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>). Compared to the more prolific bacterial producers of biologically active secondary metabolites, such as actinomycetes and myxobacteria, only few natural products from <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp. are known. To date the only examples of polyketide metabolites from this genus that have been discovered and linked to a gene cluster include rhizoxins from <ce:italic>Burkholderia rhizoxinica</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib25">Ishida et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib40">Leone et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib53">Partida-Martinez and Hertweck, 2007</ce:cross-ref>), thailandamide (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib49">Nguyen et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>) and bactobolins from <ce:italic>Burkholderia thailandensis</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib69">Seyedsayamdost et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>), and enacyloxins from <ce:italic>Burkholderia ambifaria</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib46">Mahenthiralingam et al., 2011</ce:cross-ref>). The BA biosynthetic machinery is an important addition to the growing family of <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> modular PKS assembly lines. Notably, all of the previously studied pathways involve hybrid PKS-nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs), and the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS represents, to our knowledge, the first genuine PKS system.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec4" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Significance</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0095" view="all">
                     <ce:bold>The respiratory toxin bongkrekic acid caused countless health-threatening and even lethal intoxications after consumption of the Southeast-Asian specialty tempe bongkrek. Whereas it was long known that the toxin producer is a contaminant of the fungus used for fermentation, the genetic basis for BA biosynthesis has remained obscure. Through whole-genome sequencing of <ce:italic>Burkholderia gladioli</ce:italic> pv. <ce:italic>cocovenenans</ce:italic> we identified the gene locus for a giant <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-AT PKS and accessory enzymes catalyzing assembly the polyunsaturated tricarboxylic acid. For functional analyses we successfully adapted λ Red recombination and provide the proof of principle for targeted gene deletions in <ce:italic>B. gladioli.</ce:italic> This protocol is of great value because <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pathovars are the cause of onion soft rot, mushroom cavity disease, and emerging risk factors for patients suffering from cystic fibrosis and lung transplantations. In this context, it is noteworthy that a strain that infected rice plants harbors an intact <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> gene cluster. From a biosynthetic point of view, unexpected insights are granted into the stereocontrolled assembly of BA by a thiotemplate. Such multifunctional enzymes are underexplored in <ce:italic>Burkholderia</ce:italic> spp., whereas the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS represents now a characterized genuine PKS in this genus. Dissection of the <ce:italic>bon</ce:italic> PKS revealed the pathway to the unusual bifurcated fatty acid structure, suggesting an unprecedented scenario where β-branching and chain termination coincide. Through isolation of deoxybongkrekic acid from a CYP mutant we inferred an unparalleled six-electron oxidation of the methyl terminus of a complex polyketide chain. Considering that BA is one of the most potent respiratory toxins known, these insights are of significance for pinpointing toxinogenic <ce:italic>B. gladioli</ce:italic> pathovars in the context of food processing, agriculture and medicine. On pharmacological grounds knowledge of the molecular basis for BA biosynthesis paves the way for engineering specific ligands for adenine nucleotide translocator, a promising target for antiapoptosis and chemotherapy.</ce:bold>
