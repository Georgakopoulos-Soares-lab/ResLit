Abstract 

ADC-56, a novel extended-spectrum AmpC (ESAC) β-lactamase, was identified in an Acinetobacter baumannii clinical isolate. ADC-56 possessed an R148Q change compared with its putative progenitor, ADC-30, which enabled it to hydrolyze cefepime. Molecular modeling suggested that R148 interacted with Q267, E272, and I291 through a hydrogen bond network which constrained the H-10 helix. This permitted cefepime to undergo conformational changes in the active site, with the carboxyl interacting with R340, likely allowing for better binding and turnover. 

TEXT 

Acinetobacter baumannii is a Gram-negative organism that is increasingly recognized as a pathogen associated with nosocomial infections ( 13 ). Resistance to β-lactams in A. baumannii is primarily mediated by β-lactamases ( 13 ). Overproduction of the intrinsic AmpC cephalosporinase is largely responsible for cephalosporin resistance ( 2 , 3 ). Recent phylogenetic analysis found that the chromosomal AmpC genes in Acinetobacter spp. probably descended from a common ancestor ( 14 ). These AmpC variants are now designated A cinetobacter - d erived c ephalosporinases (ADCs) ( 8 ). ADCs typically hydrolyze penicillins and narrow- and extended-spectrum cephalosporins but not cefepime or carbapenems ( 14 ). Save for one exception, these cephalosporinases are predominantly restricted to the genus Acinetobacter ( 10 ). Among various ADC variants reported, only one variant, ADC-33, has been identified as an ADC enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cefepime ( 15 ). ADC-33 possesses two amino acid changes in the Ω loop, both of which are required for the extension of the spectrum. Here, we present our analysis of the ADC-56 β-lactamase, the second ADC group extended-spectrum AmpC (ESAC) cephalosporinase. 

Two A. baumannii isolates, AB78 and AB150, were recovered from epidemiologically unrelated patients at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center between 2007 and 2008. AB78 was identified in a bronchoalveolar lavage specimen of a 67-year-old female patient who was admitted for vascular insufficiency and subsequently developed ventilator-associated pneumonia. The patient was treated with intravenous meropenem and nebulized tobramycin but developed empyema due to A. baumannii and eventually died of septic shock. AB150 was cultured from a wound of a 74-year-old female patient who was admitted for wound infection following lumbar laminectomy. She was treated with intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam and had an uneventful recovery. 

AB78 and AB150 were clonally related as determined by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), and both belonged to ST208, which is part of worldwide clone 2/clonal complex 92 ( 16 ). Both isolates were positive for a bla ADC group gene by PCR analysis. Sequencing revealed that A. baumannii AB78 encoded ADC-30, which has 7 substitutions relative to ADC-7 in the amino acid sequence. A. baumannii AB150 encoded ADC-56, a novel variant of ADC-30 with an arginine-to-glutamine substitution at position 148 (R148Q) in the deduced amino acid sequence. To construct isogenic Escherichia coli clones producing ADC-30 and ADC-56, the bla ADC genes were amplified with primers ADC-F-XbaI (5′-GCG TCTAGA ATGCGATTTAAAAAAATT-3′) and ADC-R-BamHI (5′-CC GGATCC TTATTTCTTTATTGCATT-3′). The PCR products and pBC-SK(−) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) were double digested with XbaI and BamHI (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA), ligated, and used to transform E. coli DH10B. Clones encoding ADC-30/56 were selected on LB agar plates containing ampicillin (50 μg/ml) and chloramphenicol (30 μg/ml). 

Table 1 shows MICs of β-lactam agents as determined by the agar dilution method ( 4 ). A. baumannii AB78 was highly resistant to cephalosporins as well as carbapenems. A. baumannii AB150 was resistant to cephalosporins but susceptible to carbapenems. AB78 did not possess carbapenemase genes bla OXA-23 , bla OXA-40 , and bla OXA-58 as determined by PCR. In addition, it was negative for carbapenemase activity by the modified Hodge test, and its crude extracts did not display any hydrolysis of meropenem. The mechanism of carbapenem resistance in AB78 thus did not appear to be conferred by a β-lactamase. In the E. coli DH10B clones, the MIC of cefoxitin for E. coli DH10B(pADC-56) was 64-fold lower than that for E. coli DH10B(pADC-30). On the other hand, a 32-fold increase in the cefepime MIC was observed for E. coli DH10B(pADC-56) over E. coli DH10B(pADC-30) ( Table 1 ). Since the two β-lactamase genes differed by only a single nucleotide, resulting in the R148Q change, these phenotypic changes were likely due to this replacement, which is located in the proximity of the catalytic tyrosine residue at position 150.

We then purified ADC-30 and ADC-56 to determine their kinetic parameters. The E. coli DH10B clones were grown in 4 liters of LB broth containing 50 μg/ml of ampicillin overnight at 37°C, harvested, sonicated, and ultracentrifuged for 60 min at 4°C. The supernatants were subjected to gel filtration chromatography (HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75; GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI) followed by ion-exchange chromatography (HiTrap SP HP; GE Healthcare). The purified enzymes were then dialyzed overnight against 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The purity of ADC-30 and ADC-56 enzymes was estimated to be greater than 90% by SDS-PAGE, and their molecular masses were estimated as 40.5 kDa (data not shown). Steady-state kinetic parameters ( K m , k cat , and k cat / K m ) were measured in triplicate in 50 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.0) by using a UV spectrophotometer (DU800; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). The kinetic parameters of ADC-30/56 are shown in Table 2 . In general, the kinetic properties of the ADC enzymes were consistent with MICs of the E. coli clones encoding ADC-30 and ADC-56. ADC-30/56 efficiently hydrolyzed cephalothin and ampicillin. ADC-30 also hydrolyzed cefoxitin, but no hydrolysis of cefepime was seen (this is a finding expected of an AmpC enzyme). ADC-56, however, catalyzed the inactivation of cefepime but not cefoxitin. Low-level hydrolyses of ceftazidime and cefotaxime were measured with both ADC-30 and ADC-56. We were not able to detect hydrolysis of ticarcillin, aztreonam, or imipenem with the enzyme preparations available. Nonetheless, our results suggested that the R148Q substitution indeed altered the kinetic properties of ADC-56, which would account for the lower cefoxitin MIC and the higher cefepime MIC than those of its putative progenitor, ADC-30.

To further delineate the role of the amino acid at position 148, we performed site-directed mutagenesis to generate three variants of ADC-30 (R148A, R148E, and R148K) by using the QuikChange II site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies). Mutations in the recombinant plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing. MICs of cefoxitin for these derivatives were the same with E. coli DH10B(pADC-56) (MIC, 4 μg/ml) and much lower than that for E. coli DH10B(pADC-30) (MIC, 256 μg/ml) ( Table 1 ). These ADC β-lactamases also had lower MICs of cefotaxime and aztreonam as well. On the other hand, the MICs of cefepime in these mutants were 4- to 32-fold higher than that for their progenitor, with alanine and glutamic acid having a more substantial effect than lysine. 

The amino acids at position 148 that are implicated in higher levels of resistance to cefepime here are smaller than arginine and carry less positive charge, with the exception of lysine. We therefore speculated that these alternative residues created a conformation that was more favorable for cefepime to bind to the active site. To confirm this hypothesis, a molecular model of ADC β-lactamase was generated from the deposited GenBank ADC-7 protein sequence AY648950 and the Enterobac-ter aerogenes CMY-10 β-lactamase template (PDB entry 1ZKJ) ( 8 , 9 ) by the SWISS-MODEL automated protein structure homology modeling server (available at http://swissmodel.expasy.org ) ( 1 ). We optimized the generated model by energy minimization using Discovery Studio 2.1 software (Accelrys, San Diego, CA). The minimization and the quality assessment of the ADC β-lactamase model were first described by Drawz et al. ( 6 ). The ADC-30 variant was generated using Build and Edit Protein tools of DS 2.1 by substituting N57S, Q126K, P143S, T246A, S317T, and V252A. The ADC-56 variant was generated from the ADC-30 variant, adding a R148Q substitution. The models were further minimized and the side chains of altered residue refined. We used the Protein Structure and Model Assessment Tools available at http://swissmodel.expasy.org to assess the quality of the models. The atomic empirical mean force potential (ANOLEA) ( 11 ) evaluation of the model's packing quality showed that 96% of the amino acids were in the favorable energy environment. The stereochemical qualities of the ADC models were assessed using the Procheck program. The minimized and equilibrated ADC models were used to construct the acylation complexes of the ADC β-lactamase with cefepime. The ligand structure was built using DS 2.1 Fragment Builder tools. The CHARMm force field was applied; the molecule was solvated with periodic boundary condition (PBC) and minimized using a Standard Dynamics Cascade protocol. The minimized ligand was docked in the active site of the enzyme using LibDock ( 5 ). The acyl-enzyme complex was created by making a covalent bond with Ser64, and the assembly was further minimized using the Conjugate Gradient algorithm with PBC to 0.001 minimum derivatives. 

The model of ADC-30 with cefepime docked in the active site showed that R148 interacts with Q267, E272, and I291 ( Fig. 1 a). Here, the carbonyl forms hydrogen bonds with K67 and Y150 ( Fig. 1 b and c). We suspect that this forms a hydrogen bonding network that constrains the H-10 helix. When ADC-56 was modeled with cefepime, the carbonyl maintained the hydrogen bonds with K67 and Y150 ( Fig. 1 d and e). However, the R148Q substitution in ADC-56 disrupted the interactions with H-10 helix, providing the helix with more flexibility. This permits a conformational change of cefepime in the active site, with carboxyl interacting with R340, which may allow for better binding (lower K m ) and turnover (higher k cat ). This held true for A148, D148, and E148 as well but was not as significant with K148 (which partially keeps the hydrogen bonds with H-10). These findings corroborate the MICs and kinetic properties described above.

Arginine at position 148 is conserved throughout AmpC proteins from various Gram-negative bacteria ( 7 ). Thus, it is possible that the enzymes with alternative residues at this position, despite their catalytic advantages against β-lactams, are unstable and do not commonly persist after initial emergence. Among various ADC variants, ADC-33 (proline-to-arginine change at position 210 and an associated duplication of the alanine residue at position 215) has been identified as an ADC enzyme capable of hydrolyzing cefepime ( 15 ). Both of these changes were located within the Ω loop. Presently, it is believed that this change either provides an increased opening for β-lactam substrates to bind to the active site or creates disorder. In addition, various amino acid changes in the H-10 helix have also been associated with the extension of the spectrum of AmpC enzymes to cefepime in Enterobacteriaceae ( 12 ). Our analysis demonstrates that substitution of arginine at position 148, which is in the vicinity of the catalytic site, also leads to altered substrate specificity, including robust hydrolysis of cefepime.
