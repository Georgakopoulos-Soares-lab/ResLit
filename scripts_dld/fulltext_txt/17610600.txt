                  Representatives (n = 31) of outbreak strains of Acinetobacter baumannii from five countries fell into three clear groups, designated Groups 1–3, based on their ompA (outer-membrane protein A), csuE (part of a pilus assembly system required for biofilm formation) and bla
                     OXA-51-like (the intrinsic carbapenemase gene in A. baumannii) gene sequences. With the exception of the closely related alleles within the Group 1 clonal complex, alleles at each locus were highly distinct from each other, with a minimum of 14 nucleotide differences between any two alleles. Isolates within a group shared the same combination of alleles at the three loci, providing compelling evidence that the outbreak strains investigated belonged to three clonal lineages. These corresponded to the previously identified European clones I–III. Sequence differences among the alleles were used to design multiplex PCRs to rapidly assign isolates belonging to particular genotypes to sequence groups. In the UK, genotypes belonging to the Group 1 clonal complex have been particularly successful, accounting for the vast majority of isolates referred from hospitals experiencing problems with Acinetobacter.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara90" view="all">Representatives (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 31) of outbreak strains of <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> from five countries fell into three clear groups, designated Groups 1–3, based on their <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> (outer-membrane protein A), <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> (part of a pilus assembly system required for biofilm formation) and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> (the intrinsic carbapenemase gene in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>) gene sequences. With the exception of the closely related alleles within the Group 1 clonal complex, alleles at each locus were highly distinct from each other, with a minimum of 14 nucleotide differences between any two alleles. Isolates within a group shared the same combination of alleles at the three loci, providing compelling evidence that the outbreak strains investigated belonged to three clonal lineages. These corresponded to the previously identified European clones I–III. Sequence differences among the alleles were used to design multiplex PCRs to rapidly assign isolates belonging to particular genotypes to sequence groups. In the UK, genotypes belonging to the Group 1 clonal complex have been particularly successful, accounting for the vast majority of isolates referred from hospitals experiencing problems with <ce:italic>Acinetobacter.</ce:italic>
                  </ce:simple-para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">INTRODUCTION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> has emerged globally as an increasingly important nosocomial pathogen. It particularly affects patients in intensive care and burns units, and is usually resistant to most available antibiotics. Carbapenems have been the antibiotics of choice for treatment of infections caused by this organism, but resistance to carbapenems is becoming common, and very few therapeutic options remain. There have been reports of the increased prevalence of this organism in hospitals in many countries, including the UK [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>], France [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>], Spain [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>], Portugal [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>], Italy [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>], Greece [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib8 bib9 bib10">8–10</ce:cross-refs>], Israel [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>], Bulgaria [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>], the USA [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>] and Russia [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>], with outbreaks often occurring in multiple hospitals. Many of the genotypes involved have been found to belong to pan-European clones (lineages) I, II or III, as defined by amplified fragment length polymorphism analysis [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">During recent years, hospitals in the UK have experienced numerous outbreaks caused by widespread genotypes of <ce:italic>A. baumannii.</ce:italic> In particular, the genotypes known as the South-east clone and the OXA-23 clone 1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>] have affected over 40 hospitals each and have persisted for a number of years, and since at least 2000 in the case of the South-east clone. In addition, in one hospital, a further genotype, known as the T strain, which was associated originally with military casualties returning from Iraq [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>], caused continuing problems for &gt;2 years. More recently, problems with a further genotype, referred to as the North-west strain, have been associated with several hospitals in northern England and the Midlands. These highly successful strains represent a new problem in the UK, with infections and colonisations by particular genotypes not having been observed previously on this scale.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">A better understanding of how these organisms emerge and spread is urgently required. The genotypes mentioned above were defined by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) of <ce:italic>Apa</ce:italic>I-digested genomic DNA. However, it is clear that the alternative approach of sequence-based typing offers a number of advantages, and can provide important insights into population structures. A multilocus sequence typing scheme, based on housekeeping genes, has been described for <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, and has been reported to give results that are comparable to those provided by PFGE [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>]. To provide further information concerning the UK isolates, the present study chose to examine sequence variation within three genes likely to be under selective pressure. This approach has been used previously for <ce:italic>Legionella pneumophila</ce:italic>, in which context it has proved highly useful for outbreak investigations [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>]. Since higher numbers of polymorphisms are seen, such an approach has the potential for greater discrimination than schemes based on housekeeping genes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>], and has the added advantage that it can provide information relevant to pathogenicity.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">The genes investigated in the present study were <ce:italic>ompA, csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf>. Outer-membrane protein A (encoded by <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic>) is a porin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>], and has also been found to induce apoptosis of epithelial cells [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>]. The <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> gene codes for part of a pilus assembly system, thought to be essential for biofilm formation [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>]. The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> gene codes for the intrinsic carbapenemase found in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>], although clinically significant resistance to carbapenems mediated by <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> has been observed only in isolates with the insertion sequence IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> located immediately upstream of the gene; minor sequence differences are also seen between susceptible and resistant isolates [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para50" view="all">The present study began by comparing the gene sequences of representatives of outbreak strains from the UK, both within and among types defined by PFGE. The study was then extended to include outbreak strains from Spain, France, Israel and The Netherlands, including some known to belong to European clones I–III. These isolates were therefore separated by distance and, in some cases, by a considerable period of time. A number of genotypes defined by PFGE were represented, including some, e.g., OXA-23 clone 1, and the French AYE VEB-1 strain, that were widespread in the country of origin and had affected numerous hospitals, as well as others that were not so prevalent. The findings provide important information on the population structure of these organisms, which is central to any understanding of their epidemiology.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>

                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">The panel of 34 isolates included representatives of all outbreak strains of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> identified from UK hospitals by the Laboratory of HealthCare Associated Infection, Health Protection Agency, London, UK during recent years and three sporadic strains. Where multiple representatives of a genotype were included, these were, where possible, selected from different hospitals, and isolated some time apart (between 2002 and 2006). Sporadic isolates (NW1, L4, IR1) had unique PFGE profiles, and were PCR-negative for the class 1 integrase gene [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>]. Isolate designations, according to hospital (1–32) and patient (A–J), correspond to those used in previous studies [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>]. Isolates new to this study, i.e., SE6, SW1, L5A and L5B (isolated during 2006) and NW1, L4 and IR1 (sporadic strains isolated between 2003 and 2006), are labelled according to hospital region (SE, south-east England; L, London; SW, south-west England; M, Midlands; NW, north-west England; IR, Ireland), the particular hospital within each region (1–6), and the patient (A–B). The panel also included representatives of European clones I (RUH 875, RUH 2034) and II (RUH 134) isolated between 1982 and 1986, and of European clone III (RUH 5875) from outbreaks in The Netherlands [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>]. Isolates from Spain were representatives of two outbreak strains (designated clones I and II) identified in a hospital in northern Spain [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>], and those from Israel were representatives of four outbreak strains (designated A, B, F and H) identified in a tertiary-care hospital. The AYE VEB-1 strain has affected multiple hospitals in France [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Identification of all isolates as <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> was confirmed by detection of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>]. Multiplex PCR to detect other OXA carbapenemase genes was performed as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>]. Detection of IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> adjacent to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> was performed using primers IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic>F and OXA-51-likeR as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">PFGE and sequence-based typing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">PFGE of <ce:italic>Apa</ce:italic>I-digested genomic DNA was performed as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>]. Primers for amplifying and sequencing <ce:italic>ompA, csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> gene fragments are listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>. Primers for <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> were designed from sequences available at GenBank (AY485227, DQ093960; AY241696) or from sequences determined during initial studies (DQ289014–DQ289019). These were aligned and consensus primers were designed from common sequence areas. Amplicons were made using the primers in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref> that gave the 966-bp (<ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic>), 976-bp (<ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic>) or 825-bp (<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf>) products, and sequencing was performed using both these and the internal primers. Some isolates (notably sporadic isolates) failed to give the larger <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> (e.g., NW1) or <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> (IR1) amplicons, and the internal primers were therefore used to amplify the smaller fragment. PCRs were performed in 25-μL volumes containing 3 μL of extracted DNA, 10 pmol of each primer and 1.5 U of <ce:italic>Taq</ce:italic> DNA polymerase in 1 × PCR buffer containing 1.5 mM MgCl2 (Qiagen, Crawley, UK) and 200 μM each dNTP. Extracted DNA was prepared by suspending 2–3 colonies in 100 μL of tissue culture water, vortexing for 10 s, and then centrifuging at 10 000 <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 2 min. Standard PCR conditions were used in combination with the annealing temperatures listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref> for each set of primers. For example, to amplify the 966-bp <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> fragment, PCR conditions were 94°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 45 s, 50°C for 45 s and 72°C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. PCR products were initially checked by agarose gel electrophoresis and were then purified using Qiaquick purification columns (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The products were then sequenced using a Beckman Coulter CEQ Dye Terminator Cycle Sequencer with a Quick Start kit and a CEQ8000 sequencer (Beckman). Sequences were analysed and aligned using GeneBuilder software (Applied Maths, Sint-Martens-Latem, Belgium) and compared with sequences available at GenBank using BLAST searches (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs10" xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/" xlink:type="simple">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/</ce:inter-ref>).</ce:para>

                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">Multiplex PCRs for identification of the <ce:italic>ompA, csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> sequence groups defined as Group 1 and Group 2 (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref660a" refid="cesec80">Results</ce:cross-ref>) were performed using the primers listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>

These primers were designed from a comparison of Group 1 and Group 2 sequences at the three loci from GenBank accession numbers DQ648278, DQ648279, DQ289014–DQ289019, AY750909, AY750911, DQ309276 and DQ309277. Reagent concentrations and PCR conditions were essentially the same as those used for a multiplex OXA carbapenemase gene/class 1 integrase gene PCR [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>], except that only 30 cycles were used and the final concentration of each primer was 400 nM (10 pmol/25-μL reaction). PCR conditions were 94°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 45 s, 57°C for 45 s and 72°C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The temperature of the heated lid of the thermocycler was 105°C. Identification of a strain as a member of Group 1 or Group 2 required the amplification of all three fragments in the corresponding multiplex PCR, and an absence of any amplification by the other multiplex PCR. Group 3 isolates were defined by the amplification of only the <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> fragment in the Group 2 PCR, and the amplification of only the <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> fragments in the Group 1 PCR.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">PFGE and sequence-based typing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">PFGE profiles, together with the allele designations at each locus, are shown for all strains in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>. The sequences corresponding to the allele designations are listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>. Alleles 1a, 1a* and 1b differed from allele 1 by only one or two nucleotides. Such differences were minor compared to the more extensive differences revealed between alleles 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 at each locus (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). Sequence-based typing divided the isolates into three distinct groups (designated Groups 1–3), with isolates within a group all sharing the same combination of alleles at the three loci (albeit with some diversity in the Group 1 alleles). All 31 representatives of outbreak strains investigated, from all five countries, belonged to one of these three groups. The strains also clustered into these three groups by PFGE, but with a similarity of only 69% with the parameters used in the present analysis.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Sporadic isolates had further, distinct alleles and/or unique combinations of alleles at the three loci. Genotypes of all three groups were associated with additional OXA carbapenemase genes, i.e., OXA-23 clone 1 (Group 1), OXA-23 clone 2 and BUAC-1 (Group 2) with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-23-like</ce:inf> genes; North-west (NW) strain and Israel strain B (Group 1) with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-58-like</ce:inf> genes; and Israel strain A (Group 1) and Spanish clones I (Group 3) and II (Group 1) with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-24-like</ce:inf> (<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-40</ce:inf> or <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-40-like</ce:inf>) genes. The sporadic isolate L4 was also PCR-positive for a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-23-like</ce:inf> gene.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">The number of sequence differences among alleles associated with the different groups at each locus was substantial (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>), and much greater than those observed between pairs of alleles of housekeeping genes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>]. For example, individual <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> alleles differed from one another by up to 5%, with alleles 1 and 2 having 24 base differences over the entire 449-bp fragment. For the <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> gene, not only was there a considerable number of nucleotide polymorphisms (e.g., there were 52 nucleotide differences between alleles 1 and 2), but there was also a highly polymorphic area of sequence that was 9 bp and 15 bp shorter in alleles 2 and 4, respectively (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>, which compares the translated amino-acid sequences of alleles 1 and 2). In the case of allele 4 (found in sporadic isolate IR1), there was also an area of sequence that was 3 bp longer than in the other alleles; in addition, this allele had &gt;80 nucleotide differences with respect to both the Group 1 and 2 alleles. The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> alleles 1, 2 and 3 corresponded to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-66</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-69</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-71</ce:inf>, respectively, over the 693 bp of sequence compared (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl4"/>); any two of these alleles had 14–17 nucleotide differences from the other over this sequence. Although many of the nucleotide changes were silent, a number of amino-acid changes were apparent (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>), which may have a significant effect on the function of the proteins.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Although the nucleotide differences among the Group 1 alleles at each locus were relatively small (1–3 bases), these resulted, in the case of the <ce:italic>csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> genes, in amino-acid substitutions (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). Amino-acid substitutions among most of the proteins of the various <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> alleles found in this study have been compared previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. Sequence differences from <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-66</ce:inf> among Group 1 isolates (alleles 1a, 1a* and 1b) were associated in every case with the presence of IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> upstream of the gene (isolates 31J, SW1, Israel 21, 4I). These isolates had <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> as the sole carbapenemase gene and were meropenem-resistant (MIC &gt;4 mg/L). Other representatives of the same genotypes, but which did not have IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> adjacent to the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">-OXA-51-like</ce:inf> gene, had the ‘standard’ allele 1 (corresponding to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-66</ce:inf>) (isolates 16B, 4A, L5A) and were meropenem-susceptible (MIC &lt;4 mg/L). Sequence changes in <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> associated with IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> were consistent within a PFGE-defined genotype, but varied among genotypes.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Group 1 isolates included all of the highly successful genotypes currently found in the UK, including the very widespread SE clone and OXA-23 clone 1, and the representative of European clone II. Group 2 isolates included the representatives of European clone I and the French AYE VEB-1 strain. The outbreak strains from the UK in this group, known as OXA-23 clone 2, W strain and BUAC-1, have affected far smaller numbers of patients than those in Group 1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. BUAC-1 has been associated particularly with patients from burns units. The Midlands 2 genotype (Group 3) is a further relatively minor outbreak strain in the UK [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. Group 3 also included the representative of European clone III and outbreak strains from hospitals in Spain and Israel.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Multiplex PCRs for identification of sequence type groups</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">While sequencing is highly informative, it is not readily available in all laboratories and is not ideal for screening large numbers of isolates. The substantial differences found in the sequences described above presented the opportunity to design primers to selectively amplify the alleles of each group at these three loci, and thereby to allow rapid identification of isolates belonging to each of the outbreak groups.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">All strains belonging to the Group 1 clonal complex yielded all three fragments in the Group 1 PCR, and none in the Group 2 PCR, while strains belonging to Group 2 gave the expected converse results (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). Strains belonging to Group 3 (<ce:italic>ompA, csuE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> alleles 2, 3 and 3), which share the same <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic> allele as Group 2, gave only the middle fragment (for <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic>) in the Group 2 PCR, but gave the top two fragments (for the other two loci) in the Group 1 PCR, despite some mismatches between the primer sequences and these Group 3 alleles. Outbreak strains can therefore be readily assigned into these sequence types by use of these multiplex PCRs, without any need for sequencing. Nevertheless, sequencing remains the best means of confirmation of the results.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para180" view="all">The isolates investigated in this study were from five different countries and represented a number of distinct PFGE types. The genes studied had high numbers of polymorphisms, but all of the outbreak strains belonged to one of three, highly distinct, sequence type groups. These data suggest that these outbreak isolates consist of three distinct lineages, and that outbreak strains of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> are highly clonal, with a low frequency of recombination [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>]. In such populations, relatively few overall sequence types are seen, as was the case in the present study. Diversity may be caused by movement of insertion sequences [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>], a feature already recognised as being important in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib33 bib34 bib35">33–35</ce:cross-refs>]. The clonal lineages identified were found to correspond to pan-European clones I, II and III, defined by amplified fragment length polymorphism analysis (Group 1 corresponding to European clone II, Group 2 to European clone I, and Group 3 to European clone III), thus providing further evidence to support the original descriptions of these lineages [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>]. Isolates can be assigned to these lineages with absolute certainty by sequence typing, while amplified fragment length polymorphism analysis may provide more ambiguous results.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para190" view="all">These sequence types were present in multiple and distant locations, and may arise in different hospitals through a combination of independent selection from very widespread common ancestors and some degree of spread, perhaps facilitated by patient transfers [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>]. Perhaps ‘outbreak’ may not be the most appropriate term to apply to these strains, since their persistence may be a result, at least in part, of their continual selection in hospitals under antibiotic pressure, rather than a true outbreak situation. This may explain their repeated reappearance in hospitals, despite the best efforts of staff to eradicate them.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para200" view="all">All of the highly successful genotypes defined in the UK by PFGE were found to belong to sequence type Group 1 (European clone II). This group may represent a particularly successful lineage, accounting for the increased prevalence of this organism in hospitals in recent years. Similarly, the most prevalent genotypes from the Israeli hospital (Israel A and B, in that order) were both representatives of Group 1. Ikonomidis <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>] reported a predominance of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-66</ce:inf> among Greek isolates of <ce:italic>A. baumannii;as bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-66</ce:inf> is the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51-like</ce:inf> allele of Group 1 isolates (allele 1), this indicates that Group 1 genotypes are also predominant in Greece. However, in some countries, outbreak strains of the other sequence type groups appear to be more important. Thus, the AYE VEB-1 strain, which has affected large numbers of hospitals in France, belongs to Group 2. In the Spanish hospital from which the isolates of clone I (Group 3) and clone II (Group 1) were obtained, clone I is currently the most prevalent; both have <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-40</ce:inf> as an additional carbapenemase gene [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">The stability of these sequence types is remarkable; RUH 875 and RUH 2034 were isolated some 20 years before other representatives of Group 2, yet their sequences at the loci examined are identical. Similarly, Huys <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>] found that <ce:italic>adeB</ce:italic> sequences in representatives of European clones I, II and III were distinct from each other, but identical within a clone, despite representatives having been isolated from different countries at intervals of up to 20 years.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">PFGE clearly divided the outbreak strains into clusters that agreed with the sequence type groupings (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>), and also gave greater discrimination than that obtained by sequence-based typing. The so-called ‘micro-variation’ revealed by PFGE profiles, coupled with any extra information obtained from integron analysis and detection of additional genes such as <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-23-like</ce:inf> genes, is helpful in investigating individual outbreaks of this highly clonal species. Perhaps true outbreaks can only be inferred on the basis of identical or near-identical PFGE profiles, which are most consistent with spread. Even then, a situation could be envisaged in which parallel selection under similar pressures may result in highly similar strains. It is noteworthy that the PFGE profiles of some isolates in this study, such as Israel 28 and RUH 5875, were very similar, although they originated from different countries.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">The group-specific PCRs facilitated rapid identification of the sequence type group or clonal lineage of outbreak strains, without any need for sequencing or other typing techniques, thereby bringing such analysis within the scope of a wide range of users. This approach may prove helpful in identifying the genotypes that are most likely to cause problems in hospitals. In the UK, genotypes belonging to sequence type Group 1 are, by far, the most successful. Investigation of differences in the structure and function of the proteins encoded by the alleles of the three outbreak groups could provide insights into their relative pathogenicity and success.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
