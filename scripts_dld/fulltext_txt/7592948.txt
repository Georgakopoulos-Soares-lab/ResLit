                  Plasmid pJV4, containing a 2.4-kilobase pair insert of genomic DNA from the chloramphenicol (Cm) producer Streptomyces venezuelae ISP5230, confers resistance when introduced by transformation into the Cm-sensitive host Streptomyces lividans M252 (Mosher, R. H. Ranade, N. P., Schrempf, H., and Vining, L. C.(1990) J. Gen. Microbiol. 136, 293-301). Transformants rapidly metabolized Cm to one major product, which was isolated and purified by reversed phase chromatography. The metabolite was identified by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and mass spectrometry as 3′-O-phospho-Cm, and was shown to have negligible inhibitory activity against Cm-sensitive Micrococcus luteus. The nucleotide sequence of the S. venezuelae DNA insert in pJV4 contains an open reading frame (ORF) that encodes a polypeptide (19 kDa) with a consensus motif at its NH2 terminus corresponding to a nucleotide-binding amino acid sequence (motif A or P-loop; Walker, J. E., Saraste, M., Runswick, M. J., and Gay, N. J.(1982) EMBO J. 1, 945-951). When a recombinant vector containing this ORF as a 1.6-kilobase pair SmaI-SmaI fragment was used to transform S. lividans M252, uniformly Cm-resistant transformants were obtained. A strain of S. lividans transformed by a vector in which the ORF had been disrupted by an internal deletion yielded clones that were unable to phosphorylate Cm, and exhibited normal susceptibility to the antibiotic. The results implicate the product of the ORF from S. venezuelae as an enzymic effector of Cm resistance in the producing organism by 3′-O-phosphorylation. We suggest the trivial name chloramphenicol 3′-O-phosphotransferase for the enzyme.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara70" view="all">Plasmid pJV4, containing a 2.4-kilobase pair insert of genomic DNA from the chloramphenicol (Cm) producer <ce:italic>Streptomyces venezuelae</ce:italic> ISP5230, confers resistance when introduced by transformation into the Cm-sensitive host <ce:italic>Streptomyces lividans</ce:italic> M252 (Mosher, R. H. Ranade, N. P., Schrempf, H., and Vining, L. C.(1990) <ce:italic>J. Gen. Microbiol.</ce:italic> 136, 293-301). Transformants rapidly metabolized Cm to one major product, which was isolated and purified by reversed phase chromatography. The metabolite was identified by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and mass spectrometry as 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phospho-Cm, and was shown to have negligible inhibitory activity against Cm-sensitive <ce:italic>Micrococcus luteus</ce:italic>. The nucleotide sequence of the <ce:italic>S. venezuelae</ce:italic> DNA insert in pJV4 contains an open reading frame (ORF) that encodes a polypeptide (19 kDa) with a consensus motif at its NH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> terminus corresponding to a nucleotide-binding amino acid sequence (motif A or P-loop; Walker, J. E., Saraste, M., Runswick, M. J., and Gay, N. J.(1982) <ce:italic>EMBO J.</ce:italic> 1, 945-951). When a recombinant vector containing this ORF as a 1.6-kilobase pair <ce:italic>Sma</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sma</ce:italic>I fragment was used to transform <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252, uniformly Cm-resistant transformants were obtained. A strain of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> transformed by a vector in which the ORF had been disrupted by an internal deletion yielded clones that were unable to phosphorylate Cm, and exhibited normal susceptibility to the antibiotic. The results implicate the product of the ORF from <ce:italic>S. venezuelae</ce:italic> as an enzymic effector of Cm resistance in the producing organism by 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphorylation. We suggest the trivial name chloramphenicol 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphotransferase for the enzyme.</ce:simple-para>

Identification of this metabolite as the 3′-phospho ester of Cm implicates a mechanism of Cm resistance for the producing organism that has not hitherto been encountered in streptomycetes or other microbial systems.</ce:para>

                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para190" view="all">Culture broths and extracts were analyzed for Cm and its derivatives by HPLC on a 4.6 × 50-mm Phenomenex Ultracarb ODS-30 C<ce:inf loc="post">18</ce:inf> column (5-μm particle size).                         <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Isolation of 3′-Phospho-Cm from Suspensions of RM4 Mycelium</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para200" view="all">To investigate Cm metabolism by <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM3 and RM4, mycelium was grown in GNY medium supplemented with Cm at a final concentration of 12.5 μg/ml (38.7 μM). Cultures were routinely inoculated with spore suspensions and incubated at 30°C on a rotary shaker (3.7-cm eccentricity; 250 rpm). Mycelial suspensions for converting Cm to 3′-phospho-Cm were prepared as follows; 250 ml of RM4 cells grown for 48 h were centrifuged (35,000 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 10 min at 4°C), and washed aseptically with water. They were resuspended in 250 ml of water containing Cm (200 μg/ml), and incubated in a 1-liter Erlenmeyer flask for 23 h under the conditions used for growing cultures. The cells were then removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was decanted and stored at -20°C. The supernatant was concentrated to 1 ml at 40°C <ce:italic>in vacuo</ce:italic>, and acidified with 100 μl of 1 M ammonium formate buffer, pH 2.5. The resulting solution, pH 3.4, was clarified by filtration and applied to a C<ce:inf loc="post">18</ce:inf> reversed-phase silica column (1 × 23 cm, 40-μm particle size, Bakerbond) packed in water. After 1 ml of 0.1 M ammonium formate buffer, pH 2.5, had been added, the products were eluted with water. Fractions were monitored by HPLC; those that contained Cm or 3′-phospho-Cm were pooled appropriately and concentrated <ce:italic>in vacuo</ce:italic>. By this procedure 3′-phospho-Cm greater than 99% pure by HPLC and NMR analysis, and free from contamination with Cm, was isolated in milligram quantities.</ce:para>

                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">Isolation of a Novel Cm Metabolite</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para300" view="all">Analysis by HPLC of filtrates from cultures grown in GNY medium with Cm at the highest concentration tolerated (12.5 μg/ml) revealed that <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM3 and RM4 rapidly converted the antibiotic to a single major extracellular product (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>) with an absorption maximum (273 nm) identical to that of Cm. In direct comparisons, the retention time of the Cm product (5.9 min) differed not only from that of Cm (6.3 min) but also from other related reference compounds, including 1′,3′-diacetyl-Cm (6.8 min), Cm-base (the free amine of Cm; 4.9 min), and <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetyl-<ce:italic>p</ce:italic>-nitrophenylserinol (5.5 min).</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para310" view="all">Mycelium grown in a variety of media was active in converting Cm to its major metabolite; activity persisted in mycelium harvested after the end of the growth phase, or resuspended in water. Complete conversion was achieved by adding Cm at the concentration tolerated (12.5 μg/ml) to cultures of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM4 growing in GNY medium. Furthermore, complete conversion was obtained by incubating washed mycelium with 200 μg/ml of Cm in water for 48 h. The use of aqueous cell suspensions simplified isolation of the metabolite, and facilitated its purification by reversed phase chromatography. The major chromatographic product was collected as a single sharp peak, well separated from other substances absorbing at 273 nm, and was evaporated to dryness at 40°C <ce:italic>in vacuo.</ce:italic>
                        </ce:para>

                     <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">Identification of the Cm Metabolite</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para320" view="all">The <ce:sup loc="pre">1</ce:sup>H NMR spectrum of the Cm metabolite was similar to that of Cm in that it contained signals consistent with the presence of a dichloroacetyl group and a 1,4-disubstituted phenylpropanoid derivative. However, it differed in the spin-spin coupling pattern of protons associated with the propanediol moiety. Whereas the <ce:italic>H</ce:italic>-3a′ and <ce:italic>H</ce:italic>-3b′ resonances for Cm were present as quartets due to coupling with each other and with <ce:italic>H</ce:italic>-2′, the resonances in the metabolite showed complex spin-spin coupling, and were displaced downfield with respect to those given by Cm. The data suggested that the propanediol protons of the metabolite were deshielded by an electron-withdrawing functional group probably attached at C-3′.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para330" view="all">The <ce:sup loc="pre">13</ce:sup>C NMR spectrum of the metabolite was similar to that of Cm, with most of the signals slightly downfield of corresponding Cm signals. However, the signal assigned to C-3′ showed a larger shift, from 62.2 to 66.6 ppm, consistent with the presence of an electron-withdrawing substituent on this carbon. That the latter is an orthophosphate group is supported by the <ce:sup loc="pre">31</ce:sup>P NMR spectrum of the product. The chemical shift (<ce:sup loc="pre">31</ce:sup>P, d = 6.474 ppm) of the single strong signal was in the region of the spectrum predicted for an organophosphate ester (data not shown). Examination of the sample by low resolution, negative ion-spray mass spectrometry gave a group of molecular ions in the relative proportions expected for a substance containing two chlorine atoms (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>). The deduced <ce:italic>M</ce:italic>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> of the compound is 401.97, the value predicted for a monophosphate ester of chloramphenicol. From the combined evidence, the most probable structure of the metabolite is, therefore, 3′-phospho-Cm.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para340" view="all">To determine whether 3′-phospho-Cm exhibited antibiotic activity, the purified compound was compared with Cm in a disk-diffusion assay using <ce:italic>M. luteus</ce:italic> as the test organism. Inhibition zones were observed only with relatively large samples of 3′-phospho-Cm (&gt;350 μg/disk), whereas 0.15 μg of Cm gave a measurable zone; in a standard assay, the apparent specific activity of 3′-phospho-Cm was 0.04% that of Cm. We conclude from this result that phosphorylation of the 3′-OH position of Cm reduces its antibiotic activity significantly.</ce:para>

                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle170">Sequence and Analysis of the pJV4 Insert</ce:section-title>

                     <ce:section id="cesec200" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle180">Comparison of Derived Amino Acid (aa) Sequences with Data Base Sequences ORF1</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para360" view="all">There are two possible ATG start sites for ORF1 (nt 28-30 and nt 100-102; see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>); the first gives a polypeptide 25 amino acids longer than the second. A FASTA sequence alignment (Pearson and Lipman, 1988) comparing ORF1 with sequences in the GenBank and EMBL data bases showed ORF1 to be 42.2% identical over 386 amino acids to CMR, the Cm resistance protein of <ce:italic>Rhodococcus fasciens</ce:italic> (Desomer <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1992), and 39.8% identical over 372 amino acids to CmlG of <ce:italic>S</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>lividans</ce:italic> (Dittrich <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1991). The amino termini of CMR and CmlG align optimally with Met-25 of ORF1 rather than with Met-1, and since AraJ (Reeder and Schleif, 1991) and NorA (Yoshida <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990), two other hydrophobic proteins with sequence similarity to ORF1, also align at their amino termini with Met-25, the translational start codon for ORF1 is likely to be the ATG at nt 100-102. This being so, then ORF1 is a polypeptide of 412 amino acids (<ce:italic>M</ce:italic>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> 41,479).</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para370" view="all">ORF1 shows greater than 60% sequence similarity over 300 amino acids to a number of antibiotic resistance proteins presumed to be integral membrane components and capable of promoting active and specific antibiotic efflux (Levy, 1992). Examples include the quinolone resistance determinant (NorA) of <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> noted above, the methylenomycin resistance protein (Mmr) of the methylenomycin producer <ce:italic>Streptomyces coelicolor</ce:italic> (Neal and Chater, 1987), and the tetracycline resistance protein (TetL) of <ce:italic>Bacillus stearothermophilus</ce:italic> (McMurry <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1987). All such proteins probably contain 12-14 membrane-spanning α-helical segments that form a transmembrane channel (Paulsen and Skurray, 1993).</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec210" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle190">ORF2</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para380" view="all">Translation of ORF2 begins at either of two possible start codons: GTG at nt 1412-1414 or ATG at nt 1424-1426. The sequence 5′-GGTGA-3′, showing complementarity to the 3′ end of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> 16 S rRNA (Δ<ce:italic>G</ce:italic> = -11.6 kcal: Tinoco <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1973; Bibb and Cohen, 1982) is present 4 bp (nt 1403-1407) upstream of the GTG codon, and also 8 bp (nt 1411-1415) upstream of the ATG codon. The 4-bp distance is below the range (5-12 bp) commonly observed for the separation between a ribosome-binding site and the translational start point in streptomycetes (Strohl, 1992). Beginning 26 bp upstream of the GTG codon, the sequence 5′-CACCGT-3′ (nt 1381-1386) represents a possible -10 hexamer recognized by Es<ce:sup loc="post">70</ce:sup>-like RNA polymerase (Strohl, 1992). However, the region upstream of the GTG codon contains a second such sequence (5′-TACGGT-3′; nt 1400-1405). Transcription from this hexamer should initiate at the first nucleotide of the GTG codon. Such a concurrence of translational and transcriptional start sites is not uncommon in streptomycetes (Strohl, 1992).</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para390" view="all">Initiation of translation at the GTG codon would yield a polypeptide of 178 amino acids (<ce:italic>M</ce:italic>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> 18,804), whereas a polypeptide initiated at the ATG codon would contain only 174 amino acids (<ce:italic>M</ce:italic>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> 18,315). Although a FASTA comparison of the amino acid sequence with the GenBank and EMBL data bases revealed no significant similarities, use of the alignment program MPsrch (Sturrock and Collins, 1993) showed resemblance between the NH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-terminal region of the ORF2 product and predicted nucleotide-binding sites in such ATP-requiring proteins as pantothenate kinase of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (Song and Jackowski, 1992) and GlnQ of <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>stearothermophilus</ce:italic> (Wu and Welker, 1991). Examination of ORF2 using the Motifs program (Genetics Computer Group Inc., version 7.3), showed the presence of the “P-loop” phosphate-binding motif found in many classes of adenine and guanine nucleotide-binding proteins (Saraste <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990). The ORF2 sequence GGSSAGKS (aa 10-17; see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>) fits the P-loop motif consensus (A/G)<ce:italic>XXXX</ce:italic>GK(S/T) for an ATP/GTP binding site (Walker <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1982; Saraste <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990), and implicates ORF2 in a process such as phosphoryl transfer. Also present in the ORF2 polypeptide is the sequence DADG (aa 57-60; see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>), which corresponds to a proposed consensus element (D<ce:italic>XX</ce:italic>G) for GTP/GDP-binding sites (Dever <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1987). The linear separation between the aspartic acid of D<ce:italic>XX</ce:italic>G, which interacts with nucleotide-bound Mg<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup>, and the P-loop lysine, thought to interact with the β- and g-phosphates of the bound nucleotide, conforms to the observed separation (40-80 residues) between these amino acids (Saraste <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990; Mimura <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1991).</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec220" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle200">ORF3</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para400" view="all">Translation of ORF3 from the possible GTG start codon (nt 2266-2264; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>) would generate a polypeptide of 89 amino acids with a deduced <ce:italic>M</ce:italic>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> of 9,767. A comparison of the amino acid sequence with those in GenBank and EMBL data bases revealed no significant similarities.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section id="cesec230" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle210">Identification of the Cm Resistance Determinant in the 2.4-kbp Insert</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para410" view="all">The striking sequence similarity between ORF1 and gene products associated with antibiotic resistance in other bacteria suggested a role for ORF1 in the Cm resistance of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM4. However, the genes for CMR and CmlG confer resistance to concentrations of Cm greater than 200 μg/ml on their hosts (Dittrich <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1991; Desomer <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1992), whereas the maximal resistance to Cm conferred upon <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252 by pJV4 is 12.5 μg/ml. To determine if Cm resistance is affected by the absence of ORF2 and ORF3, a 1.4-kbp <ce:italic>Acc</ce:italic>651-<ce:italic>Bst</ce:italic>EII fragment containing only ORF1 was excised from pJV7 (<ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> vector pTZ18R carrying the 2.4-kbp <ce:italic>Kpn</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I insert from pJV4) and subcloned in the residual pJV4 vector segment obtained by digesting pJV4 with <ce:italic>Acc</ce:italic>65I and <ce:italic>Eco</ce:italic>ICRI to remove the 2.4-kbp insert. The presence of the 1.4-kbp insert in the resulting plasmid (pDC-ORF1; see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="fig6"/>) was verified by restriction enzyme analysis. Transformation of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252 with pDC-ORF1 gave Cm-sensitive colonies, a result indicating that ORF1 by itself did not confer the Cm resistance phenotype of pJV4 transformants.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para420" view="all">Evidence that resistance depended on the presence of an intact ORF2 in pJV4 was obtained by deleting a segment of DNA between two <ce:italic>Sal</ce:italic>I sites within ORF2. The deletion plasmid was constructed in the <ce:italic>Streptomyces-E. coli</ce:italic> shuttle vector pHJL400. Transformation of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252 with the vector carrying the 2.4-kbp <ce:italic>S. venezuelae</ce:italic> DNA insert from which the <ce:italic>Sal</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sal</ce:italic>I fragment had been deleted yielded Cm-sensitive colonies, in contrast to the Cm-resistant transformants obtained with a control plasmid in which ORF2 was intact. Transformants with the deletion in ORF2 also differed from those with ORF2 intact in that they failed to convert Cm to 3′-phospho-CM.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec240" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle220">Subcloning and Expression of ORF2 in S. lividans</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para430" view="all">To determine whether ORF2 alone conferred Cm resistance in <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM4, the 1.67-kbp <ce:italic>Sma</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sma</ce:italic>I segment, lacking large segments of both ORF1 and ORF3 (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>), was excised from pJV7 and cloned in the <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> vector pUC19. The resultant plasmid (pDC1.6) was linearized with <ce:italic>Hin</ce:italic>dIII, and ligated to the <ce:italic>Streptomyces</ce:italic> vector pIJ6017 carrying the thiostrepton-inducible promoter <ce:italic>tip</ce:italic> (Murakami <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1989). <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="FN4">
                              <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>
                           </ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:footnote id="FN4">
                              <ce:label>
                                 <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>
                              </ce:label>
                              <ce:note-para id="cenpara30" view="all">M. J. Bibb, personal communication.</ce:note-para>
                           </ce:footnote> The <ce:italic>E. coli-Streptomyces</ce:italic> shuttle vector (pSV1.6) so formed was used to transform <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252, and colonies were selected for Cm resistance. In bioassays these resistant transformants were inhibited by Cm concentrations above 10 μg/ml, compared with 12.5 μg/ml for RM4. When cultures were grown in GNY medium containing Cm, HPLC analysis of the culture supernatant showed that the antibiotic was converted to 3′-phospho-Cm; however, concentrations in the supernatant were only 20% of those in RM4 cultures. We were unable to measure 3′-phospho-Cm concentrations in the cells from these cultures. Since Cm resistance was observed without induction of the <ce:italic>tip</ce:italic> promoter, the presence of a functioning promoter upstream of ORF2 seems likely. Overall, the results indicate that phosphorylation by the ORF2 product is mainly responsible for the Cm resistance phenotype of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM4, but the ORF1 product may also have a role.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec250" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle230">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para440" view="all">In a previous report (Mosher <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990), the presence of small quantities of metabolites derived from Cm-base in cultures of the Cm-resistant <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> strain RM3 supplemented with Cm was offered as evidence that a chloramphenicol amide hydrolase mediated Cm resistance in <ce:italic>S. venezuelae.</ce:italic> In the present study, a more exhaustive analysis of the culture broths of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM3 and RM4 revealed a hitherto unidentified polar metabolite as the major product of Cm metabolism. Enough of the metabolite was obtained by incubating Cm with resting RM4 cells to allow its isolation, purification by C<ce:inf loc="post">18</ce:inf> reverse-phase column chromatography, and identification as the 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphoryl ester of Cm. That ORF2 has a role in the Cm resistance conferred by pJV4 was established by the concurrent loss of Cm kinase activity and resistance when an internal segment was deleted. We conclude that ORF2 encodes the enzyme that catalyses the phosphorylation of Cm, for which we have proposed the trivial name chloramphenicol 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphotransferase.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para450" view="all">Much early work on the relationship between the structure of Cm and its antibiotic activity (summarized by Pongs, 1979) emphasized the importance of an unmodified primary alcohol at C-3′. Consistent with this, bacterial resistance to Cm is commonly mediated by enzymic acetylation of the C-3′ hydroxyl group (Shaw and Leslie, 1991). The discovery that a Cm-producing streptomycete modifies the same functional group by an alternative mechanism suggests that this transformation is responsible for self-resistance. Phosphorylation is widely used to confer resistance to antibiotics, both in producing and in susceptible nonproducing organisms (Cundliffe, 1992). Nonetheless, in the more than four decades during which Cm has been used to treat infections in humans and animals, phosphorylation has not hitherto been reported as a mechanism for inactivating this antibiotic. Its presence in a Cm-producing streptomycete implies that, here at least, the origin of a resistance mechanism cannot easily be attributed to horizontal transfer of a gene conferring protection on an antibiotic producer.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para460" view="all">Although the deduced primary structure of chloramphenicol 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphotransferase does not show end-to-end similarity to proteins in current data bases, it does contain localized regions of sequence similarity to a number of proteins requiring nucleotide cofactors. The presence near the NH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> terminus of a consensus P-loop sequence similar to known ATP/GTP-binding sites is compatible with the role of chloramphenicol 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphotransferase in phosphoryl transfer. The P-loop motif occurs in a wide variety of proteins that, although diverse in biochemical function, have both a common nucleotide binding sequence and similar structures (Saraste <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990; Schulz, 1992). Among these are the human Ha-<ce:italic>ras</ce:italic> p21 protein, <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> adenylate kinase, EF-Tu and dethiobiotin synthetase (Jurnak, 1985; Pai <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1989, 1990; Muller and Schulz, 1992; Huang <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1994; Alexeev <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1994). The ATP-binding site is also present in the transport protein superfamily that includes the cystic fibrosis gene product (Hyde <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1990; Mimura <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1991). In many proteins of this class the polypeptide is folded into a barrel of parallel β-sheets surrounded by α-helices to give a “core structure” (Milner-White <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>., 1991). The glycine-rich region of the loop contributes to forming a giant anion hole accommodating the nucleotide triphosphate oxygen atoms.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para470" view="all">Although three intact ORFs are present in the 2.4-kbp <ce:italic>Kpn</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I DNA fragment cloned in pJV4 from <ce:italic>S. venezuelae</ce:italic>, large segments of ORF1 and ORF3 can be deleted with only a 10-20% loss of the Cm resistance phenotype conferred on <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> M252 transformants by the plasmid. Cm resistance and the ability to phosphorylate Cm are retained only when ORF2 remains intact; selective disruption of ORF2 eliminates both capabilities. The conclusion that <ce:italic>cpt</ce:italic> is involved in the resistance conferred by pJV4 is consistent with chemical characterization of the Cm inactivation product as 3′-phospho-Cm, and sequence analysis of the deduced gene product indicated that the primary structure contains a nucleotide-binding motif. Although ORF1 contributes to the Cm resistance of <ce:italic>S. lividans</ce:italic> RM4, it does not have a predominant role. This is unexpected given its similarity to proteins associated with resistance to greater than 200 μg/ml Cm in other bacteria. Its lack of activity in pJV4 could be attributed to the absence of upstream regulatory sequences, but the comparable Cm resistance conferred by pJV3, in which 4.1 kbp of upstream sequence from <ce:italic>S. venezuelae</ce:italic> remains intact, renders this explanation unsatisfactory. A possible alternative is that ORF1 encodes an efflux protein for 3-phospho-Cm, rather than for Cm itself, and that it functions in the presence of chloramphenicol 3′-<ce:italic>O</ce:italic>-phosphotransferase to facilitate the export of inactivated antibiotic.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
