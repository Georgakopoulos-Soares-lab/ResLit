                  Cleavage of phosphatidylinositol (PI) to inositol 1,2-(cyclic)-phosphate (cIP) and cIP hydrolysis to inositol 1-phosphate by Bacillus thuringiensis phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C are activated by the enzyme binding to phosphatidylcholine (PC) surfaces. Part of this reflects improved binding of the protein to interfaces. However, crystallographic analysis of an interfacially impaired phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase (W47A/W242A) suggested protein dimerization might occur on the membrane. In the W47A/W242A dimer, four tyrosine residues from one monomer interact with the same tyrosine cluster of the other, forming a tight dimer interface close to the membrane binding regions. We have constructed mutant proteins in which two or more of these tyrosine residues have been replaced with serine. Phospholipid binding and enzymatic activity of these mutants have been examined to assess the importance of these residues to enzyme function. Replacing two tyrosines had small effects on enzyme activity. However, removal of three or four tyrosine residues weakened PC binding and reduced PI cleavage by the enzyme as well as PC activation of cIP hydrolysis. Crystal structures of Y247S/Y251S in the absence and presence of myo-inositol as well as Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S indicate that both mutant proteins crystallized as monomers, were very similar to one another, and had no change in the active site region. Kinetic assays, lipid binding, and structural results indicate that either (i) a specific PC binding site, critical for vesicle activities and cIP activation, has been impaired, or (ii) the reduced dimerization potential for Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S is responsible for their reduced catalytic activity in all assay systems.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara170" view="all">Cleavage of phosphatidylinositol (PI) to inositol 1,2-(cyclic)-phosphate (cIP) and cIP hydrolysis to inositol 1-phosphate by <ce:italic>Bacillus thuringiensis</ce:italic> phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C are activated by the enzyme binding to phosphatidylcholine (PC) surfaces. Part of this reflects improved binding of the protein to interfaces. However, crystallographic analysis of an interfacially impaired phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase (W47A/W242A) suggested protein dimerization might occur on the membrane. In the W47A/W242A dimer, four tyrosine residues from one monomer interact with the same tyrosine cluster of the other, forming a tight dimer interface close to the membrane binding regions. We have constructed mutant proteins in which two or more of these tyrosine residues have been replaced with serine. Phospholipid binding and enzymatic activity of these mutants have been examined to assess the importance of these residues to enzyme function. Replacing two tyrosines had small effects on enzyme activity. However, removal of three or four tyrosine residues weakened PC binding and reduced PI cleavage by the enzyme as well as PC activation of cIP hydrolysis. Crystal structures of Y247S/Y251S in the absence and presence of <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol as well as Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S indicate that both mutant proteins crystallized as monomers, were very similar to one another, and had no change in the active site region. Kinetic assays, lipid binding, and structural results indicate that either (i) a specific PC binding site, critical for vesicle activities and cIP activation, has been impaired, or (ii) the reduced dimerization potential for Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S is responsible for their reduced catalytic activity in all assay systems.</ce:simple-para>

Fluorescence quenching experiments were consistent with dimer formation of pyrene-labeled Cys mutants placed in the vicinity of the dimer interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). However, the hydrophobic probes introduced could drive formation of a dimer that does not occur with wild type protein and is not directly relevant to membrane binding and catalysis.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">To further explore the relevance of this surface region of the protein to PI-PLC activity, we have constructed a series of mutants converting the tyrosine residues in the W47A/W242A dimer interface to serines (Y247S/Y251S, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S, and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S). As more tyrosines are replaced by serine, there is a decrease in catalytic activity, both phosphotransferase cleavage of PI to cIP and diacylglycerol (in a variety of assay systems) and cyclic phosphodiesterase hydrolysis of cIP to I-1-P. There is an impairment of protein binding to PC vesicles when two or more tyrosine residues are removed that correlates with reduced specific activity in assays with PI/PC vesicles. Binding of the mutant proteins to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles has also been examined (intrinsic fluorescence and <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P NMR methods) and provides evidence that this interaction is very different when three or four tyrosines have been replaced by serine. Crystal structures of two of the mutant enzymes, Y247S/Y251S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S, have been solved, and these show that the proteins are very similar to the very closely related <ce:italic>Bacillus cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>) in that they fail to crystallize as dimers (presumably because helix B and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> prevent close approach of two monomers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>)). Their active sites also superimpose well on that of the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC. The only difference is in the loss of Tyr side chains in the area that forms the crystallographic dimer interface in W47A/W242A. These results can be interpreted as evidence for either (i) surface-induced dimerization of the PI-PLC or (ii) identification of a very specific PC binding site on the protein.</ce:para>

                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">High Resolution <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P Field Cycling</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Field-cycling <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P T<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> experiments were run at 22 °C on an unmodified Varian Unity<ce:sup loc="post">plus</ce:sup> 500 spectrometer at Brandeis University using a standard 10-mm Varian probe in a custom-built device which can move the sample, sealed in a standard 10-mm tube, from the sample probe up to or from a higher position within or just above the magnet, where the magnetic field is between 0.06 and 11.7 tesla (T) or can be varied with the aid of a small Helmholtz coil atop the magnet down to 0.002 T. The method and shuttling device are essentially as described previously, although improved by use of a stepper motor-based shuttle and many other new features (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>). Relaxation rates at each field strength were measured using 6–8 programmed delay times. Data were analyzed by curve-fitting to equations as described earlier (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs220" refid="bib15 bib16 bib17">15–17</ce:cross-refs>). The results are parameterized quite well with only six constants. These are based on a standard “model-free” theory (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>), which is not fully appropriate to membranes. The parameters are nevertheless useful as indicating general time scales of motion and structural preferences. Four of the parameters are derived from the high-field part of the measurement, above 0.08 T. These are: τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>, which is the time scale (typically ∼5 ns) associated with a restricted, rotational diffusion process motion of an individual phospholipid; C<ce:inf loc="post">H</ce:inf>, which reports the contribution of chemical shift anisotropy (CSA) relaxation produced by sub-nanosecond motions within the lipid; C<ce:inf loc="post">L</ce:inf>, which is proportional to CSA relaxation produced by the part of the CSA relaxation remaining un-averaged by the above sub-nanosecond motions and resulting from the slower internal motions fluctuating with time scale τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>; R<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>(0), which is the dipolar-relaxation contribution resulting from <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P interacting with its nearest protons, mediated by the internal time-scale τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>. Theory and other knowledge (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>) then allow us to estimate three other parameters from these, namely τ<ce:inf loc="post">hf</ce:inf>, which is the time scale of the highest frequency motions mentioned above, S<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>
                              <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>, the square of the chemical shift anisotropy order parameter, and r<ce:inf loc="post">eff</ce:inf>, which indicates the general size of the distance between the phosphorus and its nearest proton neighbors that relax it.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para110" view="all">There is also a distinct new dispersion observed by extending measurements to very low fields (&lt;0.03 T) that is easily defined on top of a base line of magnitude R<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>(0) that is expected to be flat at these low fields. This base line can, thus, be subtracted to produce a pure dispersion. This dispersion is parameterized using Solomon theory with a correlation time τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>, and an extrapolated rate in the zero field limit (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). We treat τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> as equal to the rotational correlation time D<ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf>/6, where D<ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> is the rotational diffusion constant of the individual lipid axes due to both Einstein-Debye diffusion of the entire phospholipid aggregate in the absence or presence of PI-PLC and translational diffusion of individual lipids in the aggregate. The area under this dispersion can be related to the spatial probability-density distributions of the closest protons to the phosphorus, averaged over a time long compared with the time scale of internal rotation τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>. Because the detailed spatial distribution is unknown, we have used a rigid rotator model due to Woessner (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>), with the axis assumed perpendicular to the aggregate surface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). The R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> rate is then predicted to be proportional to (1–3 cos<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf>), where θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf> is the angle between the director and the P-to-H vector. Only one of these four angles is reported here for PC micelles, as a comparison of data for dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine vesicles with molecular dynamics simulations suggest the θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf> ∼ 45° is likely to be the correct value for PC species (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>

                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Overexpression of Tyr Ridge Mutants; Secondary Structure and Thermostability</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Four tyrosines in W47A/W242A, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup>, form the hydrophobic core of the dimer interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). In rPI-PLC protein, their replacement with serine residues should dramatically weaken any membrane-induced dimerization, assuming the dimer is similar to W47A/W242A, but maintain any hydrogen-bonding interactions with solvent or phospholipid head groups. If, instead of direct dimer formation, these residues are involved in specific binding of PC molecules (possibly via a Tyr π-cation interaction with the choline moiety as has been observed in peptide systems (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>)), both activity and PC binding might also be affected.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para150" view="all">The mutated PI-PLC proteins Y247S/Y251S, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S, and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S were examined by CD for changes in secondary structure. Y246S/Y247S/Y248S exhibited a far-UV spectrum equivalent to rPI-PLC. However, both Y247S/Y251S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S appeared to have less α-helix and more β-sheet (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>) at low concentrations in 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> borate buffer. Upon the addition of <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol (a soluble portion of the substrate molecule that binds, although not with terribly high affinity, to the active site) a CD spectrum identical to rPI-PLC was observed for Y247S/Y251S (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). The α-helix content for the quadruple Tyr mutant, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S, dispersed in this solution was not recovered with added <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol. For comparison, <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol had no effect on the secondary structure of rPI-PLC or the triple tyrosine mutant. This suggested that occupation of the active site with the polar part of the substrate can affect the structure of helical regions in the protein and that the double tyrosine mutant likely resembles rPI-PLC under those conditions. The presence of 30% isopropyl alcohol has also been shown to activate rPI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). This co-solvent has little effect on rPI-PLC secondary structure (if measured at 4 °C) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>); however, it did lead to recovery of α-helix for the quadruple mutant (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>inset</ce:italic>). To the extent that a co-solvent system may mimic an interfacial environment of a membrane, these results indicate that conditions that lead to kinetic activation (<ce:italic>e.g</ce:italic>. isopropanol) tend to lead to changes in Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S secondary structure that approximate rPI-PLC.</ce:para>

                        <ce:section id="cesec170" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Specific Activities of Tyr Mutants</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para160" view="all">PI-PLC enzymes catalyze the hydrolysis of PI to I-1-P in two steps (cleavage of PI to water-soluble cIP, then hydrolysis of cIP to I-1-P) that can be analyzed separately. Y247S/Y251S exhibited specific activity toward PI solubilized in diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC (1:4 PI/diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>), comparable with wild type enzyme. Changing the detergent matrix to Triton X-100 instead of an activating PC molecule leads to reduced specific activity for rPI-PLC, typically 30–40% (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). However, Y247S/Y251S phosphotransferase activity with PI/Triton X-100 micelles was noticeably lower than that of rPI-PLC. In comparison to Y247S/Y251S, the other tyrosine mutants had even lower specific activity toward PI whether solubilized in diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC or Triton X-100 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). A similar trend was observed for cleavage of PI/PC (4:1) vesicles; Y247S/Y251S was closest to rPI-PLC in cleavage ability, whereas the mutants with three or four of the tyrosines removed were much less active.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para170" view="all">In the absence of an activating interface, the second step of the PI-PLC reaction, hydrolysis of cIP to I-1-P, was greatly reduced for all the tyrosine mutants compared with rPI-PLC. In the presence of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles, the specific activity of Y247S/Y251S increased dramatically, within a factor of two of the wild type enzyme-specific activity (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). However, as more tyrosine residues were replaced by serine, the mutant PI-PLC enzymes exhibited a much smaller activation by diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC to the point where PC activation was virtually abolished for Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S. The larger effect on the phosphodiesterase activity compared with the phosphotransferase activity is better seen when the specific activity for each mutant enzyme is normalized to that of rPI-PLC for that assay system (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). Hydrolysis of cIP in the presence of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC is reduced to a much greater extent for Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S compared with decreases in any of the PI cleavage assays.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para180" view="all">A striking kinetic feature of this bacterial PI-PLC is the lack of surface dilution inhibition until the mole fraction PI is reduced below 0.1 at fixed PI (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). Because removal of surface Tyr residues might alter this, we monitored the specific activity of the Tyr to Ser mutant enzymes in experiments where the bulk concentration of PI was held constant at 6 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> and the amount of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC increased (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>). Y247S/Y251S, like rPI-PLC, has a moderate affinity for PI as 50% inhibition was not observed until a diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC/PI ratio of 10 (mole fraction of 0.091 PI). In contrast, the other two tyrosine mutants started at lower specific activities and showed a continuously decreasing activity (referenced to the value for 6 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> PI/18 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC), as the diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC concentration was increased. Clearly, removal of three or four of the surface tyrosines that make up the core of the dimer interface in W47A/W242A reduces the activities of both steps of the reaction. The enhanced surface dilution suggests that removal of these Tyr reduces the affinity of the protein for interfacial substrate.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Binding of Tyr Mutant Proteins to Phospholipid SUVs and diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC Micelles</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para190" view="all">The lower activities as tyrosine residues were replaced with serine could reflect a progressively weaker binding of PI-PLC to phospholipid interfaces. Previous work has shown that <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC binds to PC vesicles with a large hydrophobic component, whereas it binds to pure anionic phospholipid vesicles through electrostatic interactions (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>). Y247S/Y251S exhibited a 2-fold weaker affinity for PC vesicles than rPI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). Y246S/Y247S/Y248S presented an interesting case in that partitioning of the protein onto the vesicles very much depended on the protein concentration. At moderate protein concentrations (0.01–0.1 mg/ml, 0.29–2.9 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>), the protein bound to PC vesicles with an apparent <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">D</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic> of 0.5 ± 0.2 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> as measured by the centrifugation/filtration assay. When all four tyrosine residues were replaced by serine, partitioning of the enzyme on PC vesicles was considerably weaker (apparent <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">D</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic> ∼ 1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> with the centrifugation/filtration assay). Although this binding assay is not very accurate for proteins with weak affinities for PC SUVs, it does stress that removal of these Tyr residues on the surface in a region that is below the barrel rim and not near the active site is clearly correlated with the lower activities in assay systems with PI/PC. However, specific activities progressively decreased for assay systems with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles as well so that interaction of these micelles with the proteins must be evaluated.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para200" view="all">The intrinsic fluorescence of PI-PLC is sensitive to surface binding with the intensity increasing when PLC binds to activating PC interfaces (diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC or POPC SUVs) and decreasing when molecules bind at the active site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>). This is a particularly useful method for characterizing PI-PLC binding to short-chain phospholipid micelles (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>). For rPI-PLC, the fluorescence intensity increases above the critical micelle concentration of the short-chain PC, and the steepness of the increase reflects the apparent binding constant of the enzyme to micellar phospholipids. Comparison of fluorescence changes for Y247S/Y215S binding to rPI-PLC protein showed that the double Tyr mutant protein had a weaker interaction with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles than rPI-PLC, consistent with vesicle partitioning assays (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig6"/>). Assuming the critical micelle concentration of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC in the presence of the protein is comparable with the 1.5 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> value for pure diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>), we would estimate an apparent <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">D</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic> of 1.1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> “micellar” PC for Y247S/Y251S compared with rPI-PLC, for which the <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">D</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic> is 0.5 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> micellar diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC. With Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S, the fluorescence intensity did not increase with increasing micellar diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC; rather, it decreased (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>). In rPI-PLC, Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> has been identified as the major contributor to the increased intrinsic fluorescence upon PC binding (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>). Y246S/Y247S/Y248S clearly binds to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, as there is a change in protein intrinsic fluorescence. The decrease in intensity as this mutant protein binds to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles must reflect a different conformational change upon diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC binding. Interestingly, the decreased fluorescence of Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S was comparable in magnitude to that for diC<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>PA binding to rPI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>). That anionic phospholipid binds competitively with PI at the active site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>). Indeed, if the quadruple Tyr mutant protein is titrated with diC<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>PA, a change comparable with rPI-PLC was obtained. These intrinsic fluorescence changes indicate the Y246S/Y247S/Y248S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S proteins bind diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC but not in the same way as rPI-PLC and Y247S/Y251S. The differences correlate with the lower activities of the three and four Tyr-to-Ser enzymes.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para210" view="all">Another way of assessing PI-PLC binding to phospholipid micelles is to examine the <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P line width of the short-chain phospholipids, in this case diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, in the presence of the enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). The addition of enzyme to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC causes a large increase in the <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P line width above the critical micelle concentration of the lipid. This increase is due in part to formation of large particles in this system (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>), although an intermediate exchange rate could also contribute to the large line width. The very active Y247S/Y251S enzyme also broadened the diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P resonance, almost to the extent of rPI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="fig7">Fig. 7</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig7"/>). However, the addition of the triple mutant, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S, which binds to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, as monitored by the intrinsic fluorescence experiments, did not lead to large increases in PC line width; only small increases were seen. This reinforces that there is a pronounced difference in the way these two mutant proteins interact with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC interfaces.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para220" view="all">This difference in how rPI-PLC and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S interact with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC was further explored using high resolution <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P NMR field cycling (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig8"/>). Such a rod-shaped micelle would have a transverse radius of 18 Å and a length ∼80 Å. The <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>, the spin-lattice relaxation rate (1/T<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>), for 3 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC in the absence of protein (<ce:italic>open circles</ce:italic> in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>) was ∼ 0.9 s<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup> at 11.7 T. R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> then decreased with a decreasing field to a minimum around 2 T and increased again as the field decreased below 2 T. The high field behavior reflects relaxation by chemical shift anisotropy (here R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> is proportional to the square of the field) with two characteristic correlation times; that is, a fast motion at 150–200 ps and a slower one (τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>) of 5.8 ± 1.7 ns.                               <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>) was considerably smaller than obtained for PC in SUVs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>), consistent with the more disordered structure of micelles. As the field was decreased below 2 T, the increasing R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> found in all phospholipid aggregates indicates that dipolar relaxation of the 5–10-ns slower motion has now become the dominant relaxation mechanism. Extrapolation of R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> to zero field in this field region provides the parameter R<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>(0), which reflects both τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>, a correlation time for effective rotation/wobbling of the head group, and r<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf>, the effective P-H distance related to an 〈<ce:italic>r</ce:italic>
                              <ce:sup loc="post">−</ce:sup>
                              <ce:sup loc="post">6</ce:sup>〉<ce:sup loc="post">6</ce:sup> average of distances r from P to H (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>). Decreasing the field below 0.06 T (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>) led to a substantial increase in the dipolar relaxation rate for diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC. From the shape of this very low field curve and knowing R<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>(0) from the analysis of the R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> field dependence in the 0.08 to 11.74 T region, we can extract a correlation time, τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>, that has components from both particle rotation and exchange (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). For this concentration of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, the τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> was 0.36 ± 0.08 μs. We can obtain a rough estimate of the average particle size by relating τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> to an isotropic rotational diffusion constant (τ<ce:inf loc="post">r</ce:inf> = 1/(6D<ce:italic>

                              </ce:italic>) and D<ce:italic>
                                 <ce:inf loc="post">o</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic> = <ce:italic>kT</ce:italic>/(8 πη<ce:italic>r</ce:italic>
                              <ce:sup loc="post">3</ce:sup>)) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>). The extracted <ce:italic>r</ce:italic> for the diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles would then be 70 Å. Integration of the very low field dipolar contribution compared with the integration for dipolar relaxation over the entire field range examined reflects an averaged angle θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf> for the P to glycerol CH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> with respect to the field (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). For the pure diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles at this concentration, the average θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf> was 45.6°. DiC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC was previous examined at 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>, and the averaged angle was the same (45.4°) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para230" view="all">In the presence of 3 mg/ml or 0.086 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> rPI-PLC (<ce:italic>filled circles</ce:italic> in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1010" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>

                              <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1020" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). In the very low field region the τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> correlation time for diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC in the presence of rPI-PLC was 0.22 ± 0.08 μs. The decrease in τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> corresponds to a slightly smaller average radius for the particle (60 Å) compared with pure diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles. However, the R<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>(0) value was more than 2-fold higher (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1030" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>

The increased R<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>(0) with a slightly lower τ<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf> is consistent with a different average orientation of the POCH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> in the rPI-PLC·diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC complex (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1040" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). The averaged θ<ce:inf loc="post">PH</ce:inf> now corresponded to 37.8°. This is a very significant change in the average orientation of the P-to-glycerol C(3)-H vector in the particle formed with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC and rPI-PLC.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para240" view="all">In contrast to rPI-PLC, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S had a very different effect on diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC parameters (<ce:italic>open squares</ce:italic> in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1050" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>) at the same ratio of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC/protein (35:1). The τ<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf> decreased significantly to 1.2 ns, R<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>(0) was also much smaller than with rPI-PLC, and the extrapolated fast correlation time was also significantly shorter (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1060" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). The order parameter S<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>
                              <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> was smaller than that observed with rPI-PLC but greater than the value for pure diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC at this concentration. Most interestingly, at very low fields there was only a small increase in R<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>, indicating that the diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC now forms small aggregates with the tyrosine mutant enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1070" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>). These may resemble the detergent-solubilized integral membrane proteins now used for NMR structural studies in that the micelle contribution to particle size is quite small. The relaxation profile for diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC with the mutant Y246S/Y247S/Y248S PI-PLC enzyme is much more like that for diC<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>PC micelles (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1080" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>), which form small nearly spherical aggregates of 19 ± 1 molecules. This cannot be the result of slow exchange between protein-bound diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC and free diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, as going to lower and lower fields would shift such an equilibrium to the fast exchange regime. All these different binding studies indicate that as these surface Tyr are replaced by Ser, the interaction with PC molecules is significantly altered.</ce:para>

                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">Structures of Y247S/Y251S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/ Y251S</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para250" view="all">To explore if removal of these surface tyrosine residues alters the PI-PLC active site, we determined the crystal structure of two of the tyrosine mutants, Y247S/Y251S (PDB entry <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs100" xlink:href="pdb:3EA1" xlink:type="simple">3EA1</ce:inter-ref>) and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S (PDB entry <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs110" xlink:href="pdb:3EA3" xlink:type="simple">3EA3</ce:inter-ref>). Crystallographic data and refinement statistics are presented in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1090" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>. The Y247S/Y251S mutant crystallized in the space group of <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> with two monomers in the asymmetric unit. The PI-PLC Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S mutant protein crystallized in space group P1; its asymmetric unit also contained two monomers. Both tyrosine-to-serine mutant structures are of high quality at 1.75 and 1.78 Å resolution (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1100" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>), and the individual monomers in both mutant structures closely resemble the PI-PLC structure from <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> (PDB entry <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs120" xlink:href="pdb:1PTD" xlink:type="simple">1PTD</ce:inter-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1110" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>). The proteins fold as an incomplete (βα)<ce:inf loc="post">8</ce:inf> barrel (TIM-barrel) with an inner layer composed of eight β-strands surrounded by six major α-helices of the outer layer. As was the case with the structure of W47A/W242A (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1120" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>), the last two residues of PI-PLC, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">297</ce:sup> and Glu<ce:sup loc="post">298</ce:sup>, did not have observable electron density. The main-chain r.m.s.d. is 0.610 Å between the structures of the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC and the Y247S/Y251S mutant and 0.654 Å between the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC and the Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1130" refid="tbl5">Table 5</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl5"/>). The wild type-like double tyrosine mutant (Y247S/Y251S) and the PC interface-impaired quadruple mutant structures are even more similar to each other, with an r.m.s.d. of only 0.310 Å (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1140" refid="tbl5">Table 5</ce:cross-ref>). Structural alignments and comparisons indicate the mutations introduced little conformational change in the crystallized proteins (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1150" refid="fig9">Fig. 9</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig9"/>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>).</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para260" view="all">The helix B region and the rim loop in both mutant protein crystal structures are similar to the corresponding regions in the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> monomer structure (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1160" refid="tbl5">Table 5</ce:cross-ref>), except that the position of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> in the flexible rim loop was slightly shifted. The active sites of the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC and both tyrosine mutants, Y247S/Y251S, with activity similar to wild type protein, and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S, with greatly reduced interfacial activity, are superimposable except for His<ce:sup loc="post">82</ce:sup>, which is rotated by about 90° in both mutant protein crystal structures. A crystal of Y247S/Y251S PI-PLC was later soaked with 100 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> 
                              <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol (PDB entry <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs130" xlink:href="pdb:3EA2" xlink:type="simple">3EA2</ce:inter-ref>) for comparison to the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC structure with <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol in the active site (PDB entry <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs140" xlink:href="pdb:1PTG" xlink:type="simple">1PTG</ce:inter-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1170" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>). The r.m.s.d. between those two structures is 0.597 Å, and the structural alignment between them reveals no significant conformational changes for active site residues; bound <ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol is also at the same position for the two protein structures (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1180" refid="fig9">Fig. 9</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). Therefore, in the crystal and in the absence of an interface, the tyrosine-to-serine mutations of PI-PLC appear to have little influence on the active site geometry either with or without an inositol moiety bound.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para270" view="all">The PI-PLC W47A/W242A mutant was crystallized as a dimer, prompting the conjecture that the membrane state of the enzyme might be a dimer. As shown in <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs230" refid="fig2 fig10">Figs. 2 and 10</ce:cross-refs>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup> from each subunit of the dimer form a quasi-herringbone pattern that stabilizes the dimer interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1190" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). Although both Y247S/Y251S and Y246S/Y247S/Y248S/Y251S mutant monomers are structurally similar to a crystallographic monomer of the W47A/W242A PI-PLC dimer, neither tyrosine mutant crystallized as a dimer. For rPI-PLC, the lack of dimer formation in solution is presumably because helix B is still intact, and the hydrophobic tryptophan “plug” this feature forms in solution prevents close approach of another PI-PLC and subsequent dimerization via the surface tyrosine residues (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1200" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). A closer examination of the putative dimer interface region for the tyrosine mutants shows that the essential interactions cannot be made when four of the crucial tyrosine residues have been replaced by serine (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1210" refid="fig10">Fig. 10</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig10"/>
                              <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>). However, Y247S/Y251S still has Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup> and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup> as well as Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup> to stabilize the hydrophobic core of a membrane-induced dimer interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1220" refid="fig10">Fig. 10</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). The remaining interactions may be adequate to allow dimers to form on the membrane surface. Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup> in particular may be a key interaction for a surface-induced dimer that resembles that of W47A/W242A.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para280" view="all">The high similarity between the structures of the double/quadruple mutant and the wild type PI-PLC indicate that the reduction in enzymatic activities of the triple and quadruple tyrosine mutant proteins and altered binding affinity toward PC vesicles is not likely to be caused by large conformational changes within the single monomer domain. Two possible explanations may explain how replacement of these surface Tyr residues with Ser reduces enzymatic activity; (i) removal of these tyrosine residues disrupts the transient dimer that forms when protein binds to PC-containing membranes, or (ii) these same tyrosine residues are critical parts of a defined PC binding site that aids in anchoring the protein as a monomer to interfaces.</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="cesec200" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para290" view="all">Although the binding of peripheral proteins to membranes often has a strong electrostatic component, tryptophan insertion into the membrane is also frequently observed, particularly for interfacial binding to PC surfaces (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs240" refid="bib37 bib38 bib39">37–39</ce:cross-refs>). Tryptophan residues are particularly prominent in the region of the bilayer interfaces in membrane proteins as well (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs250" refid="bib40 bib41 bib42">40–42</ce:cross-refs>). Previous work has suggested that Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> of the <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC insert into the membrane surface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1230" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1240" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>) and that this interaction is facilitated by PC molecules present in the interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1250" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>). Trp insertion in turn helps anchor the PI-PLC to vesicles and is ultimately connected to enzymatic activation in that assay system. A variety of biophysical techniques suggested that binding of the protein to the interface changed the enzyme conformation and shifted the enzyme to an “activated” form (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs260" refid="bib4 bib5 bib6">4–6</ce:cross-refs>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1260" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). However, no specifics were available on the structure of that activated form.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para300" view="all">Interaction of a phospholipid with a specific binding site on the protein has been detected with a wide variety of peripheral membrane proteins. It usually is identified by enhanced membrane association (<ce:italic>e.g</ce:italic>. C2 domains and Ca<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup>-mediated interaction with anionic phospholipids, PH domains, and phosphoinositides) or altered kinetics (if the protein is an enzyme). Unless the interaction is of fairly high affinity, localizing the binding site can be difficult and is usually explored with mutagenesis. Along with specific phospholipid binding, protein oligomerization may also provide a way of regulating function. There are integral membrane proteins that are purified as monomers but are believed to function as dimers or oligomers. For example, a tetracycline-divalent cation efflux protein TetL was suggested to exist as an oligomer rather than a monomer in the membrane based on SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis of membrane samples (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1270" refid="bib43">43</ce:cross-ref>). However, exploring membrane-induced oligomerization of peripheral membrane proteins, particularly where such aggregation might not be necessary for enzymatic activity but rather enhance catalysis, is very difficult. Chemical cross-linking only works if suitable chemical handles are available (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1280" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). Association and dissociation of the proteins from the membrane can also make trapping aggregates difficult.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para310" view="all">Initial studies (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs270" refid="bib4 bib5 bib6">4–6</ce:cross-refs>) with the <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC suggested that kinetic activation of cIP (and monomeric diC<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>PI) hydrolysis by PC involved the protein binding to a PC interface at a site distinct from the active site. That interaction then induced a conformational change in the active site that enhanced catalysis of water-soluble substrates. Localization of a distinct binding region on PI-PLC was restricted to the observation that the Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> seemed to aid in binding of the protein to interfaces. More recent work, that of a crystal structure of a dimeric protein when the two key surface tryptophan residues were changed to alanine, suggested that the specific kinetic activation of PI-PLC by PC interfaces was the result of interface-induced dimerization of the protein (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1290" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). The extrapolation of that dimer to rPI-PLC posited that when the Trp side chains were present, a hydrophobic Trp plug centered on helix B prevents dimer formation in solution. Thus, the initial approach of the protein to the lipid interface would be as a monomer. Changes in this helix B feature as well as the rim of the βα-barrel as a result of partitioning of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> into the membrane would displace the Trp plug and allow transient dimers to form on the surface. In the case of PI cleavage, the dimer could enhance catalytic activity by allowing each monomer to alternate substrate binding and catalysis. Although the product diacylglycerol could easily be released back into the bilayer, water-soluble cIP must be released into solution. This would require the rim of the barrel to be at least partially solvent-exposed. For a monomeric protein, release of cIP might destabilize membrane binding of the protein and cause the protein to dissociate from the membrane. Dimerization could effectively increase the affinity of the protein for substrate by increasing the residence time of the protein on the membrane, essentially promoting “scooting” rather than “hopping” of the enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1300" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1310" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>). However, anchoring of a PI-PLC monomer via a discrete PC binding site could also keep the protein on the surface long enough for processive catalysis. Can these two possibilities be distinguished or at least constrained with the mutagenesis experiments reported in this work?</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para320" view="all">A dimer interface similar to what we see with W47A/W242A would be held together by four key tyrosine residues, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup>. Because these tyrosine residues are involved in both hydrophobic and hydrophilic binding between the two subunits, replacing some or all of these Tyr residues should impair dimer formation, reduce the affinity of the protein for interfaces, and reduce catalytic activity. The three different tyrosine mutants constructed to test the importance of PI-PLC dimer formation for interfacial activation of PI-PLC by PC surfaces definitely show that this region of the protein is linked to kinetic activation. At least in the crystal, removal of tyrosines does not alter the active site structure. In solution, there may be a small loss of helix in the area of the tyrosine strip, but at least for Y247S/Y251S this can be retrieved by occupation of the active site. The difference in crystal and solution conformations does highlight one important point; occupation of the active site can affect the secondary structure of the protein, at least around helix G. So if the dimer is formed, it could influence detailed arrangements of groups in the active site, at least enough to increase <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">cat</ce:inf>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para330" view="all">Clearly, when the four key tyrosines are replaced by serine, the herringbone intersubunit interactions can no longer occur. Consistent with this, the quadruple mutant has reduced activity (and reduced PC affinity) in all assay systems. The other two tyrosine mutant enzymes would be expected to have different dimerization potentials. The double mutant Y247S/Y251S behaved kinetically almost the same as rPI-PLC. The crystal structure of this mutant suggests that whereas the putative dimer interface might be somewhat destabilized, many intersubunit interactions can still be formed to stabilize the dimer on a PC-containing membrane. Because occupation of the active by a ligand (<ce:italic>myo</ce:italic>-inositol) was shown to enhance structure in this protein, it is likely that a phospholipid at the active could work in concert with the Tyr residues in stabilizing, at least transiently, a surface-bound dimer.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para340" view="all">The alternate explanation is that these Tyr form part of a well defined PC binding site on a monomer that is distinct from the interaction of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> with interfaces. Tyr aromatic rings could form a binding site for the positively charged choline moiety. This region of PI-PLC is rich in surface Tyr, and several of them could stabilize PC binding via π-cation interactions. In that case one would also expect removal of the Tyr would dramatically reduce the enzyme activity, particularly for PI/PC cleavage and diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC activation of cIP.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para350" view="all">The observation that both triple and quadruple mutant enzymes have dramatically altered interactions with diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles provides clues into what happens to the protein when it binds activator lipids. Both these less active enzymes still bind diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC molecules with fairly good affinity, as assessed by the intrinsic fluorescence experiments. However, the change in intrinsic fluorescence of the protein upon binding to diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC is more in keeping with a ligand binding to the active site. In the field cycling experiments, Y246S/Y247S/Y248S, compared with rPI-PLC, converts the polydisperse rod-shaped diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles to much smaller structures at a ratio of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC to protein where the maximum line broadening is observed. At this total concentration of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC to PI-PLC, 35:1, there are no larger micelles detected that coexist. A PI-PLC monomer could bind a relatively small and finite number of these diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC molecules to form a nearly spherical small mixed micelle. However, the very elongated dimer, like that observed for W47A/W242A, would certainly form larger aggregates. It would present an extended rim with a number of hydrophobic contacts.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para360" view="all">If by replacing the Tyr with Ser we have removed a specific PC binding site, then there should be little difference in specific activity for PI cleavage in diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC <ce:italic>versus</ce:italic> Triton X-100 for the triple and quadruple Tyr mutant proteins. Yet there is a 2–3-fold decrease in the Triton X-100 assay system (even more pronounced than for rPI-PLC). If transient surface-bound dimers are the optimally active species, then they must form in PI/Triton X-100 as well as in PC-containing systems. The high relative activities for the Tyr mutants in both micelle systems compared with PI/PC SUV or cIP/diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC assay systems would then suggest that an amphiphilic molecule occupying the active site is also very important for biasing the distribution to dimers.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para370" view="all">On balance, then, these experiments seem more consistent with a surface-activated dimer analogous to W47A/W242A. Could dimers form in the absence of interfaces? Some indication of this may be provided by examining the second step of the reaction, cIP hydrolysis. In solution in the absence of interfaces, monomer and a very small fraction of dimers of rPI-PLC could exist in equilibrium (indeed, in previous cross-linking experiments we could observe a very small amount of dimer for protein alone in solution (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1320" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>), suggesting aggregates might occur). Removing any of the tyrosine residues that contribute to the dimer interface of W47A/W4242A would be expected to bias the equilibrium even more toward monomer. The reduced cIP hydrolysis rates for all the tyrosine-to-serine mutant enzymes in the absence of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC are consistent with this possibility. If a diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC interface can still enhance dimerization, then the cIP hydrolysis rate should increase dramatically as observed for Y247S/Y251S. These kinetics are harder to explain with the postulate of a specific PC binding site. It is interesting that all the Tyr mutant cIP hydrolysis activities are similar (0.2 to 0.6 μmol min<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup> mg<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup>) and considerably smaller than for rPI-PLC (2.3 μmol min<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup> mg<ce:sup loc="post">−1</ce:sup>). If disruption of a PC binding site were the result of the Tyr replacements with Ser, then one would not expect much of difference between rPI-PLC and these mutant enzymes in the absence of diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC (and this is what is observed with examining the kinetics of W47A, W242A, and W47A/W242A (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1330" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>), mutants that reduce binding to PC interfaces but would not alter a monomer-dimer equilibrium involving Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para380" view="all">In summary, we have probed the importance of the strip of tyrosine residues that form the core of the dimer interface observed previously in the mutant protein W47A/W242A. The reduction in specific activity in all assay systems with the removal of three or four of these tyrosines can arise from either two effects. (i) The lack of the tyrosine strip disfavors the surface-induced dimer formation that is needed for optimal activity. (ii) Removal of these tyrosines abolishes a specific and critical PC binding site. Because both phosphotransferase and cyclic phosphodiesterase activities are reduced by these mutations (and the physical interaction of the mutant protein with PC interfaces is altered), we cannot absolutely distinguish between these two alternatives (and the definition of the activated form of the enzyme they imply). However, the evidence, particularly kinetic changes in PI/Triton X-100 and cIP in the absence of interfaces, appears more consistent with dimerization of this PI-PLC as the activated form of the enzyme.</ce:para>
