                  Antimicrobial resistance patterns in a collection of 33 motile Aeromonas species were described in this study. Quinolone has been frequently employed for treatment of Aeromonas-related diseases, and prolonged use of antimicrobial compounds has led to development of resistant strains. In a sample of diseased fish and environmental water, we evaluated nalidixic acid (n = 19) and ciprofloxacin (n = 4) resistance via minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays and the genetic basis was also investigated. Among the isolated Aeromonas spp., 17 strains encoded for chromosomal mutations of quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDRs) in gyrA, 11 strains encoded for mutations of QRDRs in parC, 1 strain harbored plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) qnrS1-like gene and 4 strains harbored the PMQR qnrS2 gene. In particular, the new variant (qnrS1-like) differed from qnrS1 by 6 amino acid substitutions at positions 5 (Asn5→Arg5), 120 (Ser120→Thr120), 148 (Asn148→His148), 206 (Leu206→Glu206), 207 (Ile207→ Leu207), and 216 (Tyr216→Phe216), and the gene was designated qnrS5. These resistant strains may function as reservoirs of quinolone resistance.

            <ce:abstract xml:lang="en" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">Antimicrobial resistance patterns in a collection of 33 motile <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> species were described in this study. Quinolone has been frequently employed for treatment of <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic>-related diseases, and prolonged use of antimicrobial compounds has led to development of resistant strains. In a sample of diseased fish and environmental water, we evaluated nalidixic acid (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 19) and ciprofloxacin (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 4) resistance via minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays and the genetic basis was also investigated. Among the isolated <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp., 17 strains encoded for chromosomal mutations of quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDRs) in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic>, 11 strains encoded for mutations of QRDRs in <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic>, 1 strain harbored plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) <ce:italic>qnrS1-</ce:italic>like gene and 4 strains harbored the PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> gene. In particular, the new variant (<ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like) differed from <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic> by 6 amino acid substitutions at positions 5 (Asn<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup>→Arg<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup>), 120 (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>→Thr<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>), 148 (Asn<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup>→His<ce:sup loc="post">148</ce:sup>), 206 (Leu<ce:sup loc="post">206</ce:sup>→Glu<ce:sup loc="post">206</ce:sup>), 207 (Ile<ce:sup loc="post">207</ce:sup>→ Leu<ce:sup loc="post">207</ce:sup>), and 216 (Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup>→Phe<ce:sup loc="post">216</ce:sup>), and the gene was designated <ce:italic>qnrS5</ce:italic>. These resistant strains may function as reservoirs of quinolone resistance.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="sec1" view="all">
                  <ce:label>1</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">Members of the genus <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> are found in a broad range of habitats, including soil, fresh and brackish water and sewage (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib8">Daskalov H, 2006</ce:cross-ref>). These organisms are also known to be primary fish pathogens (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib2">Austin and Adams, 1996</ce:cross-ref>) and, in humans, some motile <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. (i.e. <ce:italic>Aeromonas hydrophila</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Aeromonas sobria</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>Aeromonas veronii</ce:italic>) have received increasing attention as emergent agents of food-borne gastrointestinal disease (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib10">Galindo et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">Quinolone is one of the most commonly used classes of antimicrobial agents in human and veterinary medicine, and their extensive use has been associated with a trend toward rising quinolone resistance levels (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib13 bib14">Hooper, 2001, 2005</ce:cross-refs>). Quinolone resistance is due principally to chromosomal mutations that alter the target enzymes for these drugs (i.e. DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV) in Gram-negative bacteria (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib1 bib26">Akasaka et al., 2001; Piddock, 1999</ce:cross-refs>); in addition, mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDRs) of the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> genes have been shown to be related to quinolone resistance in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib12 bib22">Goni-Urriza et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2011</ce:cross-refs>). Plasmid-mediated transferable quinolone resistance (PMQR) determinants (<ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>) have been identified exclusively in Enterobacteriaceae, but have very rarely been identified in Vibrionaceae and Aeromonadaceae (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib21 bib5 bib24 bib27">Kim et al., 2009; Cattoir et al., 2008; Lunn et al., 2010; Sanchez-Cespedes et al., 2008</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">In this study, using a set of diseased fish and environmental water samples, we investigated the quinolone resistance of genetically identified motile <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. frequently implicated in human infections. The phenotype governing nalidixic acid and ciprofloxacin resistance was assayed by a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay, and their genetic determinants were also accessed via analysis of point mutations in the QRDRs and detection of PMQR <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" view="all">
                  <ce:label>2</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Materials and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.1" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Isolation and identification of bacterial isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">Moribund fish were sampled from Korean fish farms and aquariums; environmental water from rivers, sewage and lakes was collected from the Seoul or Gyeonggi province in Korea for bacterial isolation by the Laboratory of Aquatic Animal Medicine, College of Veterinary Medicine and Research Institute for Veterinary Science, Seoul National University (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). Swabs from the internal organs and skin of these fish along with water samples were streaked onto tryptic soy agar (TSA; Difco, USA), incubated for 20 h at 35 °C and then identified using the Vitek System<ce:sup loc="post">®</ce:sup>2 (bioMerieux, France) and partial sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene at the Macrogen Genomic Division, Korea (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib23">Lane, 1991</ce:cross-ref>). The reference strain was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC): <ce:italic>A. hydrophila</ce:italic> ATCC<ce:sup loc="post">®</ce:sup>7966 (hereinafter referred as AHH).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.2" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>MIC determinations</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-tests were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, an overnight culture of the bacteria diluted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard was used to inoculate Mueller–Hinton agar plate (Oxoid, UK). After drying, the <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-test strips were applied on the plates and incubated overnight at 35 °C. The MIC on both ends was taken at the intersection of the inhibition ellipse and the <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>-test strip edge. MIC results of <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> strains were used to classify strains as resistant or sensitive according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute M45-A (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib7">CLSI, 2006</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> ATCC<ce:sup loc="post">®</ce:sup> 25922 was utilized as a quality control bacterial strain.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.3" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Molecular identification of quinolone-resistant genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">Total DNA was extracted from isolated bacteria by suspending bacteria in sterile water followed by boiling for 10 min. After 3 min of centrifugation at 10,000 g, supernatants were collected and 1:100 dilutions in sterile water were utilized as PCR templates. For analysis of chromosomal quinolone-resistant determinants, QRDRs of the <ce:italic>gyr</ce:italic>A and <ce:italic>par</ce:italic>C genes (encoding for subunits of DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, respectively) were detected in accordance with the method described by <ce:cross-ref refid="bib11">Giraud et al. (2004)</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">In addition, plasmid DNA was extracted via a method involving alkaline lysis (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">Kado and Liu, 1981</ce:cross-ref>) and employed as a template for detection of the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes (<ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>) (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib3 bib4">Cattoir et al., 2007a, b</ce:cross-refs>). The primers employed for detection of resistance genes are shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">Direct sequencing was carried out commercially by the Macrogen Genomic Division, Korea using ABI PRISM Big Dye TM Terminator Cycle Sequencing technology (Applied BioSystems, USA). Electrophoresis of sequencing reactions was completed using an automated ABI PRISM 3730XL DNA Sequencing System (Applied BioSystems, USA). The sequences were subsequently analyzed then with the AlignX tool in the Vector NTI program (Invitrogen, USA). BLAST searches were carried out using both the blastn and blastx algorithms provided by the National Center for Biotechnology Information website (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov" xlink:type="simple">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</ce:inter-ref>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.4" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>A full-length coding sequence (CDS) for <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">To obtain the full-length coding sequence (CDS) for <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes, an additional PCR experiment was carried out. A 657 bp <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene was amplified using plasmid DNA and the primer set (QNRS-F: 5′-ATGGAAACCTACCGTCACACATAT-3′ and QNRS-R: 5′-CTAGTCAGGAAAAACAACAATACCC-3′) (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>). The PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 50 μl containing 100 ng of plasmid DNA, 5 μl 10x Taq-Buffer, 4 μl 10 mM dNTP, 1 U of <ce:italic>Taq</ce:italic> DNA polymerase (Invitrogen, USA) and 2 pmol of each primer. After initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, amplification was attempted by 30 cycles under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, 1 min at the appropriate annealing temperature (55 °C for SNUFPC-A5, 10 and 11, 50 °C for SNUFPC-A1, and 48 °C for SNUFPC-A4) and 1.5 min at 72 °C. Cycling was followed by a final extension for 7 min at 72 °C. The PCR product was ligated into the pTOP TA V2 plasmid vector using a TOP PCR cloning kit (Enzynomics, Korea) and this plasmid was transformed into <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strain DH5α (Enzynomics, Korea). The recombinant plasmid was extracted via the alkaline lysis procedure (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">Kado and Liu, 1981</ce:cross-ref>) and sequenced as described previously.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">Alignment of the nucleotide and amino acid sequences was generated with BioEdit software (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://www.mbio.ncsu.edu/BioEdit/bioedit.html" xlink:type="simple">http://www.mbio.ncsu.edu/BioEdit/bioedit.html</ce:inter-ref>) using the amplified <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes and known <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes obtained from GenBank (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:AB187515.1" xlink:type="simple">AB187515.1</ce:inter-ref>, HM125706.1, <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:EF501989.1" xlink:type="simple">EF501989.1</ce:inter-ref>, GQ914055.1, <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:EU715253.1" xlink:type="simple">EU715253.1</ce:inter-ref> and HM126016.1 for the <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic> gene and <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:FJ233867.1" xlink:type="simple">FJ233867.1</ce:inter-ref>, <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:EU439940.1" xlink:type="simple">EU439940.1</ce:inter-ref>, <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:DQ485530.1" xlink:type="simple">DQ485530.1</ce:inter-ref> and <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:EU439941.1" xlink:type="simple">EU439941.1</ce:inter-ref> for <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> gene, <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:EU077611.1" xlink:type="simple">EU077611.1</ce:inter-ref> for <ce:italic>qnrS3</ce:italic> and <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="ncbi-n:FJ418153.1" xlink:type="simple">FJ418153.1</ce:inter-ref> for <ce:italic>qnrS4</ce:italic>). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using Bioedit software and Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) 3 software, version 3 with bootstrap values calculated from 1000 replicates. The neighbor joining method was used to construct the phylogenetic tree.</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="sec4" view="all">
                  <ce:label>4</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0105" view="all">In aquaculture systems, diagnoses are frequently presumptive and front-line treatment for the causative pathogen is generally administered without susceptibility data. In many cases, use of inappropriate antibiotics causes not only therapeutic failures, but also an increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistant strains (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib11">Giraud et al., 2004</ce:cross-ref>). This situation can create serious public health problems because these strains from fish and water may be transferred to humans following handling of diseased fish, when diseased fish are consumed or upon drinking contaminated water (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib25 bib19">Petersen and Dalsgaard, 2003; Khajanchi et al., 2010</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0110" view="all">In this study, we evaluated resistance to nalidixic acid and ciprofloxacin using an MIC assay for isolated <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> strains. According to the results, nalidixic acid resistance predominated over ciprofloxacin resistance in the isolated strains and all strains resistant to nalidixic acid were also ciprofloxacin-resistant. We further investigated the genetic determinants of these drugs in this study, and their resistance patterns were found to be related to chromosomal mutations of QRDRs in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic>, or PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:section id="sec4.1" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Analysis of the QRDRs of the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0115" view="all">The acquisition of quinolone resistance by Gram-negative bacteria has been associated principally with mutations in the QRDRs of the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> genes (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib1 bib26">Akasaka et al., 2001; Piddock, 1999</ce:cross-refs>). In 13 strains used in this study, the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic>-encoding subunit was shown to contain an amino acid substitution (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Ile<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>). Only 3 strains possessed the substitution (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Arg<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>) and 1 strain showed (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Val<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>) in the GyrA QRDR. The (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Ile<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>) substitution was observed in all <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> species, but (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Arg<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>) was shown in only <ce:italic>A. veronii</ce:italic> and (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>→Val<ce:sup loc="post">83</ce:sup>) occurred in only <ce:italic>A. sobria.</ce:italic> Among 17 strains harboring mutations in the QRDRs of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic>, 16 strains demonstrated quinolone (nalidixic acid or ciprofloxacin) resistance as shown by MIC. This point mutation at codon 83 of the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> QRDR was also determined to be the most common among <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib11 bib12">Giraud et al., 2004; Goni-Urriza et al., 2002</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0120" view="all">In contrast, no resistant mutants harboring only a <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> mutation were observed, and when mutations were detected in the <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> QRDRs these occurred in isolates also possessing <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> mutations. These data suggest that topoisomerase IV is a secondary target for quinolones in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib9 bib20 bib12">Drlica and Zhao, 1997; Khodursky et al., 1995; Goni-Urriza et al., 2002</ce:cross-refs>). Despite previous reports that quinolone resistance in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. is related to mutations in the QRDRs of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> at codons 87 and 92 and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> at codon 84, mutations in these codons were not associated with quinolone-resistant strains in this study (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib11 bib12 bib22">Giraud et al., 2004; Goni-Urriza et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2011</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec4.2" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Analysis of PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0125" view="all">PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> genes have been identified in a few members of the Aeromonadaceae (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib5 bib28 bib27">Cattoir et al., 2008; Verner-Jeffreys et al., 2009; Sanchez-Cespedes et al., 2008</ce:cross-refs>) and no other <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene has been previously detected in this family. We isolated 1 strain harboring the <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like gene and 4 strains harboring the <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> gene. All were clinical isolates and no <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene was found in environmental isolates. The <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like gene identified in <ce:italic>A. sobria</ce:italic> showed 96% similarity to the known <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrS3</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS4</ce:italic> groups, but <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> genes identified in the isolated strains were closely related to known <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> genes (99%–100% identity). All strains positive for the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene were resistant to nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, or both.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0130" view="all">Importantly, PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes from isolated resistant strains might function as important vectors for the transfer of quinolone resistance, since <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. are ubiquitous and inhabit a wide range of environments. Since transmission of these determinants is plasmid-dependent, we sought to investigate whether plasmids from <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>-positive strains (SNUFPC-A1, A5, A6, A10, A11) can be cured or transferred to competent cells (AHH, quinolone-susceptible strain), but all attempts were unsuccessful (data not shown). However, the genetic characteristics of these plasmids should be further studied in greater detail to determine the transmission route of these mechanisms.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec4.3" view="all">
                     <ce:label>4.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>A new <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> variant (<ce:italic>qnrS5</ce:italic>)</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0135" view="all">Within each family, plasmid-mediated <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes show minor (≤10%) variations at particular positions. Amino acid substitutions at positions 5, 11, 12, 16, 21, 31, 41, 44, 89, 91, 102, 106, 120, 148, 201, 206, 207 and 216 for <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic>-encoding genes and their variants were discussed based on a previous study (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib15">Jacoby et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>). At these particular positions, no differences were found between amplified <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> genes (HQ631375 and HQ631376) and the reference <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> gene. Amino acid differences at these 18 positions suggest that these amplified genes belong to the <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> group. However, the amplified <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like gene sequence (HQ631377) showed some variation from the known <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic> gene at 6 of the 18 positions, notably 5, 120, 148, 206, 207 and 216, and this sequence differed from <ce:italic>qnrS3</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qnrS4</ce:italic>. The amplified <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like gene sequence was not closely related to the reference <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrS3</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrS4</ce:italic> genes as shown in the phylogenetic tree. However, sequences of the amplified <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>-like gene (HQ631377) showed higher similarity to the reference <ce:italic>qnrS1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrS3</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>qnrS4</ce:italic> genes than to the <ce:italic>qnrS2</ce:italic> gene. This sequence was finally named <ce:italic>qnrS5</ce:italic> (nomenclature according to <ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://www.lahey.org/qnrStudies" xlink:type="simple">http://www.lahey.org/qnrStudies</ce:inter-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0140" view="all">In this study, quinolone resistance was described in terms of a chromosomal mutation in the QRDR of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> and PMQR <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> genes, and this report is the first description of a new <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> variant (termed <ce:italic>qnrS5</ce:italic>) identified in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> strains. Other mechanisms that were not explored in the present study, such as decreased accumulation due to impermeability of the membrane and/or an overexpression of efflux pump systems (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib26">Piddock, 1999</ce:cross-ref>), may also be responsible for quinolone resistance in these <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> strains. Among strains studied which showed different MICs, some carried the same mutations in <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> genes and the <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> gene, while others possessed neither chromosomal mutations nor <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes. Although not all mechanisms of quinolone resistance were discussed in this study, the quinolone-resistant strains identified may act as carriers of this resistance type; thus, appropriate use of antibiotics in an aquaculture system should be emphasized so as to prevent potential spread of resistance to pathogens of fish and humans and to improve therapeutic treatment of fish.</ce:para>
