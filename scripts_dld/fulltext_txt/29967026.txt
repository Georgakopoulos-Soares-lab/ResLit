The mobile colistin resistance gene mcr-3 is globally disseminated in both Enterobacteriaceae and Aeromonas species, with the latter potentially serving as a reservoir for this gene. Here, we investigated the prevalence of mcr-3 in rectal swabs from humans, in food-producing animals and their products, and in the aquatic environment, and we investigated the genetic relationships between the mcr-3 -positive isolates. 

KEYWORDS: mcr-3 , Aeromonas , aquatic environment 

ABSTRACT 

The mobile colistin resistance gene mcr-3 is globally disseminated in both Enterobacteriaceae and Aeromonas species, with the latter potentially serving as a reservoir for this gene. Here, we investigated the prevalence of mcr-3 in rectal swabs from humans, in food-producing animals and their products, and in the aquatic environment, and we investigated the genetic relationships between the mcr-3 -positive isolates. An enriched broth screening method was used to detect mcr-3 in samples, and species identification of isolates from positive samples was carried out by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry and shotgun sequencing. All mcr-3 -positive isolates were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing, conjugation, and whole-genome sequencing. Ten Aeromonas isolates, including 2 from human rectal swabs, 1 from pork, 3 from chicken meat, and 4 from the aquatic environment, were positive for mcr-3 , but only 2 showed resistance to colistin. In addition to the mcr-3 variants identified previously (the novel variants were termed mcr-3.13 to mcr-3.18 ), all isolates harbored mcr-3 -like genes downstream of the mcr-3 variants. The MCR-3.13 to MCR-3.18 proteins exhibited only 89.2% to 96.1% amino acid identity to the original MCR-3 protein. Whole-genome sequence analysis indicated diversity within the genetic environments of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates and possible transmission between different sources in China and even worldwide. Close relationships between mcr-3 -positive and mcr-3 -negative Aeromonas isolates suggested that mcr-3 might be common in Aeromonas species, which are not inherent hosts of mcr-3 but may act as an important reservoir of this mobile colistin resistance gene. 

INTRODUCTION 

To date, the mobile colistin resistance genes, mcr-1 to mcr-7 , have attracted international attention even beyond the medical and biological sciences ( 1 – 5 ; S. R. Partridge, V. D. Pilato, Y. Doi, M. Feldgarden, D. H. Haft, W. Klimke, S. Kumar-Singh, J. H. Liu, S. Malhotra-Kumar, A. Prasad, G. M. Rossolini, S. Schwarz, J. Z. Shen, T. R. Walsh, Y. Wang, and B. B. Xavier, unpublished observations). Moreover, mcr-1 was the most prevalent mcr gene in over 40 countries ( 6 ). However, subsequent to the reporting of the third mcr -type gene, termed mcr-3 , this gene was identified in both Salmonella and Escherichia coli isolates from human infections in Denmark and in an E. coli isolate from cattle in Spain ( 3 , 7 – 9 ). It is speculated that mcr-3 most likely originates from Aeromonas species, as the MCR-3 protein shows a high degree of similarity to MCR-3-like sequences from 10 Aeromonas species that originated from humans, animals, and the aquatic environment ( 3 ). Furthermore, the flanking regions of mcr-3 in Enterobacteriaceae strains, including the truncated transposon ΔTn As2 and the insertion sequence IS Kpn40 , exhibited high levels of nucleotide sequence identity to corresponding regions in aeromonads ( 3 ). The 5 recently identified mcr-3 variants ( mcr-3.3 and mcr-3.6 to 3.9 ) in Aeromonas species originated from retail chicken meat in China and from a turkey and aquatic animals in Germany. This observation suggested that aeromonads represent a potential reservoir of mcr-3 ( 10 , 11 ). However, whether mcr-3 and mcr-3 -like genes, which are located immediately downstream of mcr-3 , are naturally occurring determinants in Aeromonas species remains to be answered. 

Aeromonas species are widely distributed in soil, foodstuffs, and the aquatic environment, where they are opportunistic pathogens of poikilotherms, causing numerous diseases such as “red leg” disease in frogs, ascites in carp ( 12 ), and other diseases in freshwater fish ( 13 ). Additionally, Aeromonas species are known to cause gastrointestinal and extraintestinal infections in humans ( 14 , 15 ). Based on human fecal carriage rates, Aeromonas species are present in the gastrointestinal tracts of up to 4% of the healthy population ( 16 ). The initial report of the discovery of mcr-3 , and additional retrospective studies, identified mcr-3 variants in Aeromonas isolates from poultry and aquatic animals. However, the presence of and genetic relationships among mcr-3 -carrying Aeromonas isolates from animals (food-producing animals and their products), the aquatic environment, and humans have not been studied extensively. In this study, we screened various samples, including human rectal swabs, swabs obtained from swine, chickens, and retail pork and chicken meat, and water samples obtained from rivers in Hangzhou, China, for the presence of mcr-3 to identify variants of mcr-3 and to analyze the genetic relationships among mcr-3 -positive isolates. 

RESULTS 

Species diversity. 

To investigate the prevalence of mcr-3 in samples from various origins, we collected a comprehensive set of samples from humans, farm animals, animal products, and river water in Hangzhou, the capital of Zhejiang Province, China. In total, 2 (1.3%), 1 (2.9%), 3 (9.4%), and 4 (10.0%) mcr-3 -positive samples, and corresponding single isolates, were identified among the human, pork, chicken meat, and river water samples, respectively ( Fig. 1 ). All mcr-3 -positive isolates were negative for mcr-1 , mcr-2 , mcr-4 , mcr-5 , mcr-6 , and mcr-7 . None of the samples from pigs or chickens was mcr-3 positive. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry and 16S rRNA, gyrB , and rpoB sequencing analyses revealed that the 10 mcr-3 -positive isolates belonged to Aeromonas species, including Aeromonas caviae ( n = 3), Aeromonas veronii ( n = 2), Aeromonas bivalvium ( n = 2), Aeromonas media ( n = 1), Aeromonas salmonicida ( n = 1), and Aeromonas allosaccharophila ( n = 1) ( Table 1 ). The A. caviae isolates were recovered from both human and pork samples, while the A. veronii isolates originated from human and river water samples. The mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates were more prevalent in the aquatic environment and chicken meat samples than in human rectal samples (10.0% versus 1.3% [ P = 0.018] and 9.4% versus 1.3% [ P = 0.037], respectively).

Variants of mcr-3 . 

The 1,623-bp mcr-3 variant genes exhibited 90 to 100% nucleotide sequence identity to mcr-3.3 , and the corresponding proteins exhibited 93 to 100% amino acid sequence identity to MCR-3.3; however, they showed just 87.2% to 95.7% nucleotide sequence identity and 89.2% to 96.1% amino acid sequence identity to the original mcr-3 and MCR-3, respectively ( 3 ). The 6 novel mcr-3 variant genes were termed mcr-3.13 to mcr-3.18 ( Table 1 ). Downstream of the mcr-3 variant genes, 1,626-bp mcr-3 -like genes encoding MCR-3-like proteins were found, which exhibited 98 to 99% nucleotide sequence and amino acid sequence identity to the mcr-3 -like gene and MCR-3-like protein from A. veronii ( 10 ). The 3 novel downstream variants were identified and termed mcr-3 -like2, mcr -like3, and mcr -like4 ( Table 1 ; also see Table S1 in the supplemental material). The mcr-3 -like gene has been cloned and shown not to confer colistin resistance ( 10 ). 

Alignments of the predicted amino acid sequences of MCR-3, MCR-3.3, MCR-3.5 to MCR-3.10, MCR-3.13 to MCR-3.18, and MCR-3-like to MCR-3-like4 conducted by using MEGA 7.0 showed distinct variations in the MCR-3 variants and the MCR-3-like proteins (Fig. S1). Within the MCR-3 proteins, two domains could be distinguished, i.e., a C-terminal putative transmembrane domain that encompassed amino acids 1 to 212 and a N-terminal catalytic domain that included amino acids 228 to 540 (Fig. S1). Compared with the original MCR-3 protein, the MCR-3-like proteins exhibited more variations in the N-terminal region and in the region of amino acids 488 to 540, which belongs to the catalytic domain. Interestingly, A. allosaccharophila ZJ9-6 from chicken meat showed 37 and 22 amino acid substitutions in the putative transmembrane region and catalytic region, respectively (Fig. S1). Notably, residues 228 to 487 within the catalytic region of both MCR-3 variants and MCR-3-like proteins contained only 1 to 9 amino acid substitutions, indicating that this region is highly conserved among MCR-3 variants and MCR-3-like proteins (Fig. S1). 

Antimicrobial resistance profiles. 

Although all 10 mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates identified in this study harbored variants of mcr-3 , only 2 isolates from chicken meat ( A. salmonicida ZJ5-5 and A. allosaccharophila ZJ9-6) showed resistance to colistin (MICs of ≥32 mg/liter) and polymyxin B (MICs of >32 mg/liter). The remaining isolates exhibited considerably lower MIC values of <0.12 to 2 mg/liter. Eight of the isolates showed resistance to cephalosporins, including ceftriaxone and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid ( Table 1 ). Several bla OXA variants, including bla OXA-21 , bla OXA-10 , and bla OXA-12 , were detected in 4 isolates. Coincidentally, the A. veronii isolates ZJ12-3 and ZJ2-7 and the A. allosaccharophila isolate ZJ9-6 harbored bla CEPH-A3 together with bla OXA-12 . All isolates were susceptible to meropenem and imipenem, except for the human A. veronii isolate ZJ12-3, which was resistant to imipenem (MIC of >64 mg/liter). A 726-bp Aeromonas metallo-β-lactamase gene that exhibited 90.5% nucleotide sequence identity to the corresponding region of the 726-bp imiS gene from A. veronii ( 17 ) was detected in isolate ZJ12-3. Tetracycline resistance was observed in 7 isolates, with MIC values ranging from 16 to 128 mg/liter. All isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and amikacin, however, and 2 A. bivalvium isolates (ZJ20-2 and ZJ19-2) collected from river water were susceptible to all tested antimicrobial agents and did not harbor any additional resistance genes ( Table 1 ). 

Phylogenetic relationships. 

A dendrogram generated from the core genome sequences of 16 mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates (10 from this study, 5 from our two recent publications [ 10 , 11 ], and 1 from GenBank) (Table S1) and 8 mcr-3 -negative Aeromonas isolates (obtained from GenBank) (Table S1) revealed 8 distinct clades, with each clade consisting of sequences from a separate Aeromonas species ( Fig. 2 ). Most isolates ( n = 14) harbored exeA , which is associated with a type II secretion system. All A. salmonicida and A. hydrophila isolates carried the ahh1 gene, coding for the extracellular hemolysin AHH1. No additional antibiotic resistance genes or virulence-associated genes were detected in A. bivalvium isolates ZJ19-2 and ZJ20-2 (105 single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs], including 14 indels), both from river water. Apart from A. bivalvium , there was no difference in virulence-associated genes among the Aeromonas species ( Fig. 2 ). Furthermore, 3 A. caviae isolates (ZJ33-3 from a human and ZJ17-2 and ZJ66-1 from river water) harbored identical virulence-associated genes ( hlyA , responsible for a hemolysin), and 2 of them (ZJ17-2 and ZJ33-3 [>1,000 SNPs]) also had very similar resistance gene contents. These 3 isolates showed very little sequence divergence, suggesting a possible common origin, possibly through contamination of the aquatic environment with mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas from human intestinal flora. In addition, potential food chain transmission was assumed in a clade containing 3 A. veronii isolates (ZJ12-3, 172, and Z2-7 [>1,000 SNPs between 172 and Z2-7], from human feces, retail chicken meat, and retail pork, respectively). Interestingly, A. media IMT29879 ( mcr-3.7 ) and A. allosaccharophila IMT43045 ( mcr-3.6 ) from Germany clustered with A. media Z1-6 ( mcr-3.15 ) and A. allosaccharophila Z9-6 ( mcr-3.17 ) from China, suggesting a close relationship of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas species from the two continents. Importantly, we noted close relationships between several mcr-3 -negative and mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates, such as in the clades containing A. salmonicida and A. veronii , suggesting that mcr-3 -related genes do not intrinsically exist in Aeromonas species.

Genetic environment. 

All mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates identified in this study were subjected to conjugation by filter mating using E. coli strains J53, E600, EC600, and ZJ12-3 as recipients. Conjugation was not successful for any of the isolates. To determine the genetic context of the mcr-3 gene variants, the sequences of the mcr-3 -harboring contigs of the 10 isolates were assembled using SPAdes 3.9.0. The assembled contigs ranged in size from 7,074 to 216,458 bp. None of the contigs contained a plasmid replicon sequence included in the PlasmidFinder database, implying that the mcr-3 variants might be located in the chromosomal DNA rather than on plasmids (Table S1). A segment consisting of mcr-3 variant- mcr-3 -like- dgkA was discovered in all 10 isolates, and the region flanking this segment showed high levels of nucleotide sequence identity to the corresponding regions of A. hydrophila strain 173 (GenBank accession number AOBN01000008.1 ), which was isolated from the peritoneal fluid of a human in Malaysia, and A. veronii 172 (GenBank accession number MF495680 ), which was isolated from chicken meat in China. However, the flanking regions exhibited distinct differences from those of the mcr-3 -carrying plasmid pWJ1 ( Fig. 3 ). The scaffold regions of mcr-3.3 from A. veronii isolates ZJ12-3 (human feces) and Z2-7 (pork) showed high levels of similarity to the corresponding region from A. veronii isolate 172 (chicken meat). Two insertion sequences, IS Kpn3 and IS As17 , were located upstream of mcr-3.13 in A. media isolate Z1-6 from chicken meat. The sequences were flanked by two genes encoding membrane proteins that exhibited high levels of similarity to the corresponding region of A. hydrophila strain 173. IS As17 was absent in the upstream region of mcr-3.14 from A. salmonicida isolate Z5-5 from chicken meat, and both IS Kpn3 and IS As17 were missing in the upstream region of mcr-3.16 from A. caviae ZJ66-1 from river water.

DISCUSSION 

This is the first point-prevalence survey of mcr-3 in samples from humans, animals (pigs and chickens), retail meat (pork and chicken meat), and the aquatic environment in China. The mcr-3 variants, including mcr-3.3 and mcr-3.13 to 3.18 , were identified among 10 Aeromonas isolates from different sources but not in other Gram-negative bacteria. Notably, the discovery of a mcr-3.3 -positive A. veronii isolate and a mcr-3.13 -positive A. caviae isolate in the human rectum represents the first identification of mcr-3 -carrying Aeromonas isolates in healthy individuals. In contrast, several E. coli and Salmonella isolates harboring variants of mcr-3 have been identified in blood cultures from patients in Denmark ( 8 , 9 ). 

Aeromonas species are recognized enteric pathogens ( 18 ), and it is possible that the presence of Aeromonas isolates that carry these mcr-3 variants in the intestine will facilitate the spread of these genes to other bacterial species living in the same habitat. Samples from the aquatic environment had the highest prevalence (10.0%) of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates in the current study, indicating that, although mcr-3 appears to have been disseminated throughout various environments, the aquatic environment may be the major reservoir. The absence of mcr-3 -positive isolates in farm animals but their presence in food products of farm animal origin implies contamination of meat at some point in the slaughtering and processing chain. 

The mcr-3 -positive E. coli isolate WJ1 has MICs of 8 mg/liter for both colistin and polymyxin B, while E. coli isolates SNTR36B6 and ZTA15/01169-1EB1, which carry both mcr-1 and mcr-3 , have colistin MICs of 4 mg/liter ( 3 , 7 ). Additionally, 5 previously identified Aeromonas isolates carrying mcr-3.3 to mcr-3.7 exhibited colistin MICs ranging from 2 to ≥128 mg/liter ( 10 ). In the original report on mcr-3.3 in A. veronii , the mcr-3.3 -positive isolate demonstrated borderline susceptibility to colistin (MIC of 2 mg/liter). However, E. coli and A. salmonicida transformants carrying pUC19- mcr-3.3 exhibited 8- and 64-fold higher MIC values (MICs of 2 mg/liter and 64 mg/liter, respectively) than the transformant carrying pUC19 alone ( 10 ), suggesting that the location on a plasmid and the copy number of the mcr-3 gene also affect colistin MICs. These observations not only confirmed that the mcr-3 variants can confer colistin resistance in both Enterobacteriaceae strains and aeromonads but also suggested that the colistin resistance levels conferred by these genes may vary among different Aeromonas species. It is likely that the expression of these genes may be regulated, however, mitigating potentially toxic effects, as has been seen for mcr-1 , although this phenomenon has yet to be fully studied ( 19 ). 

Significant diversity within the flanking regions and among the few horizontal transfer elements was observed in the 10 mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates identified in this study. However, whole-genome sequence analysis and comparisons of the genetic environment revealed some close relationships between the mcr-3 -positive isolates from different sources and even different countries. For instance, A. media Z1-6 from chicken meat in China and A. media IMT29879 from a turkey in Germany were closely related, as were A. allosaccharophila Z9-6 from chicken meat in China and A. allosaccharophila IMT43045 from a golden orfe in Germany. These results suggest the transmission of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates between different species. The global prevalence of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas species in food, aquatic environments, and the human intestine might promote the dissemination of the mcr-3 gene and resistance to colistin. In addition, several mcr-3 -negative A. media , A. salmonicida , A. hydrophila , and A. veronii isolates clustered closely with mcr-3 -positive isolates belonging to the same species, indicating that the transmission of mcr-3 among Aeromonas species is highly possible. 

In conclusion, this study identified mcr-3.3 along with 6 novel mcr-3 variants ( mcr-3.13 to mcr-3.18 ) in 6 different Aeromonas species present in samples collected from various sources, including animal products (pork and chicken meat) and aquatic environments. This is also the first identification of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates in healthy humans. Importantly, the study confirmed that mcr-3 appears to be more common in Aeromonas than in other bacterial species but aeromonads do not inherently carry the mcr-3 gene. However, our findings showed that the aquatic environment may serve as a reservoir for the dissemination of mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates, which might pass mcr-3 genes to other bacteria. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Identification of isolates. 

Between 1 April 2017 and 30 April 2017, we collected 153 individual rectal swab samples from people who attended the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (Hangzhou, China) for a health examination. Additional samples were collected from swine ( n = 84), chickens ( n = 200), retail pork meat ( n = 34), and retail chicken meat ( n = 32) in and around Hangzhou, and 40 samples were acquired from 5 rivers, 4 lakes, and 1 fountain in the surroundings of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, with 2 samples being collected from each of the two sites at each location ( Fig. 1 ). To identify mcr-3 -positive isolates, all samples were inoculated into alkaline peptone water (Luqiao, Beijing, China) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h without shaking. Aliquots of each broth culture were then boiled to release DNA, which was used as the template for PCR amplification of mcr-3 using specific primers, as described previously ( 3 ). Additional aliquots of the alkaline peptone water broths were then cultured on Salmonella - Shigella agar (Luqiao) plates at 30°C for 18 h. Resulting colonies were selected for further purification, and mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates were confirmed by PCR and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry analysis (Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen, Germany). Species confirmation of the Aeromonas isolates was conducted by carrying out sequencing of partial regions of 16S rRNA, gyrB , and rpoB genes and comparing the sequences against those of Aeromonas species obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), as reported previously ( 20 ). All mcr-3 -positive isolates were also screened for the presence of mcr-1 , mcr-2 , mcr-4 , mcr-5 , mcr-6 , and mcr-7 by shotgun sequencing and comparison of the resulting data against the GenBank database by BLAST analysis. 

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing. 

All mcr-3 -positive isolates were examined for their susceptibility to 10 different antimicrobial agents by using the broth microdilution method. The following antibiotics were examined: colistin, polymyxin B, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, amikacin, meropenem, imipenem, and tetracycline. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute documents M100S ( 21 ) and M45-A2 ( 22 ). E. coli ATCC 25922 served as a quality control strain in all assays. 

Conjugation experiments. 

Conjugation of all mcr-3 -positive isolates was performed by filter mating ( 23 ), using streptomycin-resistant E. coli EC600 (MIC of 1,500 mg/liter), rifampin-resistant E. coli C600 (MIC of 500 mg/liter), sodium azide-resistant E. coli J53 (MIC of 200 mg/liter), and imipenem-resistant A. veronii ZJ12-3 (MIC of 8 mg/liter) as recipient strains. The transconjugants were selected on medium containing colistin, at a concentration consistent with the MIC of the donor, and the appropriate antibiotic for the recipient. Resultant colonies were screened for the presence of mcr-3 by PCR, as described above. 

Genetic analyses. 

Genomic DNA was extracted from each of the mcr-3 -positive Aeromonas isolates by using a Wizard genomic DNA purification kit (Promega, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The genomic DNA was then used to construct indexed DNA libraries, using a KAPA Hyper Prep kit for the Illumina platform (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). All complete libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform, which produced 150-bp paired-end reads (Sinobiocore, Beijing, China). Draft genomes were assembled using SPAdes 3.9.0 ( 24 ). Assembled contigs were analyzed via the Center for Genomic Epidemiology website, to identify plasmid incompatibility types and to screen for the presence of acquired antimicrobial resistance genes ( 25 , 26 ). Virulence-associated genes were detected in the VFDB database ( 27 ). Phylogenetic trees based on the complete genome sequences of the Aeromonas isolates and the specific mcr gene regions were constructed using Harvest ( 28 ) and MEGA 7.0, respectively, using the neighbor-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Results were visualized using the online tool iTOL 3.0 ( 29 ). All sequences used to produce the phylogenetic trees were generated in this study or obtained from the GenBank database (see Table S1 in the supplemental material). Contigs harboring mcr-3 were annotated using the RAST pipeline ( 30 ), following the analysis of flanking regions. 

Statistical analyses. 

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The prevalences of mcr-3 in samples from different sources were compared using a chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, with a significance level of 0.05. 

Accession number(s). 

All sequences have been deposited in GenBank under BioProject PRJNA408193 . The nucleotide sequences of mcr-3.13 to mcr-3.18 have been deposited in GenBank under accession numbers MH332763 to MH332768 .
