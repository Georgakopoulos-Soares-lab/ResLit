                  Metallo-β-lactamases (MβLs) are zinc-dependent enzymes able to hydrolyze and inactivate most β-lactam antibiotics. The large diversity of active site structures and metal content among MβLs from different sources has limited the design of a pan-MβL inhibitor. Here we report the biochemical and biophysical characterization of a novel MβL, GOB-18, from a clinical isolate of a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, Elizabethkingia meningoseptica. Different spectroscopic techniques, three-dimensional modeling, and mutagenesis experiments, reveal that the Zn(II) ion is bound to Asp120, His121, His263, and a solvent molecule, i.e. in the canonical Zn2 site of dinuclear MβLs. Contrasting all other related MβLs, GOB-18 is fully active against a broad range of β-lactam substrates using a single Zn(II) ion in this site. These data further enlarge the structural diversity of MβLs.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara80" view="all">Metallo-β-lactamases (MβLs) are zinc-dependent enzymes able to hydrolyze and inactivate most β-lactam antibiotics. The large diversity of active site structures and metal content among MβLs from different sources has limited the design of a pan-MβL inhibitor. Here we report the biochemical and biophysical characterization of a novel MβL, GOB-18, from a clinical isolate of a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, <ce:italic>Elizabethkingia meningoseptica</ce:italic>. Different spectroscopic techniques, three-dimensional modeling, and mutagenesis experiments, reveal that the Zn(II) ion is bound to Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">121</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">263</ce:sup>, and a solvent molecule, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> in the canonical Zn2 site of dinuclear MβLs. Contrasting all other related MβLs, GOB-18 is fully active against a broad range of β-lactam substrates using a single Zn(II) ion in this site. These data further enlarge the structural diversity of MβLs.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">The expression of β-lactam degrading enzymes (β-lactamases) is the most common mechanism of antibiotic resistance among bacteria (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>). These enzymes have been grouped into four classes (A–D) according to sequence homology (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). Class A, C, and D enzymes use an active site serine residue as a nucleophile, whereas class B lactamases (generically termed metallo-β-lactamases, MβLs)<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="FN10">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>
				              <ce:footnote id="FN10">
                     <ce:label>9</ce:label>
                     <ce:note-para id="cenpara10" view="all">The abbreviations used are: MβL, metallo-β-lactamase; EXAFS, extended x-ray absorption fine structure; MES, 4-morpholineethanesulfonic acid.</ce:note-para>
                  </ce:footnote> employ one or two Zn(II) ions to cleave the β-lactam ring.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">MβLs have particular importance in the clinical setting since they can hydrolyze a broader spectrum of β-lactam substrates than the serine-type enzymes and are resistant to most clinically employed inhibitors (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib5 bib6 bib7 bib8 bib9 bib10 bib11">5–11</ce:cross-refs>). The design of an efficient pan-MβL inhibitor has been mostly limited by a striking diversity in the active site structures, catalytic features, and metal ion requirements for activity among different enzymes. Based on this heterogeneity, MβLs have been classified into three subclasses: B1, B2, and B3 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>). Subclass B1 includes several chromosomally encoded enzymes such as BcII from <ce:italic>Bacillus cereus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib12 bib13 bib14">12–14</ce:cross-refs>), CcrA from <ce:italic>Bacteroides fragilis</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib15 bib16 bib17 bib18">15–18</ce:cross-refs>), BlaB from <ce:italic>Elizabethkingia meningoseptica</ce:italic> (formerly, <ce:italic>Chryseobacterium meningosepticum</ce:italic>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>), as well as the transferable VIM (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>)-, IMP (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>)-, SPM (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>)-, and GIM-type enzymes. Subclass B2 includes the CphA (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>) and ImiS (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>) lactamases from <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> species. Subclass B3, originally represented only by L1 from <ce:italic>Stenotrophomonas maltophilia</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib28 bib29 bib30">28–30</ce:cross-refs>), now includes enzymes from other opportunistic pathogens like FEZ-1 from <ce:italic>Legionella gormanii</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>) and GOB from <ce:italic>E. meningoseptica</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>), as well as from environmental bacteria such as CAU-1 from <ce:italic>Caulobacter crescentus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>) and THIN-B from <ce:italic>Janthinobacterium lividum</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Molecular structures of MβLs from the three subclasses have been solved by x-ray crystallography (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>). Comparison of the tertiary structure of enzymes belonging to the different subclasses reveals a common αβ/βα sandwich fold, in which different insertions and deletions have resulted in different loop topologies and, ultimately, in different zinc coordination environments and metal site occupancies among B1, B2, and B3 enzymes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>). MβLs bind up to two metal ions in their active sites. In B1 and B3 enzymes, one Zn(II) ion (Zn1) is tetrahedrally coordinated to three histidine ligands (His<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">118</ce:sup>, and His<ce:sup loc="post">196</ce:sup> in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>) and a water/OH<ce:sup loc="post">–</ce:sup> molecule (3-H site) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). The coordination polyhedron of Zn2 in B1 enzymes is provided by Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, Cys<ce:sup loc="post">221</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">263</ce:sup>, and one or two-water molecules (DCH site). Notably, this site represents the active species in mono-Zn(II) B2 enzymes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>). Instead, two mutations (Cys<ce:sup loc="post">221</ce:sup> → Ser and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">121</ce:sup> → His) affect the Zn2 coordination geometry in B3 MβLs, and the metal ion is bound to Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">121</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">263</ce:sup>, and one- or two-water molecules, while Ser<ce:sup loc="post">221</ce:sup> is no longer a metal ligand (DHH site) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). A remarkable exception is provided by the deepest branching member of the MβL B3 subclass, GOB from <ce:italic>E. meningoseptica</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). In all reported GOB sequences, His<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> and Ser<ce:sup loc="post">221</ce:sup> are substituted by Gln and Met, respectively, suggesting the presence of an unusually perturbed metal binding site.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para40" view="all">Here we show that <ce:italic>E. meningoseptica</ce:italic> GOB-18 represents a novel type of broad spectrum MβL unique in being maximally active in the mono-Zn(II) form and in which the metal ion occupies only the Zn2, DHH site. This contrasts with two generally accepted ideas: broad spectrum MβLs are maximally active in the dinuclear form (B1 and B3 enzymes), and mono-Zn(II) enzymes are carbapenemases (B2 enzymes). Finally, the findings presented here confirm that Zn2 is central for MβL-mediated catalysis and that the attacking nucleophile could be provided either by a non-metal center or by the Zn2 site. This claims for a revisited strategy for the design of broad spectrum MβL inhibitors.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle20">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Biochemical Characterization of GOB-18</ce:italic>—The gene coding for a GOB-type MβL was cloned from a carpabenem-resistant <ce:italic>E. meningoseptica</ce:italic> clinical strain. Sequence analysis indicated a predicted molecular mass of 31.4 kDa for the encoded protein. At the time of sequencing there were 17 known variants of GOB enzymes. This protein differed from those reported previously and was named GOB-18. This enzyme differed from the firstly reported GOB enzyme, GOB-1 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>), by three conserved mutations: Leu<ce:sup loc="post">94</ce:sup> → Phe, Ala<ce:sup loc="post">137</ce:sup> → Val, and Asp<ce:sup loc="post">282</ce:sup> → Asn (standard consensus numbering) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">Recombinant GOB-18 was overproduced as a fusion to GST in the cytoplasm of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> BL21(DE3) pLysS cells, cleaved, and purified to homogeneity. GOB-18 is a monomeric enzyme according to size exclusion chromatography. Mass spectrometry confirmed the molecular mass expected from the gene sequence. The β-lactamase activity (measured as cefotaxime hydrolysis) was inhibited by EDTA with an IC<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> of 2.3 ± 0.3 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>, indicating that GOB-18 holds a tightly bound divalent cation essential for activity. Inductively coupled plasma and atomic absorption analyses showed that recombinant GOB-18 contained significant amounts of zinc and, notably, iron. Although the relative amounts of these two metals varied among different enzyme preparations (0.45–0.75 iron/GOB-18 and 0.01–0.20 zinc/GOB-18), the total metal content never exceeded one metal ion per protein molecule. Addition of excess Zn(II) did not alter the CD spectrum of GOB-18 as isolated, neither in the near nor in the far UV (data not shown). Overall, these data suggest that GOB-18 is a mono-metallic enzyme, in sharp contrast to the other B3 MβLs.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">The apoprotein was devoid of lactamase activity that could be recovered by addition of Zn(II). Remarkably, apo-GOB-18 remetallated with Zn(II) bound only one equivalent of Zn(II) and its activity largely exceeded that of the enzyme as isolated (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). On the other hand, Fe(II)-GOB-18 and Fe(III)-GOB-18 displayed negligible activities compared with that of Zn(II)-GOB-18. In fact, Fe(III)-GOB-18 was poorly active even upon addition of Zn(II), suggesting that both metal ions compete for the same binding site. The kinetic parameters of Zn(II)-GOB-18, reported in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>, show that mono-Zn(II)-GOB-18 presents a broad substrate spectrum for β-lactams, similarly to that shown by dinuclear B3 enzymes. Addition of 20 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Zn(II) to the reaction medium did not modify the enzyme kinetic parameters.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">The pH dependence of GOB-18-mediated cefotaxime hydrolysis showed a plateau between pH values of 6 and 8, and an acidic limb, resulting in a complete inactivation at pH ≤ 4.5. This loss of activity could be reverted by raising the pH to 6.5, indicating that the inactivation is reversible. Metal content analysis at different pH values showed that the decrease in activity at low pH paralleled the dissociation of Zn(II) from the enzyme, despite the fact that most of the Fe(III) remained bound up to pH 4 (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="ecomp10">supplemental material</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">Overall, these data show that Zn(II)-GOB-18 is the active β-lactamase species. GOB-18 preparations with larger zinc/iron ratios were the most active ones, in line with this observation (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="ecomp10">supplemental material</ce:cross-ref>). Expression of the complete GOB-18 gene (including a transit peptide sequence) in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> results in an enzyme secreted to the periplasm confering β-lactam resistance to the bacterial host. Isoelectrofocusing analysis of the periplasmic fraction after osmotic shock revealed that the enzyme is present exclusively in the Zn(II) form, suggesting that iron uptake could be due to an artifact of protein overexpression in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> cytoplasm.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy</ce:italic>—EXAFS data recorded at the zinc and iron edges, <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3</ce:sup>χ((<ce:italic>k</ce:italic>), and the corresponding Fourier transforms for GOB-18 are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>. EXAFS curve-fitting results are presented as supporting information. Fits to Fourier-filtered first shell scattering (0.5–2.1 Å) in the case of Zn(II)-GOB-18 suggest coordination to 4 nitrogen/oxygen donors at a distance of 2.01 Å (Fit Zn-1). Fit residuals did not improve significantly upon inclusion of a sulfur, or a mixed shell of nitrogen/oxygen. Multiple scattering analysis indicates ligation to 2 histidine residues (Fit Zn-2). The low Debye-Waller factor for the second multiple scattering path (C<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>-N<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>) most likely reflects some rotation of the imidazole plane about the axis normal to the zinc-nitrogen bond. Inclusion of a single zinc-carbon scattering interaction at a distance of 2.71 Å, representing carboxylate carbon scattering, modestly improved the fit (∼15%) (Fit Zn-3).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para270" view="all">In the case of Fe(III)-GOB-18, single shell fits give an average of 5 nitrogen/oxygen scatterers at 2.11 Å (Fit Fe-1). Inclusion of a sulfur atom increased the fit residual. The larger metal-ligand distance is consistent with a higher coordination number for iron relative to zinc. Separate shells of 3 oxygen atoms at 2.04 Å and 2 nitrogen atoms at 2.20 Å could be resolved (Fit Fe-2), and multiple scattering fits indicate the presence of two His ligands (Fit Fe-3). Inclusion of an iron scatterer in fits for Zn(II)-GOB-18 (Fit Zn-4) and a zinc scatterer in fits for Fe(III)-GOB-18 (Fit Fe-3) slightly improved the fits but led to different metal-metal distances (3.61 and 3.55 Å, respectively). This allows us to discard the existence of a heterodimetallic site in GOB-18. Thus, EXAFS data, together with the biochemical studies, suggest that Zn(II) and Fe(III) compete for the only metal binding site in GOB-18. Based on this, we decided to exploit Fe(III) as a spectroscopic probe of the metal site.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para280" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Spectroscopic Characterization of Fe(III)-GOB-18</ce:italic>—The UV-visible spectrum of Fe(III)-GOB-18 revealed an absorption band centered at 375 nm, which was not present in the apoenzyme nor in the Zn(II)-reconstituted form (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="fig4">Fig. 4<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). This feature can be attributed to a typical His-Fe(III) charge transfer band, similar to those observed in lipoxygenase, iron superoxide dismutase, and other non-heme, non-Fe-S iron proteins (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="bib43">43</ce:cross-ref>). EPR spectra of GOB-18 recorded at 6 K and 25 K (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="fig4">Fig. 4<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>) indicated an isolated, “rhombic” (<ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> mean <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>/<ce:italic>D</ce:italic> ∼ <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>/<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>) Fe(III) ion. The almost complete loss of the <ce:italic>M<ce:inf loc="post">S</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> = |±1/2 〉 (or <ce:italic>M<ce:inf loc="post">S</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> = |±5/2 〉) signals at 25 K suggests a zero field splitting, Δ, of less than about 30 cm<ce:sup loc="post">–1</ce:sup>. The asymmetry of the lines at <ce:italic>g</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">eff</ce:inf> ∼ 4.3 and at <ce:italic>g</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">eff</ce:inf> ∼ 9 indicates significant strain in <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>/<ce:italic>D</ce:italic>, although the shoulder on the crossover at <ce:italic>g</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">eff</ce:inf> ∼ 4.3 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="fig4">Fig. 4<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>, <ce:italic>inset</ce:italic>) indicates a lower degree of strain than in the often-seen “adventitious iron” signal and provides strong evidence for the Fe(III) being tightly protein-bound. In contrast to the iron-loaded glyoxylase II (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>), no signals attributable to an Fe(III)-Fe(II) coupled system were observed from GOB-18.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para290" view="all">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>H NMR spectra recorded under conditions that allow the detection of paramagnetic signals revealed a set of isotropically shifted resonances spanning from 100 to 20 ppm (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="fig4">Fig. 4<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>): a broad feature (A), accounting for three resonances centered at 80 ppm, a signal of fractional intensity at 38 ppm (B), and a two-proton resonance at 30 ppm (C). When the spectrum was recorded in D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>O, a loss of intensity corresponding to two protons was noticed in the broad envelope A, revealing the presence of two exchangeable resonances. The broad nature of these resonances is comparable with that reported for mononuclear Fe(III) enzymes such as protocatechuate dioxygenase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>), allowing us to discard the presence of a coupled, di-iron site. The two exchangeable resonances located at ∼80 ppm can be attributed to two His ligands, in agreement with the analysis of the EXAFS data. The broad nature of these signals prevented us to perform one-dimensional or two-dimensional nuclear Overhauser effect spectra to assign them.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para300" view="all">Altogether, EPR, NMR, and EXAFS data allow us to rule out the formation of a dinuclear Fe(III) site in GOB-18, supporting the above assumption that Fe(III) and Zn(II) bind to the same site. EXAFS and NMR reveal the presence of two His ligands at the metal site, with five nitrogen/oxygen ligands for Fe(III). The <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>H NMR spectrum shows only 5–6 resonances corresponding to metal ligands, two of them corresponding to imidazolic His NHs, suggesting that one or two water molecules would be needed to achieve a penta-coordinated site.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para310" view="all">This picture can be accounted for by two possibilities: (i) that the metal ion binds to a modified Site 1 in GOB-18, in which His116 has been replaced by a Gln residue, with His<ce:sup loc="post">118</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">196</ce:sup>, and a H<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>O/OH<ce:sup loc="post">–</ce:sup> moiety completing the coordination sphere or (ii) by placing the metal ion into Site 2 (DHH ligand set), with Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">121</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">263</ce:sup>, and a H<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>O/OH<ce:sup loc="post">–</ce:sup> as metal ligands.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para320" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Three-dimensional Modeling</ce:italic>—Three-dimensional models for Zn(II)-GOB-18 were built by homology modeling from the two available x-ray structures of B3 MβLs: FEZ-1 and L1 (38 and 21% sequence identity, respectively), including metal-ligand constraints based on spectroscopic data. We examined the possibility of binding a single Zn(II) ion to each of the two canonical metal binding sites. Best GOB-18 models for both mono-zinc active site conformations show low backbone root mean square deviation compared with FEZ-1 and L1 (0.6 and 1.5 Å, respectively), suggesting that the protein fold can accommodate a single Zn(II) ion in either binding site. Quantum chemical geometry optimizations based on density functional theory on unconstrained models of these metal sites with water or hydroxide as the fourth ligand suggested a regular tetrahedral geometry for a Zn(II) ion at a modified Site 1 and a distorted tetrahedron for the metal ion bound to Site 2. Predicted averaged zinc-nitrogen/oxygen distances are within ±0.04 Å from the values retrieved from EXAFS data, preventing us from discerning between the two sites (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="ecomp10">see supplemental material for additional “Experimental Procedures”</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para330" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Site-directed Mutagenesis of Putative Metal Ligands</ce:italic>—To unequivocally identify the metal binding site in GOB-18, we constructed mutants of possible metal ligands. In one of them Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, which is conserved in all MβLs as a Zn2 ligand, was changed to Ser; and Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup>, distinctive of GOB enzymes, was replaced by a His. The Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His mutant, in particular, would be expected to provide a 3-H binding site similar to that found in all B1 and in most B3 MβLs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para340" view="all">Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His GOB-18 was purified as a mixture of zinc and iron forms, typically with 0.06–0.70 zinc/protein and 0.20–0.60 iron/protein. Remarkably, the total metal content of this mutant never exceeded one equivalent per protein, as determined for wild-type GOB-18. Apo-Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His GOB-18 remetallated with Zn(II) bound only one equivalent of Zn(II). The kinetic parameters of zinc-Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His GOB-18 showed a partially decreased activity when compared with that of wild-type GOB-18 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Addition of 20 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Zn(II) to the reaction medium gave rise to minor changes in the mutant enzyme activity (data not shown). UV-visible and EXAFS data at the iron edge of holo-Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His GOB-18 were identical to those recorded for the wild-type enzyme. Similarly, the paramagnetic <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>H NMR spectrum of this mutant showed minor perturbations in the Fe(III) ligand signals when compared with that of the wild-type enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="fig4">Fig. 4<ce:italic>C</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The fact that the metal site is largely unperturbed suggests that residue 116 is not a metal ligand neither in wild-type GOB-18 nor in Gln<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> → His GOB-18.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para350" view="all">Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup> → Ser GOB-18 displayed a markedly decreased metal binding ability for both iron and zinc. The iron binding capacity of Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup> → Ser GOB-18 was abolished, and the Zn(II) content reached at most 0.45 Zn(II)/protein (even after exhaustive dialysis against metal containing buffer). Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup> → Ser GOB-18 was inactive toward different assayed antibiotics. These data reveal that Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup> is a metal ligand, being also essential for catalytic efficiency.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para360" view="all">B1 and B2 MβLs have a Cys ligand in the Zn2 site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>). The present three-dimensional model suggests that the only Cys residue present in GOB-18 is far away from the active site. This Cys residue is only accessible to 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) under denaturing conditions. To definitively discard the possibility that this Cys residue may serve as a metal ligand in GOB-18, we constructed a Cys<ce:sup loc="post">201</ce:sup> → Ser mutant. The metal content, catalytic efficiency, and spectroscopic data of Cys<ce:sup loc="post">201</ce:sup> → Ser GOB-18 were similar to those of the wild-type enzyme, indicating the absence of a Cys ligand in the metal binding site, in agreement with the present three-dimensional model and EXAFS data.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para370" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>GOB Enzymes Are Monometallic Lactamases</ce:italic>—The biochemical, spectroscopic, and mutagenesis data herein shown allow us to propose that GOB-18 is a mono-metallic MβL, with the catalytic Zn(II) ion bound to Asp<ce:sup loc="post">120</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">121</ce:sup>, His<ce:sup loc="post">263</ce:sup>, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> the canonical Zn2 site (DHH ligand set in subclass B3) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>). The coordination sphere would be completed by one or two solvent molecules in the Zn(II) and Fe(III) variants, respectively. The presence of a naturally occurring Gln residue rather than a His at position 116 in GOB-18 resembles the situation found for subclass-B2 MβLs, in which an Asn is found (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>). In both cases, the absence of the otherwise conserved His<ce:sup loc="post">116</ce:sup> ligand would induce the loss of the Zn1 site. However, engineering a putative 3-H site in GOB-18 does not lead to a dimetallic enzyme, suggesting additional structural differences in the metal site of GOB-18.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para380" view="all">GOB enzymes differ by a few point mutations, apparently far from the active site. This allows us to confidently extend our results to all GOB lactamases. GOB MβLs thus present features of B2 (being Zn2-only enzymes) and B3 (based on sequence homology) lactamases. However, two major differences of GOB MβLs with B2 enzymes should be noted: (i) GOB is active even in the presence of 0.1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Zn(II), while B2 enzymes are inhibited upon binding of a second Zn(II) equivalent and; (ii) B2 enzymes are selective carbapenemases, while GOB is a broad spectrum MβL. Thus, GOB MβLs are unique in being the only broad spectrum mono-Zn(II) β-lactam hydrolases and in presenting active site mutations within one subclass.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para390" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Toward a General Catalytic Mechanism for Metallo-</ce:italic>β<ce:italic>-Lactamases</ce:italic>—The effect of the Zn(II) content on the enzymatic activity in MβLs is still a controversial issue. The Zn1 (3-H) site present in all B1 and in most B3 enzymes, GOB MβLs being the only exception, has been regarded as the catalytic site providing the attacking zinc-bound nucleophile. As inferred from a series of elegant spectroscopic studies, the Zn2 site (DCH ligand set in B1 enzymes and DHH ligands in B3 lactamases) contributes to β-lactam hydrolysis by binding to the bridging β-lactam nitrogen (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1010" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1020" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1030" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>). The trapping of hydrolyzed moxalactam in the active site of <ce:italic>S. maltophilia</ce:italic> L1 recently confirmed that both Zn1 and Zn2 are implicated in substrate binding and catalysis in B3 MβLs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1040" refid="bib47">47</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para400" view="all">The structure of both the free and product-complexed form of the B2 enzyme CphA revealed only one catalytic Zn(II) ion bound to the DCH site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1050" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>). This metal ion is also involved in stabilization of the negative charge on the bridging nitrogen atom of the β-lactam ring, as in B1 and B3 enzymes. Thus, the Zn2 site would play the same role in all MβLs, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> the stabilization of the negative charge developed at the β-lactam nitrogen to favor cleavage of the C–N bond, and the proton delivery through a water molecule bound to it. This is further stressed by the fact that in all studied cases, the rate-determining step is the C–N bond cleavage (that involves the Zn2 ion) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1060" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1070" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1080" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1090" refid="bib48">48</ce:cross-ref>). This suggests that a strategy aimed for the design of a general inhibitor could be pursued by targeting the Zn2 site.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para410" view="all">The issue that remains unsolved is the identification of the nucleophile in Zn2-only enzymes. The absence of the Zn1 site in B2 lactamases has led to the proposal of a non-metal activated nucleophile, a hypothesis later supported by theoretical and enzymological studies (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1100" refid="bib48">48</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1110" refid="bib49">49</ce:cross-ref>). However, the possibility of a Zn2-bound nucleophile cannot been definitively ruled out. The results herein presented reveal that Zn2-only MβLs are not only restricted to B2 subclass and that they can display a broad substrate spectrum. Therefore, a general mechanism of Zn2-only enzymes needs to be definitively elucidated and compared with those of dinuclear and Zn1-only enzymes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para420" view="all">Several reports have indicated that the mono-Zn(II) variants of B1 and B3 enzymes are active (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1120" refid="bib50">50</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1130" refid="bib51">51</ce:cross-ref>). Since the first solved MβL structure revealed one metal ion bound to the 3-H site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1140" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>), this has been regarded as the catalytic center in monometallic variants. However, spectroscopic data have revealed that when BcII (a well studied B1 lactamases) binds one equivalent of metal ion, it is distributed between the canonical Zn1 and Zn2 sites (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1150" refid="bib52">52</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1160" refid="bib53">53</ce:cross-ref>). These data should be reanalyzed at the light of the present evidence, considering that active mono-Zn2 enzymes may also occur among B1 and B3 enzymes other than GOB.</ce:para>
