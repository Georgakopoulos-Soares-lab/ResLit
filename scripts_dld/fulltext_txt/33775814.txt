                  Objectives
                  To analyse the strains collected during a 1-year survey of ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, in order to investigate the molecular mechanisms potentially responsible for their resistant phenotype.
               
                  Methods
                  Clinical KPC-producing K. pneumoniae isolates were collected from 31 patients in six different hospitals in Rome. For eight of the patients, an additional strain grown before the start of treatment was also available, bringing the total of isolates studied to 39. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by automated system, broth microdiluition and E-test as appropriate. In silico analysis of acquired resistance genes was achieved by whole-genome sequencing, while multilocus sequence typing and core genome multilocus sequence typing were employed for molecular typing. Mutations associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance were identified by Sanger sequencing of the bla
                     KPC gene. Possible mutations in OmpK35 and OmpK36 outer membrane proteins were also investigated.
               
                  Results
                  Molecular analyses highlighted the circulation of the ST512, 101 and 307 high-risk clones; 26 of the 31 patients carried a mutated KPC variant, five had a wild-type KPC-3. Among the KPC variants detected, 11 were different mutations within the bla
                     KPC-3 gene, four of which were novel mutational changes.
               
                  Conclusions
                  Different mutations including single amino-acid substitutions, insertions or deletions within the bla
                     KPC gene were found in 26/31 ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing K. pneumoniae strains belonging to high-risk clones circulating in Italy. Of note, in 14/31 cases the isolates displayed resistance to both ceftazidime-avibactam and carbapenems, raising concerns for the possible selection of a multidrug-resistant phenotype.

                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0015">Objectives</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">To analyse the strains collected during a 1-year survey of ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic>, in order to investigate the molecular mechanisms potentially responsible for their resistant phenotype.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0015" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0020">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0015" view="all">Clinical KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolates were collected from 31 patients in six different hospitals in Rome. For eight of the patients, an additional strain grown before the start of treatment was also available, bringing the total of isolates studied to 39. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by automated system, broth microdiluition and E-test as appropriate. <ce:italic>In silico</ce:italic> analysis of acquired resistance genes was achieved by whole-genome sequencing, while multilocus sequence typing and core genome multilocus sequence typing were employed for molecular typing. Mutations associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance were identified by Sanger sequencing of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene. Possible mutations in OmpK35 and OmpK36 outer membrane proteins were also investigated.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0020" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0025">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0020" view="all">Molecular analyses highlighted the circulation of the ST512, 101 and 307 high-risk clones; 26 of the 31 patients carried a mutated KPC variant, five had a wild-type KPC-3. Among the KPC variants detected, 11 were different mutations within the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> gene, four of which were novel mutational changes.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0025" role="conclusion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0030">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0025" view="all">Different mutations including single amino-acid substitutions, insertions or deletions within the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene were found in 26/31 ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strains belonging to high-risk clones circulating in Italy. Of note, in 14/31 cases the isolates displayed resistance to both ceftazidime-avibactam and carbapenems, raising concerns for the possible selection of a multidrug-resistant phenotype.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:section id="sec1" role="introduction" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0040">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">In recent years, the increasing prevalence of carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> has led to high levels of drug resistance, treatment failure and increased mortality. In Italy, the production of <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> carbapenemases (KPCs) is the predominant mechanism of resistance, and is especially associated with <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0080" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. Since its approval in 2015, avibactam, a broad-spectrum β-lactamase inhibitor active against serine β-lactamases, has been used in association with ceftazidime for treating complicated KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> infections [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0085" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>]. After initial promising results, various KPC variants resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam began to emerge <ce:cross-refs id="optnlFnEpFiYl" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5">[3–5]</ce:cross-refs>. Although increased gene expression of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf>, differences in susceptibility of KPC subtypes or decreased membrane permeability can potentially result in resistance [<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0010" refid="bib6 bib7 bib8">6–8</ce:cross-refs>], the emergence of mutations in <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> seems to be the main factor involved.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">In an effort to understand the mechanisms underlying ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, we designed a study to collect and analyse all KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strains isolated in various hospitals in Rome.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0045">Materials and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Between January 2019 and January 2020, we collected from six hospitals in Rome all single-patient, ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> clinical strains, including naturally resistant isolates such as metallo-β-lactamase producers. Only isolates that were confirmed to be ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> were further characterized. Whenever available, also a previous <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strain susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam, obtained from the same patients, was collected for the study.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">Species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility were confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) and Phoenix system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were obtained by broth microdilution for colistin and ceftazidime-avibactam (Sensititre dried MIC susceptibility system; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA), and by MIC gradient strip for tigecycline (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy). Results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines (2019. v9). Sanger sequencing was performed to identify <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene variants, and to analyse the <ce:italic>mgrB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>ompK35</ce:italic>/<ce:italic>ompK36</ce:italic> genes for colistin resistance and membrane permeability, respectively.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">Genomic DNAs were sequenced on an Ion Torrent GSS5 platform (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All raw reads were submitted to the Sequence Read Archive under the BioProject ID PRJNA649421. Sequence types (STs) were identified by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) (<ce:inter-ref id="intref0010" xlink:href="https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr" xlink:type="simple">https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr</ce:inter-ref>); genetic relationships were investigated by the validated whole-genome sequencing (WGS) -based core genome MLST (cgMLST) scheme v6.0, (Ridom GmbH, Münster, Germany). Based on the defined <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae sensu lato</ce:italic> cgMLST, comprised of 2358 target genes, a gene-by-gene approach was used to compare genomes [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0100" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>]. Genotypically related isolates (distance of ≤15 alleles) were grouped within a single Complex Type. Antimicrobial resistance genes were extracted from the WGS data using the <ce:small-caps>ResFinder</ce:small-caps> v6.0 (<ce:inter-ref id="intref0015" xlink:href="http://www.genomicepidemiology.org" xlink:type="simple">http://www.genomicepidemiology.org</ce:inter-ref>).</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec3" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0050">Statement on ethics</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">This study was performed on anonymized strains grown from clinical samples. Patients' consent was not needed.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec4" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0055">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">Of 76 ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> strains, 45 were metallo-β-lactamase producers, and therefore naturally resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam: 28 were VIM (14 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, 2 <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> and 2 <ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii</ce:italic>) and 17 were NDM (12 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> and 5 <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic>) (not shown). The remaining 31 (designated as ‘R’) were all KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> with a MIC for ceftazidime-avibactam ranging from 16 to ≥256 mg/L. Confirmation of ceftazidime-avibactam treatment before strain isolation was available for 15/31 patients. The study also included eight ceftazidime-avibactam-susceptible KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strains (designated as ‘S’) obtained from eight patients (all treated with ceftazidime-avibactam) before the emergence of resistance. Source of isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility test results are shown in the Supplementary material (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0105" refid="appsec1">Table S1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">Genotyping based on WGS analysis showed that 29 KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> belonged to ST512, seven to ST307 and three to ST101. According to cgMLST results, isolates were categorized as belonging to five different clusters (CT-1 to CT-5). Cluster types (CT) 1 and 2 included 19 and 6 isolates, respectively, all belonging to ST512, CT-3 comprised three isolates belonging to ST101, while four and two isolates grouped into CT-4 and CT-5, respectively, all classified as ST307. Five strains did not cluster into any group. The isolates belonging to CT-1 were obtained from four hospitals, those in CT-4 were from two different hospitals, while CT-2, CT-3 and CT-5 comprised isolates collected from a single hospital each (see Supplementary material, <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0110" refid="dfig1">Figure S1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene was detected in all isolates, either as a single resistance determinant (32 isolates) or in association with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-15</ce:inf> (in seven strains), all of these belonging to ST307 (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0115" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). BLAST analysis of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> revealed the presence of wild-type KPC-3 enzymes not only in the eight ceftazidime-avibactam-susceptible strains, as expected, but also in five of the 31 ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant isolates. The remaining 26 ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant strains harboured 11 KPC variants putatively associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, four of which were new. Of these newly identified mutations, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-62</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-63</ce:inf> comprised single nucleotide substitutions (T to A and A to C), which caused leucine/glutamine and tyrosine/serine amino acid changes at positions 169 (L169Q) and 241 (Y241S), respectively. One- and two-amino-acid insertions were identified in <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-64</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-65</ce:inf>: serine at position 181 plus amino acid treonine/isoleucine changes at position 244 (S181ins + T244A) for <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-64</ce:inf>, isoleucine and proline at position 180 (T180_Y181ins) for <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-65</ce:inf>. Of the known mutations linked to ceftazidime-avibactam resistance listed in <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0120" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>, we identified by <ce:italic>in silico</ce:italic> analysis mostly KPC-29 and KPC-31 (6/26 each) followed by KPC-46 (3/26), KPC-39 and KPC-53 (both 2/26 isolates), KPC-8 and KPC-28 (1/26 isolate each). The new KPC-62 variant was detected in two isolates, KPC-63, KPC-64 and KPC-65 (also new) in one isolate each.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">Insertion sequences (IS) in the <ce:italic>mgrB</ce:italic> chromosome gene (IS5-like element IS<ce:italic>Kpn</ce:italic>26) of five isolates and one point mutation (causing a premature stop-codon) could explain colistin resistance in six strains (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0125" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">In accordance with literature data [<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0015" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5">3–5</ce:cross-refs>,<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0020" refid="bib10 bib11 bib12">10–12</ce:cross-refs>], the KPC variants displayed different susceptibilities to carbapenems: KPC-8, KPC-29 and KPC-39 (10/31 isolates) were resistant or intermediate to carbapenems, while KPC-28, -31, -46, -52, -62, -63, -64 and -65 (17/31 isolates), showed decreased carbapenem MICs (see Supplementary material, <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0130" refid="appsec1">Table S1</ce:cross-ref>). To investigate membrane permeability we analysed the <ce:italic>ompK35</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>ompK36</ce:italic> porin genes. Ninety-four per cent (37/39) and 74% (29/39) of KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolates harboured non-functional <ce:italic>ompK35</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>ompK36</ce:italic> genes, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0135" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec5" role="discussion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0060">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">Following the introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam in clinical practice for the treatment of KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, reports of resistance began to emerge, mainly due to mutations in the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene [<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0025" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5 bib6">3–6</ce:cross-refs>]. The omega loop of the KPC enzyme in particular (a conserved region surrounding the active site, at amino-acid residues 164–179) and two additional regions (one close to the hinge-loop at amino acids 240–243 and one covering amino acids 263–277) seem to be ‘hot spots’ with a high frequency of mutations [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0140" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0145" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>]. To date, 79 variants of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene have been reported, 31 of which are ceftazidime-avibactam -resistant (<ce:inter-ref id="intref0020" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/isolates#/refgene/KPC" xlink:type="simple">https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/isolates#/refgene/KPC</ce:inter-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">In this paper, we describe the results of a 1-year molecular analysis of all ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant clinical isolates collected from six hospitals in Rome. The strains studied, all belonging to the high-risk STs 512, 101 and 307, were found to harbour 11 of the 30 known mutations conferring resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam, and four new mutants: KPC-62 to KPC-65 (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0150" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). No correlations were found between the distribution of these KPC variants and any particular hospital cluster (see Supplementary material, <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0155" refid="appsec1">Table S2</ce:cross-ref>), suggesting that the different mutants can emerge independently during antibiotic treatment.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">Surprisingly, we found that five isolates with wild-type KPC-3 enzymes were also ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant; we therefore analysed our strains for porin gene content, as several reports indicated that porin deficiency may also contribute to decreased susceptibility to ceftazidime-avibactam in KPC-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0160" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0165" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>]. No significant differences in mutations in the two major membrane proteins were observed among our isolates, suggesting the involvement of other mechanisms (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0170" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). It has been postulated that resistance could result from an increased <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> gene expression (due to a higher copy number of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf>-carrying plasmids) in combination with decreased membrane permeability [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0175" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>]. An important limitation of our study is that we did not evaluate KPC expression, which could have explained the ceftazidime-avibactam resistance observed in those wild-type KPC-3-producing isolates. Moreover, we could not gather enough clinical information regarding the antibiotic treatment of 16 of the 31 patients studied. However, the fact that most of our strains came from patients with complicated infections and that in eight cases a pre-treatment, ceftazidime-avibactam-susceptible strain was collected, are strong suggestions of a selective pressure exerted by ceftazidime-avibactam (see Supplementary material, <ce:cross-ref id="crosref0180" refid="appsec1">Table S1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">In conclusion, our findings illustrate the potential molecular complexity of ceftazidime-avibactam resistance that can emerge in different clones of <ce:italic>K. pneumonaie</ce:italic> carrying different mutations. In accordance with literature data [<ce:cross-refs id="crosrefs0030" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5 bib6">3–6</ce:cross-refs>], for 8/11 (72%) KPC variants found in 17/31 isolates, ceftazidime-avibactam resistance was associated with reversion or decreased carbapenem MICs (<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0185" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>). Although we recorded 100% susceptibility for tigecyline and a lower rate (15.3%) of colistin resistance than previously observed in Italian hospitals (almost 40%) [<ce:cross-ref id="crosref0190" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], it is alarming that 14/31 isolates were found to harbour not only KPC variants, but also wild-type KPC-3 with a highly resistant phenotype, i.e. resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam and carbapenems. Our findings highlight the need for a closer surveillance also at the molecular level to monitor the emergence of resistance and prevent the spread of multidrug-resistant variants that could lead to the loss of current or new antibiotic associations.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
