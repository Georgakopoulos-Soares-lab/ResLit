ABSTRACT 

Infections caused by ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant P. aeruginosa infections are an emerging concern. We aimed to analyze the underlying ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance mechanisms in all multidrug-resistant or extensively drug-resistant (MDR/XDR) P. aeruginosa isolates recovered during 1 year (2020) from patients with a documented P. aeruginosa infection. Fifteen isolates showing ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance were evaluated. Clinical conditions, previous positive cultures, and β-lactams received in the previous month were reviewed for each patient. MICs were determined by broth microdilution. Multilocus sequence types (MLSTs) and resistance mechanisms were determined using short- and long-read whole-genome sequencing (WGS). The impact of Pseudomonas - derived cephalosporinases (PDCs) on β-lactam resistance was demonstrated by cloning into an ampC -deficient PAO1 derivative (PAOΔC) and construction of 3D models. Genetic support of acquired β-lactamases was determined in silico from high-quality hybrid assemblies. In most cases, the isolates were recovered after treatment with ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam. Seven isolates from different sequence types (STs) owed their β-lactam resistance to chromosomal mutations and all displayed specific substitutions in PDC: Phe121Leu and Gly222Ser, Pro154Leu, Ala201Thr, Gly214Arg, ΔGly203-Glu219, and Glu219Lys. In the other eight isolates, the ST175 clone was overrepresented (6 isolates) and associated with IMP-28 and IMP-13, whereas two ST1284 isolates produced VIM-2. The cloned PDCs conferred enhanced cephalosporin resistance. The 3D PDC models revealed rearrangements affecting residues involved in cephalosporin hydrolysis. Carbapenemases were chromosomal (VIM-2) or plasmid-borne (IMP-28, IMP-13) and associated with class-1 integrons located in Tn402-like transposition modules. Our findings highlighted that cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitors are potential selectors of MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa strains producing PDC variants or metallo-β-lactamases. Judicious use of these agents is encouraged. 

KEYWORDS: Pseudomonas aeruginosa , antibiotic resistance, beta-lactamases, beta-lactams, cephalosporin, mechanisms of resistance 

INTRODUCTION 

The World Health Organization identified Pseudomonas aeruginosa as a multidrug-resistant “critical priority” pathogen against which new therapeutic and infection control strategies are urgently needed ( 1 ). This emerging threat is the result of a complex interplay between the ability of P. aeruginosa to potentiate its levels of antimicrobial resistance through the selection of mutations in chromosomal genes, the increasing prevalence of horizontally transferred broad-spectrum resistance mechanisms (e.g., carbapenemases and extended-spectrum β-lactamases [ESBLs]), and the ongoing irruption of high-risk clones in hospitals worldwide ( 2 – 4 ). 

Given the scarcity of new therapeutic options, the arrival of the recently developed cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and ceftazidime-avibactam, has cleared the way for the β-lactams to counterattack in the fight against MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa infections ( 5 ). The high antipseudomonal activity of these new combinations is based on their increased stability against the most important chromosomally encoded P. aeruginosa resistance mechanisms ( 6 , 7 ). Ceftolozane is a novel oxyimino amino thiazolyl cephalosporin containing a large pyrazole moiety at position 3 that confers enhanced resistance against Pseudomonas AmpC (also known as Pseudomonas - derived cephalosporinase [PDC]) hydrolysis. Moreover, ceftolozane-tazobactam MICs are not affected by overexpression of efflux pumps such as mexAB-oprM , mexXY, or by alterations in the OprD porin ( 8 ). Ceftazidime-avibactam combines the classic antipseudomonal ceftazidime with a first-in-class diazabicyclooctane-type β-lactamase inhibitor that confers protection to its partner against strains showing high levels of ampC expression ( 9 ). The combination with avibactam also expands the therapeutic coverage of ceftazidime against P. aeruginosa strains whose β-lactam resistance is due to the production of certain class A β-lactamases, such as GES- or VEB-like enzymes, which are increasingly found in MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa strains worldwide ( 10 ). 

In recent large-scale surveys performed in Spain, ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam were the most active β-lactams available for the treatment of P. aeruginosa infections, with susceptibility rates of around 97% ( 11 ). Metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), which can inactivate most of the antipseudomonal β-lactams and are recalcitrant to inhibition by commercially available β-lactamase inhibitors, appears to be an increasing cause of primary resistance to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam in our country ( 12 ). These enzymes are frequently colocated with aminoglycoside modifying enzymes on class-1 integrons embedded in plasmids and/or transposons that can promote their mobilization ( 13 ). The alarming association between MBLs and epidemic high-risk clones, such as ST235 or ST111, may accelerate their spread ( 14 ). Meanwhile, the evolution of intrinsic β-lactamase PDC, leading to variants with enhanced cephalosporin hydrolysis that confer cross-resistance to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam, has been recently recognized as the main cause of within-patient resistance development when these agents are used to combat MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa infections ( 15 ). As a result of specific rearrangements of the PDC Ω-loop architecture, these treatment-emergent mutants typically exhibit enhanced cephalosporin MICs but increased susceptibility to other antipseudomonals, such as imipenem and piperacillin ( 15 ). Changes at position Glu219Lys are those most frequently associated with this phenotype in P. aeruginosa ( 16 ), although substitutions at other remote locations have also been noted to be involved in this phenotype ( 17 ). 

To date, previous work dealing with ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in P. aeruginosa has mostly been focused on the evaluation of the susceptibility rates of both agents against clinical isolates from different clinical contexts and epidemiological areas. Moreover, some recent works have alerted the impact of previous antibiotic exposure in the selection of resistance to these agents ( 18 , 19 ). Thus, we aimed to shed light on the multiple resistance mechanisms that can be selected for and lead to the acquisition of resistance to these agents, and the influence, if any, of previous infections and treatments on the selection of these mechanisms. 

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Resistance phenotypes, molecular epidemiology, and mechanisms of β-lactam resistance. 

During the study period, a total of 15 isolates showing resistance to ceftolozane-tazobactam and/or ceftazidime-avibactam were prospectively collected. Eleven out of the total 15 isolates (73.3%) were recovered from patients that had been previously exposed to ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam. The isolates were grouped in two clusters according to demographic parameters, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and underlying mechanisms of β-lactam resistance. Seven of these isolates ( Table 1 ) were recovered from respiratory samples of patients with different active respiratory diseases (cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia), infection of cardiac implantable devices, spinal cord injuries, and heart failure who had a previous infection caused by P. aeruginosa susceptible to ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam (not available for analysis) and were treated in all cases with these agents (7/7, 100%). The length of treatment ranged from 14 to 21 days with an average duration of 16 days. In most cases, the isolates were clonally unrelated and belonged to unique genotypes, including the ST175 clone, which is widespread in Spain and France and frequently highly resistant to β-lactams due to a set of distinctive chromosomal mutations involving AmpR (Asp135Asn), MexZ (Gly195Glu) and OprD (Gln142) ( 20 ), and STs 260, 313, 560, 1716 and a new ST designated 3611. In addition to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, these isolates exhibited high ticarcillin, ceftazidime, cefepime, and aztreonam MICs, but in most cases conserved susceptibility to imipenem, aminoglycosides, and colistin. This whole set of isolates also exhibited susceptibility toward the recently developed antipseudomonal combination imipenem/relebactam with MICs falling in all cases below 1 mg/liter, and to cefiderocol, which demonstrated high activity against all but one isolate (MIC = 16 mg/liter). 

Analysis of the mechanisms underlying the resistance phenotypes of these isolates revealed the presence of a plethora of chromosomal mutations involved in β-lactam resistance, including (i) alterations in the porin OprD, involved in carbapenem uptake, and in the transpeptidase domain of PBP-3 (Arg504Cys, frequently found in high-level cephalosporin-resistant cystic fibrosis strains and present in the isolate showing a cefiderocol MIC of 16 mg/liter, and Ile524Thr, a previously undescribed PBP-3 mutation); (ii) frameshifts in mexAB-oprM ( mexR , nalD ) and mexXY ( mexZ ) negative regulators, and (iii) inactivating/gain-of-function mutations in the genes coding for ampC regulators ampD or ampR . Indeed, consistent with the high cephalosporin MICs and the multiple mutations found in the ampC regulators, comparative RT-PCR experiments targeting the ampC gene revealed high levels of ampC upregulation in all isolates, thus confirming AmpC overproduction. Further examination revealed that all these isolates exhibited specific amino acid substitutions in the PDC protein: Phe121Leu + Gly222Ser, Pro154Leu, Ala201Thr, Gly214Arg, ΔGly203-Glu219, and Glu219Lys. Some of these substitutions have previously been found to confer enhanced cephalosporin resistance and are effectively selected during/after exposure to ceftolozane-tazobactam (e.g., ΔGly203-Glu219, Glu219Lys) ( 21 ) but others had not been reported previously (e.g., Ala201Thr). Taken together, these findings suggest that resistance to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam was selected in vivo after targeted therapy with these agents and was likely caused by the accumulation of multiple chromosomal mutations in combination with stable overexpression of mutated bla PDC alleles. 

The eight remaining isolates ( Table 2 ) were obtained from blood, abdominal fluid, urine, rectal swabs, and respiratory samples. Four of the isolates (8,11,13, and 14) were recovered from patients with a previous infection by P. aeruginosa susceptible to ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam that was targeted with these agents (4/8, 50%) during a period that ranged from 5 to 12 days with an average length of 9 days. The other four isolates were recovered from patients without previous microbiological data ( 10 , 12 ) or with a history of ceftolozane-tazobactam-resistant P. aeruginosa infection but treated with other antipseudomonals, including piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, and meropenem. In contrast with the previous group of isolates, which consisted of multiple sequence types, here the ST175 was overrepresented and grouped all except two isolates but that were assigned to the ST1284 clone. A comprehensive analysis of the resistance phenotypes revealed high levels of resistance to ticarcillin, cephalosporins, carbapenems, imipenem/relebactam, and aminoglycosides, but susceptibility to piperacillin-tazobactam, aztreonam, and colistin. Cefiderocol demonstrated high in vitro potency against this second group of isolates, with MICs ranging in all cases between 0.25 to 0.5 mg/liter. 

Genomic analysis failed to identify any mutation-driven mechanisms responsible for ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, although analysis of horizontally acquired resistance determinants revealed the presence of class B carbapenemases: VIM-2 in the two ST1284 isolates, one IMP-13 (IMP-2 cluster), and five IMP-28-type (IMP-1 cluster) enzymes in the ST175 isolates. These findings were partially unexpected: although a recent nationwide survey conducted in Spain found that subclass B1 MBLs, such as VIM and IMP-type enzymes were the most frequent cause of combined resistance to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam ( 11 ), selection of treatment-emergent infections caused MBL-producing P. aeruginosa after exposure to these agents has not been documented previously. Of note, the IMP-28 carbapenemase was biochemically characterized by our group almost 10 years ago and its presence outside Klebsiella oxytoca species has not so far been described ( 22 ), whereas IMP-13 has recently been identified in Spain linked to the P. aeruginosa ST27 clone ( 12 ). The implications of these findings are further enhanced by the association of both these enzymes with the ST175 clone, which has demonstrated an overwhelming potential for intrahospital and interhospital dissemination and has become widely disseminated in Spanish hospitals ( 23 ). Based on the data presented here, it is tempting to suggest that previous treatment with broad-spectrum β-lactams, including cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, may potentially select MBL-producing P. aeruginosa infections if the patient has a history of infection/colonization by MBL-positive P. aeruginosa or if epidemic strains (such as the ST175 clone) with these mechanisms of resistance are circulating in the patient´s environment. 

Genetic and structural characterization of PDC-variants responsible for resistance to cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. 

A closer look at the structural modifications identified in the PDC enzymes of the isolates showing mutation-driven resistance ( Table 1 ) revealed that they were, except for Phe121Leu and Pro154Leu, located in the Ω-loop. This region is conserved throughout AmpC β-lactamases and is particularly prone to harbor amino acid changes that are likely to alter the natural resistance of P. aeruginosa to β-lactams ( 24 ). This loop is located at the entrance to the active site, isolating the key conserved motifs Tyr-Ser-Asn, Ser-Val-Ser-Lys, and Lys-Thr-Gly from the solvent environment and interacting with the R1 side chains of the cephalosporins ( Fig. 1A ) ( 25 , 26 ). To determine the precise role of these variants on β-lactam resistance, they were cloned in parallel with the WT bla PDC-1 gene from PAO1 in an isogenic ampC- deficient derivative of PAO1 (PAOΔC) ( Table 3 ). Structural models of these variants were constructed to decipher in atomic detail the impact of these amino acid changes on the architecture of the PDC enzyme ( Fig. 2 ).

Complementation with the mutated bla PDC alleles resulted in a 2- to 8-fold, 2- to 16-fold and 8- to 128-fold increases in the MICs of ceftazidime, ceftazidime-avibactam, and ceftolozane-tazobactam, respectively, confirming that resistance to ceftazidime and cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations was, in all cases, caused by modifications of the intrinsic PDC enzyme. Substitutions present in PDC-221 (Glu219Lys) and PDC-223 (ΔGly203-Glu219) conferred the greatest increase in cephalosporin resistance, as well as a marked reduction on piperacillin and imipenem MICs. These variants were first described by Fraile-Ribot et al. ( 21 ) in within-patient-selected ceftolozane-tazobactam-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates, and, in both cases, the modification affected or included the key residue Glu219. Their biochemical properties in relation to PDC-1 were further analyzed by Slater et al. ( 27 ), who demonstrated a >10-fold increase in the catalytic efficiency of both variants for ceftolozane. Of note, the deletion of 19 amino acids in PDC-223 was by far the most striking change in the overall structure of the parental PDC enzyme ( Fig. 2A ) and resulted in a large and deep cavity adjacent to the catalytic center. This architecture likely facilitates access of the cephalosporin to the active center, as well as of water molecules for final hydrolysis of the acyl-enzyme adduct. In the case of PDC-221, the Glu219 residue located on the H7 helix of the Ω-loop is replaced by a positively charged lysine residue ( Fig. 2B ). This change would lead to the destabilization of a salt bridge between residues Asp219 (Ω-loop) and Lys127 observed in the parental PDC-1 through competition with Lys221 for this electrostatic interaction. As a result, reorganization of the Lys127-Asp219-Lys221 electrostatic interactions and rigidization of the Ω-loop is expected. 

Similarly, the expression of PDC-477 (Ala201Thr) and PDC-482 (Gly214Arg) also conferred significant levels of cephalosporin resistance. However, compared with PDC-1, overproduction of these enzymes was coupled with the greatest decrease in the MIC of piperacillin-tazobactam, which fell within the susceptibility range for both clinical isolates and transformants. Ala201Thr represented a previously undescribed amino acid change capable of increasing AmpC-mediated cephalosporin resistance. The role of Gly214 residue in cephalosporin resistance has, on the other hand, been documented previously in isolates recovered from patients not previously exposed to ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam, appearing as a single amino acid substitution (Gly214Arg) or as part of larger deletions ( 17 , 28 , 29 ). At the atomic level, both the Ala201Thr and the Gly214Arg substitutions occur in the Ω-loop. In the case of PDC-477, the replacement of Ala201 (arginine, neutral) by a threonine (polar) residue would lead to reduced Ω-loop flexibility by establishing hydrogen bonding interactions with the Ala46 and Asn342 residues in the vicinity ( Fig. 2C ). In the case of PDC-482, the substitution of a neutral residue (Gly214) by a positively charged arginine would induce strong hydrogen bonds with Val125, Asp123, and Gln120 residues, all of them located in the long loop connecting the H4 and H3 helices and located above the Tyr-Ser-Asn motif containing the catalytic tyrosine Tyr151 ( Fig. 2D ). 

Compared with the other variants, overexpression of bla PDC-478 and bla PDC-483 conferred a slightly different resistance profile when overexpressed in PAOΔC. Their impact on the MIC of ceftolozane-tazobactam was significantly lower, although they were not associated with collateral susceptibility to piperacillin or carbapenems, highlighting that the moderate increase in cephalosporin resistance observed in these variants is not detrimental to the ability of the PDC enzyme to inactivate other natural substrates. Of note, PDC-478 and PDC-483 showed two different but close amino acid substitutions outside the Ω-loop: Phe121Leu and Pro154Leu. Substitution of planar and conformationally restricted residues (Phe121, Pro154) by a branched and more conformationally flexible residue (leucine) is likely to induce structural adjustments in the active site affecting the natural conformation of the loop that connects the H4 and H3 helices ( Fig. 2E and F ). 

Genetic platforms associated with mobilization of bla VIM and bla IMP carbapenemase genes. 

Class B carbapenemases represent a growing cause of carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa but also an unresolved challenge to the recently developed cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations ( 30 ). Acquisition of carbapenemases has been identified as one of the possible factors underlying the success of P. aeruginosa high-risk clones ( 31 ). Thus, to gain an in-depth perspective of the genetic contributors driving the mobilization of MBL-encoding genes found in this study isolates carrying MBLs were subjected to hybrid whole-genome sequencing. Interestingly, analysis of the high-quality assemblies obtained by combining long-read and short-read WGS data revealed that bla IMP-13 and bla IMP-28 genes in the ST175 isolates were plasmid-borne. The plasmid structures had not been described before and were designated pJAS ( bla IMP-13 ) and pCHUAC( bla IMP-28 ). In contrast, the bla VIM-2 gene was embedded in a 6,345,465 bp contig, which matched the chromosome of the ST1284 isolates, an MBL-clone association recently described in Portugal ( 32 ). 

Analysis of the pJAS and pCHUAC plasmids revealed that they had a different overall structure ( Fig. 3A ) but shared common features. The pJAS plasmid carrying bla IMP-13 was 36,898 bp in length, comprised 49 open reading frames (ORFs), and showed an average GC content of 60.4%. It showed ∼60% homology with the pMATVIM-7 plasmid, previously known to carry bla VIM-7 in P. aeruginosa ( 33 ). The pCHUAC plasmid was 71,715 bp in length, comprised 90 open reading frames, and had an average GC content of 58.5%, although its plasmid backbone was distantly related from any plasmid previously known to support carbapenemase genes in P. aeruginosa . However, it showed ∼59% identity with IMP-carrying plasmids from Pseudomonas putida and Pseudomonas mosselii , suggesting that this plasmid was acquired from an environmental reservoir ( 34 ). Both plasmids had a poorly delimited core region, which in both cases included incomplete conjugative transfer gene machinery and a mercury resistance operon. The absence of a complete conjugation system suggests that these plasmids were acquired using some other transmission mechanism, such as transduction, transformation, membrane vesicles, or the help of other self-transmissible plasmids. Finally, a detailed analysis of the region surrounding the MBL-encoding genes revealed that they were embedded in all cases with aminoglycoside modifying enzymes within class-1 integrons in Tn402-like transposition modules. As illustrated in Fig. 3B , the presence of the classic inverted repeats (IRL and IRR) on the boundaries indicates that bla IMP-13 , bla IMP-28 , and bla VIM-2 genes were probably acquired as a result of en bloc transposition of Tn402-like transposons in the corresponding plasmid ( bla IMP-13 , bla IMP-28 ) or chromosomal scaffolds ( bla VIM-2 ).

Concluding remarks. 

Most previous work on the mechanisms leading to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance is exclusively focused on the characterization of variants of intrinsic (PDC) or horizontally acquired β-lactamases (OXA, GES) that emerge in vivo in patients exposed to these agents ( 35 – 37 ). Here, we provide a deeper perspective, examining in detail the various resistance mechanisms that could potentially be selected in a real-world scenario, and considering the effect of clinical condition, previous infection, and treatment. 

The first group of isolates evaluated in this work adds further evidence of the role of the naturally occurring PDC enzyme in making P. aeruginosa refractory to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam. In all cases, the different PDC variants were identified in isolates from patients previously exposed for at least 2 weeks to these agents due to previous ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam-susceptible MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa infection. Thus, it is tempting to hypothesize that the resulting ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam resistance observed here emerged during or after therapy, highlighting the major pressure that cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations exert on the evolution of β-lactamase-mediated resistance in P. aeruginosa . Indeed, the transformants and 3D models presented add important mechanistic details about the considerable ability of PDCs to adapt and enhance ceftolozane hydrolysis through specific conformational changes in regions involved in cephalosporin recognition. More importantly, expression of the variants showing Ω-loop alterations and the highest cephalosporin MICs were coupled with impaired piperacillin-tazobactam and imipenem resistance, thus alerting of the potential interest of developing innovative therapeutic regimens, such as those, including cephalosporin/carbapenem antibiotic rotations to overcome mutational resistance in P. aeruginosa . 

Another finding of concern is the impact of cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations on the selection of MDR/XDR MBL-producing strains. Indeed, despite different antipseudomonal β-lactams and other antimicrobials such as aminoglycosides may exert on the selection of isolates producing this kind of resistance mechanism, a high proportion (4/8) of MBL-producing strains detected here were identified in patients previously treated for at least 5 days with ceftolozane-tazobactam. This finding may alert of the potential collateral damage that these antimicrobial combinations may exert on the microbial ecology if they are not used judiciously for the treatment of P. aeruginosa infections or as carbapenem-sparing regimens in the treatment of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales . From a therapeutic point of view, the good news is that the recently approved antipseudomonal combinations cefiderocol and imipenem/relebactam retained stability against all isolates showing PDC- (cefiderocol and imipenem/relebactam) or MBL-mediated resistance (cefiderocol) to ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam, thus demonstrating that they are excellent additions to our currently available antipseudomonal arsenal. 

Finally, our findings also add the plasmid-encoded IMP-28 (previously undescribed in P. aeruginosa ) and IMP-13 to the growing list of MBLs detected in the ST175 lineage, providing further evidence of the role of mobile genetic elements and carbapenemases probably were acquired from environmental sources as a factor contributing to the success of the P. aeruginosa high-risk clones in the nosocomial environment. Taken together, our findings underscore the interplay between previous β-lactam treatment, mutational and horizontally acquired mechanisms, and resistance to cephalosporin/β-lactamase inhibitors in P. aeruginosa and call for active surveillance to be maintained on emerging broad-spectrum resistance in this continuously evolving pathogen. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Bacterial isolates. 

A total of 15 ceftolozane-tazobactam and/or ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates recovered from samples obtained from patients admitted to the Complexo Hospitalario Universitario de A Coruña (Spain) with a clinically documented P. aeruginosa infection between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2020 were evaluated. Only one isolate per patient was considered. Underlying clinical conditions, sample type, previous positive microbiological cultures, and β-lactams received in the previous month were reviewed for each patient. In addition, for those patients that had been treated with either ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam, the length of treatment was also recorded. 

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing. 

MICs of ticarcillin, piperacillin + tazobactam (4 mg/liter), aztreonam, ceftazidime, ceftazidime + avibactam (4 mg/liter), ceftolozane + tazobactam (4 mg/liter), cefepime, cefiderocol, imipenem, imipenem + relebactam (4 mg/liter), meropenem, ciprofloxacin, tobramycin, amikacin, and colistin were determined in triplicate experiments using the reference broth microdilution method. MICs were determined in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) except for cefiderocol, for which iron-depleted CAMHB was used. Categorical interpretations applied were the clinical breakpoints and guidelines (v 11.0) of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), which are available on their website. 

WGS. 

Total genomic DNA was extracted from all isolates using a Genomic DNA Buffer Set with a Genomic-Tip 20/G (Qiagen). The DNA was quantified with the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Illumina indexed paired-end libraries were generated from a TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep kit (Illumina). The MinION libraries were constructed using a commercial SQK-RBK004 Rapid Barcoding kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Purified DNA fragments were sequenced in parallel using short (Illumina NovaSeq, Illumina) and long-read (MinION, Oxford Nanopore Technologies) approaches. Resultant long and short reads from each isolate were preprocessed with Trimmomatic v.0.39 for short reads and Filtlong v.0.2.0 for long reads and subsequently assembled using the Unicycler hybrid v.0.4.8 hybrid assembler using default settings ( 38 ). The assemblies were annotated with Prokka and the final annotations obtained for each predicted open reading frame were curated manually using BLASTn and BLASTp similarity searches ( https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ) ( 39 ). 

Molecular typing. 

Clonal relatedness of the isolates was investigated by MLST. Sequence types were inferred from assembled genomes according to the allelic profiles available in the MLST Database ( http://pubmlst.org/paeruginosa ). New allelic profiles identified in this study were curated by the PubMLST staff and deposited in the MLST databases. 

Resistance genomics. 

The mutational resistome of the isolates was investigated following previously described protocols. Briefly, the reads obtained for each isolate were mapped to the genome of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 reference strain and sequence variations for the 146 chromosomal genes associated with antimicrobial resistance were examined, as previously described ( 37 ). The presence of horizontally acquired resistance genes was determined using Resfinder ( https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk//services/ResFinder/ ) and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) web tools ( https://card.mcmaster.ca/home ). 

Determination of ampC expression. 

In isolates lacking horizontally acquired β-lactamases, ampC expression was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using DNA-free RNA preparations. Primers, probes, and instruments used for qRT-PCR purposes were as described in a previous study ( 15 ). Relative quantification of target genes was performed in triplicate, considering ≥5-fold difference in transcription levels compared to P. aeruginosa PAO1 as significantly different. 

Cloning experiments. 

Mutated bla PDC genes of the clinical isolates were cloned into the broad-host-range pUCP24 shuttle vector. Primers targeting the entire coding sequence of the bla PDC gene were: PDC-F-EcoRI ( 5′-TCGAATTCACGACAAAGGACGCCAATCC-3′ ) and PDC-R-HindIII ( 5′-CCCAAGCTTGGGACCATCATAGCCAGGACCGG-3′ ). Recombinant pUCP24-PDC plasmids were transformed into E. coli TG1. Recombinant plasmids were subsequently extracted from E. coli TG1 and electroporated into the PAO1 ampC knockout mutant (PAOΔC). The selection was performed on 30 mg/liter gentamicin-LB agar plates. The β-lactam MICs for all the transformants were determined following the above-described methodology. 

Construction of PDC homology models. 

The homology models of PDC-221 (Glu219Lys), PDC-223 (ΔGly203-Glu219), PDC-477 (Ala201Thr), PDC-478 (Phe121Leu, Gly222Ser), PDC-482 (Gly214Arg), and PDC-483 (Pro154Leu) were obtained using the SWISS-MODEL Homology Server ( 40 ). The crystal structure of the PDC-1 β-lactamase from P. aeruginosa PAO1 covalently modified by a monobactam derivative (PDC 2WZZ ( 41 ), 1.57 Å) was used as a template for model construction. Figures representing the major rearrangements present in the different variants were prepared using PYMOL ( 42 ). 

Genetic context of metallo-β-lactamases. 

High-quality hybrid assemblies obtained following the methodology described above yielded circularized contigs, which corresponded to complete closed chromosomal or plasmid structures. Bandage software was used for the visualization of contigs ( 43 ). Schematic representations of the plasmids and genetic environments surrounding the MBL genes were performed using Geneious (Biomatters, USA). 

Nucleotide accession numbers. 

The WGS data of the isolates sequenced in this work were deposited in the NCBI BioProject Database under accession number PRJNA523008 . The nucleotide sequences of the new bla PDC alleles and plasmids described in this work were deposited in the GenBank database under the following accession numbers: bla PDC-477 ( MW672263 ), bla PDC-478 ( MW672264 ), bla PDC-482 ( MZ067233.1 ), bla PDC-483 ( MZ067234 ), pJAS ( MZ673648 ), and pCHUAC ( MZ581331 ).
