                  Bacteria harbouring the novel qnrA plasmid-mediated mechanism of quinolone resistance have been described in different countries, but the frequency of their occurrence has not been investigated. In total, 1468 clinical isolates of Enterobacteriaceae with quinolone resistance or extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) phenotypes were collected from eight teaching hospitals in France during 2002–2005 and screened for qnrA. Overall, 28 isolates (22 Enterobacter cloacae, three Klebsiella pneumoniae, one Citrobacter freundii, one Klebsiella oxytoca and one Proteus mirabilis) were positive for qnrA, representing 1.9% of all isolates, 3.3%of ESBL-producing isolates (22%of the E. cloacae isolates) and 0%of non-ESBL-producing isolates. The prevalence of qnrA among consecutive ESBL-producing isolates in 2004 from the eight hospitals was 2.8% (18/639). Of the qnrA-positive isolates, 100% were intermediately-resistant or resistant to nalidixic acid, and 75% to ciprofloxacin. Twenty-one of the 22 qnrA-positive E. cloacae isolates were obtained from two hospitals in the Paris area, and molecular typing and plasmid content analysis showed clonal relationships for five, three and two isolates, respectively. The qnrA genetic environment was similar to that of the In36 integron. The remaining two isolates had qnrA variants (30 and 29 nucleotide differences, respectively, compared with the original sequence) and an unknown genetic environment. The ESBL gene associated with qnrA was bla
                     SHV-12 in most of the isolates, but bla
                     PER-1 and bla
                     SHV-2a were found in two isolates. In France, it appears that qnrA-positive isolates are predominantly E. cloacae isolates producing SHV-12, and may be associated with the dissemination of an In36-like integron.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara100" view="all">Bacteria harbouring the novel <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> plasmid-mediated mechanism of quinolone resistance have been described in different countries, but the frequency of their occurrence has not been investigated. In total, 1468 clinical isolates of Enterobacteriaceae with quinolone resistance or extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) phenotypes were collected from eight teaching hospitals in France during 2002–2005 and screened for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>. Overall, 28 isolates (22 <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic>, three <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic>, one <ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii</ce:italic>, one <ce:italic>Klebsiella oxytoca</ce:italic> and one <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic>) were positive for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, representing 1.9% of all isolates, 3.3%of ESBL-producing isolates (22%of the <ce:italic>E. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates) and 0%of non-ESBL-producing isolates. The prevalence of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> among consecutive ESBL-producing isolates in 2004 from the eight hospitals was 2.8% (18/639). Of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates, 100% were intermediately-resistant or resistant to nalidixic acid, and 75% to ciprofloxacin. Twenty-one of the 22 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive <ce:italic>E. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates were obtained from two hospitals in the Paris area, and molecular typing and plasmid content analysis showed clonal relationships for five, three and two isolates, respectively. The <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> genetic environment was similar to that of the In<ce:italic>36</ce:italic> integron. The remaining two isolates had <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> variants (30 and 29 nucleotide differences, respectively, compared with the original sequence) and an unknown genetic environment. The ESBL gene associated with <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-12</ce:inf> in most of the isolates, but <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">SHV-2a</ce:inf> were found in two isolates. In France, it appears that <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates are predominantly <ce:italic>E. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates producing SHV-12, and may be associated with the dissemination of an In<ce:italic>36</ce:italic>-like integron.</ce:simple-para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">INTRODUCTION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Plasmid-mediated resistance to quinolones was first described in 1998 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], following 20 years during which quinolone resistance was restricted to chromosomally-encoded mechanisms. The plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance gene, <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>, was shown to be transferable by conjugation from the original <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate to <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. The mechanism of resistance encoded by <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was novel, involving protection of DNA gyrase from quinolone inhibition by the Qnr protein [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Classically, chromosomally-encoded mechanisms of quinolone resistance mostly involve target site alterations following amino-acid substitutions in the subunits of DNA gyrase (mutations in the <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>gyrB</ce:italic> genes) or topoisomerase IV (mutations in the <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parE</ce:italic> genes) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Decreased quinolone uptake, associated with diminished permeability in Gram-negative bacilli or mycobacteria, and increased efflux in several other bacterial species, have also been described, and result from mutations in chromosomal regulatory genes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>]. None of these mechanisms, studied extensively since 1977, is transferable between bacterial cells.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">New plasmid-mediated <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> genes, <ce:italic>qnrS</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>] and <ce:italic>qnrB</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>], have recently been described. These new genes are 40–60% homologous to the gene discovered by Martinez-Martinez <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>. [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], which has now been named <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>. Chromosomal homologues of <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> have been discovered in the genomes of various environmental bacteria, such as <ce:italic>Photobacterium profundum</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>], Vibrionaceae and <ce:italic>Shewanella algae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. The primary structure of QnrA (218 amino-acids) indicates that it belongs to the family of pentapeptide repeat proteins [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>]. The proteins belonging to this family with the closest similarity (20% at most) to QnrA are the McbG protein that protects DNA gyrase from microcin B17 toxin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>], and MfpA isolated from <ce:italic>Mycobacterium smegmatis</ce:italic>, both of which also confer low-level quinolone resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>]. The transferable <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene is usually carried by large conjugative plasmids (50–180 kb) that also carry plasmid-mediated AmpC-type β-lactamase or extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib2 bib11 bib12">2,11,12</ce:cross-refs>]. The <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene has also been shown to form part of class 1 integrons, together with other antibiotic resistance cassettes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib2 bib11 bib12 bib13">2,11–13</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">Since 2003, <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates have been reported from several geographical regions in the USA [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib12 bib14 bib15 bib16">12,14–16</ce:cross-refs>], Asia [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib11 bib17 bib18 bib19">11,17–19</ce:cross-refs>] and Europe [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib13 bib17 bib20 bib21">13,17,20,21</ce:cross-refs>] in various species, but mostly in <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib11 bib13 bib18">11,13,18</ce:cross-refs>], <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib12 bib14 bib15">12,14,15</ce:cross-refs>] and <ce:italic>Enterobacter cloacae</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib16 bib17 bib21">16,17,21</ce:cross-refs>]. However, to date, the precise burden of quinolone resistance caused by this new mechanism has not been quantified.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para50" view="all">The objective of the present study was to investigate clinical isolates of Enterobacteriaceae, collected through a network of French teaching hospitals, for the presence of the QnrA-based resistance mechanism. Quinolone-resistant and ESBL-producing isolates from the period 2002–2005 were screened by PCR for the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene. Clonal relationships among the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were assessed by molecular typing and analysis of the plasmid content. The <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were also characterised by sequencing the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic>-containing region and by determining the antibiotic resistance patterns and ESBL genes carried on the same resistance plasmid.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">MATERIALS AND METHODS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Bacterial isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">Non-duplicate isolates of Enterobacteriaceae were collected, according to published recommendations for resistance surveillance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>], from patients hospitalised between 2002 and 2005 in eight teaching hospitals located throughout France (Hôpital Pellegrin, Bordeaux; Hôpital Gabriel Montpied, Clermont-Ferrand; Hôpital Henri Mondor, Créteil; Hôpital Charles Foix, Ivry sur Seine; Hôpital Dupuytren, Limoges; Hôpital Européen Georges Pompidou, Paris; Hôpital Pitié-Salpétrière, Paris; Hôpital d'Instruction des Armées Bégin, Saint-Mandé). For inclusion in the study, isolates had to display reduced susceptibility (intermediately-resistant or resistant) to nalidixic acid and ciprofloxacin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>] (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs10" xlink:href="http://www.sfm.asso.fr" xlink:type="simple">http://www.sfm.asso.fr</ce:inter-ref>), or suspected production of an ESBL [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib24 bib25 bib26">24–26</ce:cross-refs>] or a plasmid-mediated AmpC-type β-lactamase [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. Antibiotic susceptibility patterns were determined by the disk-diffusion method (Biorad, Marnes La Coquette, France) on Mueller–Hinton agar, and MICs were determined by the Etest method (AES, Combourg, France). Negative controls for PCR were quinolone- and βlactam-susceptible isolates (12 <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic>, eight <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, ten <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic>), and the positive control was <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strain UAB1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>] (kindly supplied by D. C. Hooper).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Study periods</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">There were three successive study periods. During the first period, 361 consecutive isolates from 2002 with reduced susceptibility to quinolones were tested retrospectively. During the second period, isolates from November 2003 to January 2004 were tested, including (i) isolates of <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> with reduced susceptibility to quinolones (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 285), and (ii) isolates suspected to produce an ESBL (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 91) or a plasmid-mediated AmpC-type β-lactamase (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 3), regardless of species or their susceptibility to quinolones. Since only ESBL-producing isolates were <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive during the first two periods, the third period determined the proportion of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates among ESBL-producing isolates with an epidemiological significance. Therefore, all consecutive ESBL-producing isolates (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 639) during 2004 from the eight hospitals were tested for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>. The third period was prolonged to the first trimester of 2005 in two hospitals, and 89 additional ESBL-producing isolates were tested.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">PCR detection and sequencing of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">Total DNA was extracted by a heat-shock method. In brief, one loopful of freshly-grown colonies was emulsified in 150 µL of sterile water, heated for 5 min at 95°C, and then cooled immediately on ice. Following centrifugation for 2 min at 13 000 <ce:italic>g</ce:italic>, the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microplate. A first PCR (PCR1) was performed on all the isolates using primers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>) amplifying an intragenic <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> 543-bp fragment (GenBank accession number AY070235). All the isolates positive by PCR1 were tested with two other pairs of primers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>), generating an intragenic 627-bp fragment (PCR2) and a 661-bp fragment (PCR3) encompassing the whole <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene. Each amplification mixture contained 5 µL of total DNA extract, 0.5 µM each primer (Proligo, Paris, France), 0.25 mM dNTPs (Amersham Biosciences, Orsay, France) and 1.25 U of <ce:italic>Taq</ce:italic> polymerase (Qbiogene, Illkirch, France). PCRs comprised 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 57°C for PCR1, or 50°C for PCR2 and PCR3, for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. PCR was performed in 96-well microplates with a positive control (<ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> UAB1) and two negative controls (water and <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> KL16). PCR products were visualised following electrophoresis in agarose 2% w/v gels in Tris-borate buffer.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">For all isolates for which the three PCRs were positive, the 661-bp fragment from PCR3 was purified with the Montage PCR purification kit (Millipore, Saint Quentin-en-Yvelines, France), following the manufacturer's recommendations. Sequencing was then performed using the Big Dye Terminator kit v.3.1 (Applied Biosystems, Courtaboeuf, France), 300 ng of purified DNA, 0.6 pmol primers/µL, and an ABI PRISM 3100 automated sequencer (Applied Biosystems). Sequences obtained were analysed with SeqScape software (Applied Biosystems) and compared with the BioEdit Sequence Alignment program (Isis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Analysis of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-containing plasmids</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Plasmid DNA was extracted using a High Speed Plasmid Midi kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) and compared following restriction with <ce:italic>Eco</ce:italic>RI and <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI and electrophoresis on agarose 0.7% w/v gels. Sequences upstream and downstream of the <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> gene were determined by PCR sequencing using a sense primer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) in the middle of <ce:italic>orf513</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>] and an antisense primer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) at the beginning of <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic>, which are two sequences often described in <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>-type integrons. Genes encoding ESBLs of the TEM and the SHV types [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>] were identified by PCR using the primers listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">Conjugation experiments were performed in brain–heart infusion broth with a sodium azide-resistant mutant of <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> BM13 J53 (rifampicin-resistant) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>] as the recipient. Donor and recipient cells in the logarithmic phase of growth were mixed (2 mL each) and incubated for 40 min without shaking. Transconjugant clones were selected on Mueller– Hinton agar plates containing rifampicin 250 mg/L, sodium azide 100 mg/L, and either ampicillin 100 mg/L, amikacin 10 mg/L or sulphamethoxazole 300 mg/L.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">Molecular typing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Total DNA from each <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive strain was analysed by enterobacterial repetitive intergenic consensus (ERIC) sequence PCR with the ERIC2 primer, and random amplified polymorphic DNA using primers 1254 (5'-CCGCAGCCAA) and 1281 (5'-AACGCGCAAC), as described previously [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib20 bib21 bib22 bib23 bib24 bib25 bib26 bib27 bib28 bib29 bib30 bib31 bib32">20–32</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">Isolates positive for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>
                     </ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">
                        <ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/> summarises the bacterial species included in the study, the numbers positive for ESBL production, and the percentage of isolates that were <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/> summarises the quinolone susceptibility patterns of the isolates included in the study. Overall, the three PCRs for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> detection were positive for 28 (1.9%) of the 1468 isolates; the positive isolates comprised 28 (3.3%) of 841 ESBL-producing isolates, and none of the 627 non-ESBL-producing isolates. Only three isolates with a plasmid-mediated AmpC-type β-lactamase phenotype were collected (two <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, one <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic>), and none of these was <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">The 28 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates comprised 22 <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic>, three <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, one <ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii</ce:italic>, one <ce:italic>Klebsiella oxytoca</ce:italic> and one <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic>. Although <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> represented half of the isolates tested, none was <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>). The 28 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were obtained from six of the eight teaching hospitals, with a majority (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 24) of isolates from only two hospitals located in the Paris area. A single <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolate was obtained from each of four other hospitals, but no <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were obtained from the two remaining hospitals, both located in the central region of France (Limoges and Clermont-Ferrand).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">The proportion of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates among ESBL-positive isolates during the third study period, i.e., 2004, ranged from 0% (three hospitals) to 10.5%, according to the hospital, with four hospitals having proportions between 0.8% and 1.9%. In these hospitals, the mean annual incidence density of ESBL-producing isolates during 2004 was 0.2/1000 hospitalisation-days, giving an incidence density of <ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 0.02/1000 hospitalisation-days for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates in the participating hospitals. In the two hospitals that provided the majority of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates, the proportion of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates among ESBL-producing isolates did not increase significantly over time, ranging from 1/19 (5.2%) in 2002, to 7/94 (7.4%) in 2003, and 15/282 (5.3%) in 2004 (p 0.62, chi-square test for trend).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">None of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates was susceptible to nalidixic acid, contrasting with 11.5% of ESBL-producing isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). However, 25% (7/28) of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin. There were more isolates resistant to ciprofloxacin among <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-negative ESBL-producing isolates (77.9%) than among <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive ESBL-producing isolates (57.1%) (p &lt; 0.01; chi-square test). All <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates displayed a multidrug resistance pattern in addition to the ESBL and quinolone resistance phenotypes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl4"/>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">Molecular typing of the 22 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates by ERIC-PCR and rapid amplified polymorphic DNA analysis yielded concordant results, and grouped 11 of the 22 isolates into three clusters (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). Cluster 1 comprised six of the 16 <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates from Pitié-Salpětrière Hospital. Cluster 2 grouped two isolates from different periods at Henri Mondor Hospital (Hm46, Hm190) with one isolate from Pitié-Salpětrière Hospital (Ps88); however, all three patients had been hospitalised previously during the same period in the same long-term care hospital. Cluster 3 included one isolate from Pitié-Salpětrière Hospital (Ps75) and one isolate from Henri Mondor Hospital (Hm477). Chart review revealed that the patient harbouring Hm477 had been hospitalised at Pitié-Salpětrière Hospital 7 months previously. The remaining 11 <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates did not form clusters on the basis of DNA fingerprinting.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Molecular characterisation of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para180" view="all">For 26 of 28 isolates, the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> sequences were identical to the gene described in the original <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> UAB1 isolate, with a silent mutation (nucleotide 537, CTA → CTG) in the leucine codon at position 179. The <ce:italic>Prot. mirabilis</ce:italic> isolate and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> He96 harboured new <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> alleles, which differed from the original gene by 30 and 29 nucleotides (GenBank accession numbers DQ151889 and DQ435306), leading to five and three amino-acid differences, respectively.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">Plasmids extracted from the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates ranged in size from 20 to 100 kb. Plasmid restriction profiles within cluster 1 were similar, except for the plasmid from Ps45, which showed additional <ce:italic>Eco</ce:italic>RI and <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>H1 restriction fragments (data not shown). The ESBL genes detected in plasmid extracts were SHV-12 (23 isolates), SHV-2a (one <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>), and PER-1 (one <ce:italic>Prot. mirabilis</ce:italic>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). For <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> He96, <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> Ps25 and <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> Cf84, no ESBL genes belonging to the TEM or the SHV types were detected, although the presence of other possible ESBL types remains to be investigated. For 25 isolates (22 <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic>, two <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> and one <ce:italic>K. oxytoca</ce:italic>), sequences upstream and downstream of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> were identical to those of plasmids pHSH1 and pHSH<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (GenBank accession numbers AY259085 and AY259086) described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>], with <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> downstream of <ce:italic>orf513</ce:italic> (encoding a putative recombinase) and upstream of <ce:italic>ampR</ce:italic> (the repressor gene for AmpC-type β-lactamase) and <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic> (a gene with a deletion that would otherwise encode resistance to quaternary ammonium compounds). For the <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> isolate, the sequence upstream of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was identical to pHSH1, but the sequence downstream was <ce:italic>cmlA</ce:italic>, a chloramphenicol resistance gene. To date, all attempts to sequence upstream or downstream of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> in the <ce:italic>Prot. mirabilis</ce:italic> isolate and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> He96 have been unsuccessful.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para200" view="all">Transfer of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> from <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> Hm477 to <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> BM13 confirmed that <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> conferred quinolone resistance, with a two- to 24-fold increase in quinolone MICs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="tbl5">Table 5</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl5"/>). However, MICs for the transconjugant were below the recommended ciprofloxacin susceptibility breakpoints of 1 mg/L (CLSI; <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs20" xlink:href="http://www.clsi.org" xlink:type="simple">http://www.clsi.org</ce:inter-ref>) and 0.5 mg/L (EUCAST, <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs30" xlink:href="http://www.eucast.org" xlink:type="simple">http://www.eucast.org</ce:inter-ref>). Co-transfer of resistance to β-lactams occurred, including extended-spectrum agents, and some of the other resistances observed in the parental strain (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="tbl5">Table 5</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">The objective of the present study was to assess the importance of the new plasmid-mediated Qnr-based mechanism of quinolone resistance among Enterobacteriaceae in France. During 2002–2005, <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was found exclusively in ESBL-producing isolates, and mainly in <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates harbouring the gene encoding SHV-12. Since the description of the original <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate in Alabama, USA, in 1994 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>. 80 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates belonging to various species have been described worldwide [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs110" refid="bib11 bib12 bib13 bib14 bib15 bib16 bib17 bib18 bib19 bib20 bib21">11–21</ce:cross-refs>]. Most of these isolates pre-date 2003 and have been obtained from strain collections [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs120" refid="bib12 bib14 bib15 bib16">12,14–16</ce:cross-refs>] or single hospitals [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs130" refid="bib11 bib13 bib18 bib19 bib20 bib21">11,13,18–21</ce:cross-refs>], thereby preventing assessment of the magnitude of the phenomenon. The present survey included isolates obtained between 2002 and 2005 from eight hospitals located throughout France, with <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates representing 1.9% of all enterobacteria included in the present study, and all of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates being ESBL producers. The results from 2004 indicate a prevalence of 2.8% for <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates among ESBL producers, giving an estimated number of 400 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates annually in French hospitals, since the number of ESBL-producing isolates is <ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 15 000 annually, with an incidence density of 0.2/1000 hospitalisation-days (RAISIN nationwide survey; <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs40" xlink:href="http://www.invs.sante.fr" xlink:type="simple">http://www.invs.sante.fr</ce:inter-ref>.).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">As two hospitals located in the Paris region accounted for 24/28 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates and 21/22 <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic> isolates, possible clonality was investigated, as described previously for ESBL-producing isolates [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>]. Molecular typing showed that 10/28 isolates belonged to three genotypic clusters.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">In the present study, all <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates were ESBL producers. To date, <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> has been associated with class 1 integrons, where multiple resistance gene cassettes are integrated [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs140" refid="bib2 bib11 bib12 bib13 bib33">2,11–13,33</ce:cross-refs>]. However, apart from uncommon ESBLs, e.g., VEB, GES and CTX-M-9 [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs150" refid="bib17 bib25 bib26">17,25,26</ce:cross-refs>], ESBL genes are usually not inserted as cassettes in class 1 integrons, and class 1 integrons occur at a similar frequency (<ce:italic>c.</ce:italic> 40%) in ESBL-positive and ESBL-negative isolates of <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>]. Therefore, the observed association between <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> and ESBL genes requires further investigation. In the present study, the association was mostly with SHV-12, first described in Switzerland [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>], in contrast to the first studies of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> in the USA, in which the plasmid-mediated AmpC-type β-lactamase FOX-5 and the SHV-7 ESBL were found [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs160" refid="bib12 bib14 bib15">12,14,15</ce:cross-refs>], and to the two isolates reported previously from France and one isolate from Turkey, which contained the VEB-1 gene [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs170" refid="bib13 bib17 bib36">13,17,36</ce:cross-refs>]. An epidemiological study of 157 ESBL-producing isolates of <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> from France during 2001 revealed that only 11 produced SHV-12, and none of the SHV-12 producers harboured <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>]. This suggests that the association of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> with SHV-12 is recent or may occur preferentially in <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic>. A similar association was found in the <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>C. freundii</ce:italic> isolates in the present study, and has also been reported previously in the UK [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">The sequences of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> genetic environment (<ce:italic>orf513–qnr–ampR–qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1–sul1</ce:italic>), as well as the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> gene itself (<ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> with a silent mutation at nucleotide 537), were identical in 25/28 isolates. These sequences were the same as those described in <ce:italic>Esch. coli</ce:italic> strains from Shanghai [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>], but differ from observations in the USA and Europe that revealed a different genetic environment and integron structures [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs180" refid="bib12 bib13 bib14">12–14</ce:cross-refs>]. Since the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> genetic environment appears to be similar to the pSAL-1 plasmid configuration [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>], in which <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>ampR</ce:italic> are co-inserted downstream of <ce:italic>orf513</ce:italic>, an insertion of <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> between <ce:italic>orf513</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>ampR</ce:italic>, associated with a deletion of <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic>, can be hypothesised. In isolates from France, it seems that dissemination of an In<ce:italic>36</ce:italic>- or In<ce:italic>37</ce:italic>-like integron may be occurring, as opposed to new <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> integration events in strains that already harbour <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>-type integrons. The In<ce:italic>36</ce:italic>- or In<ce:italic>37</ce:italic>-like integrons and plasmids carrying SHV-12 seem to have combined very successfully in nosocomial strains of <ce:italic>Ent. cloacae</ce:italic>. Interestingly, the new <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> variants described in the present study have a totally different genetic environment, which indicates that <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> variants and <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> alleles are each related to a specific genetic environment.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">It has been hypothesised that <ce:italic>qnr</ce:italic> emerged among clinical strains because of the selective pressure of quinolones [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs190" refid="bib1 bib6 bib11">1,6,11</ce:cross-refs>]. However, the low level of resistance conferred by <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> (this study) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs200" refid="bib39 bib40">39,40</ce:cross-refs>] does not seem to be sufficient to enable clinical selection, and most of the <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates to date, including 15/28 isolates in the present study, were highly resistant to ciprofloxacin (MIC &gt;4 mg/L), probably because of additional quinolone resistance mechanisms. Two possible scenarios can be proposed: (i) <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was acquired under quinolone selective pressure because it facilitates the acquisition of successive steps of quinolone resistance, as observed <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs210" refid="bib14 bib40">14,40</ce:cross-refs>]; or (ii) <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> was acquired by strains containing <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>-type integrons because of illegitimate recombination events, regardless of the quinolone selective pressure and the quinolone resistance level of the recipient strain. Since (a) most ESBL producers do not harbour <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> but harbour integrons [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>], (b) ESBL producers are more resistant to ciprofloxacin than <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic>-positive isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>), and (c) class 1 integrons are more prevalent among ciprofloxacin-resistant isolates than among their susceptible counterparts [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="bib41">41</ce:cross-ref>], the second scenario, in which <ce:italic>qnrA</ce:italic> integration is more of a genetic accident than a selected characteristic for the bacterial isolate, seems to be more likely.</ce:para>
