                  A multiresistant strain of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, PA2345, belonging to serotype O:1, was isolated at the Teaching Hospital of Besançon, France. Resistance to β-lactams, including third-generation cephalosporins, depended upon a chromosomally-located composite transposon carrying the bla
                     PER-1 gene encoding extended-spectrum β-lactamase PER-1. PA2345 was unrelated genotypically to two previous PER-1-producing isolates of P. aeruginosa. Sequence analysis of the transposon in PA2345 revealed the presence of two insertion sequences (ISPa23 and ISPa24) with very different predicted transposases (TnpA1, TnpA2), which were both bordered by closely related 16-bp inverted repeats. High resistance of PA2345 to aminoglycosides was caused, in part, by a chromosomal class-I integron containing gene cassettes aadB, encoding an ANT(2″) enzyme, and aadA11, encoding a new ANT(3″) enzyme with 281 amino-acids that conferred elevated resistance to streptomycin and spectinomycin. Stable overproduction of efflux system MexXY contributed to resistance to amikacin, while mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions of gyrA and parC accounted for the high resistance of PA2345 to fluoroquinolones. The study indicates that multidrug resistance in P. aeruginosa might arise from sequential acquisition of a variety of mechanisms provided by both horizontal gene transfers and mutations in chromosomal genes.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara70" view="all">A multiresistant strain of <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic>, PA2345, belonging to serotype O:1, was isolated at the Teaching Hospital of Besançon, France. Resistance to β-lactams, including third-generation cephalosporins, depended upon a chromosomally-located composite transposon carrying the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> gene encoding extended-spectrum β-lactamase PER-1. PA2345 was unrelated genotypically to two previous PER-1-producing isolates of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa.</ce:italic> Sequence analysis of the transposon in PA2345 revealed the presence of two insertion sequences (ISPa23 and IS<ce:italic>Pa24</ce:italic>) with very different predicted transposases (TnpA1, TnpA2), which were both bordered by closely related 16-bp inverted repeats. High resistance of PA2345 to aminoglycosides was caused, in part, by a chromosomal class-I integron containing gene cassettes <ce:italic>aadB</ce:italic>, encoding an ANT(2″) enzyme, and <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic>, encoding a new ANT(3″) enzyme with 281 amino-acids that conferred elevated resistance to streptomycin and spectinomycin. Stable overproduction of efflux system MexXY contributed to resistance to amikacin, while mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> accounted for the high resistance of PA2345 to fluoroquinolones. The study indicates that multidrug resistance in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> might arise from sequential acquisition of a variety of mechanisms provided by both horizontal gene transfers and mutations in chromosomal genes.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">INTRODUCTION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Acquisition of multidrug resistance by <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> is becoming a matter of concern in many hospitals worldwide [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib2">1,2</ce:cross-refs>]. The mechanisms leading to the emergence of strains resistant to almost all the anti-pseudomonal antibiotics available for parenteral administration are often multiple and complex. Thus, resistance may evolve through the transfer of foreign genes from other Gram-negative species, or through the development of intrinsic resistance mechanisms (e.g., up-regulation of efflux systems and AmpC β-lactamase, target alterations, loss of porins) subsequent to spontaneous mutations [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">In this context, integrons and transposons have been detected repeatedly in multidrug resistant <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib4 bib5">4,5</ce:cross-refs>]. Integrons are genetic elements encoding the components of a site-specific recombination system that recognises and captures mobile gene cassettes, mostly resistance determinants [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>]. Such elements may be located within transposons, which in turn contribute actively to the dissemination of resistance determinants to aminoglycosides and β-lactams among Gram-negative species [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that integrons and transposons are associated with the spread of resistance to third-generation cephalosporins when they encode extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) such as VEB-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>], GES-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>] or TEM-21 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Pagani <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>] reported that <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>, the gene encoding ESBL PER-1, was situated close to the gene of a putative transposase in epidemic strains of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa.</ce:italic> PER-1, which is responsible for high-level resistance to ceftazidime, has been found in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. and <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates from Turkey [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib11 bib12">11,12</ce:cross-refs>], Belgium [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>], Italy [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib10 bib14">10,14</ce:cross-refs>] and France [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>]. However, the genetic structures bearing <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> in all these bacteria have not been elucidated completely. The present study describes the mechanisms responsible for multidrug resistance in a strain of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolated at the Teaching Hospital of Besançon, France.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">MATERIALS AND METHODS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Bacterial strains and plasmids</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para40" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> PA2345, belonging to serotype O:1, was isolated in October 2001 from the sputum of a male aged 52 years who was hospitalised in the medical intensive care unit of the Teaching Hospital of Besançon, France. Two other PER-1 producing strains of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> were used for genotyping comparison, namely RNL-1, isolated in France in 1991 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>], and PABG, isolated in Belgium in 1998 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. The wild-type strain PAO1, which has been sequenced completely [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>], and its MexXY-overproducing mutant MutGr1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>], served as controls for quantification of <ce:italic>mexX</ce:italic> expression by quantitative real-time RT-PCR. DNA cloning experiments were performed in <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> DH5α with plasmid vectors pUC18 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>] (coding for ampicillin resistance, Ap<ce:sup loc="post">r</ce:sup>) and pK18 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>] (a pUC18 derivative coding for kanamycin resistance, Km<ce:sup loc="post">r</ce:sup>). In Southern blot experiments, plasmid pSTI1 (81 MDa) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>] was used as a positive control for determining the plasmid location of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Determination of drug susceptibilities</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para50" view="all">Production of ESBL by <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> PA2345 was detected initially with the double-disk synergy test (typical enlargement of the inhibition zone between disks of ceftazidime and ticarcillin-clavulanic acid), performed according to the guidelines of the French Society for Microbiology [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>]. MICs were determined by the microdilution method in Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) with adjusted concentrations of Ca<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> and Mg<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> (BBL, Cockeysville, MD, USA) and an inoculum of 2.5 × 10<ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup> CFU/mL [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>]. Tobramycin was provided by Eli Lilly (Indianapolis, IN, USA), and amikacin by Bristol-Meyers Squibb (Paris, France); all other antibiotics were obtained from Sigma (Saint-Quentin Fallavier, France).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Analytical isoelectric focusing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">After culture in Tryptone Soya Broth (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), supplemented with yeast extract 5 g/L and glucose 10 g/L, bacteria were disrupted by sonication. Unbroken cells and cell debris were removed by centrifugation. Isoelectric focusing was performed on polyacrylamide gels (acrylamide 7% w/v, bis-acrylamide 0.2% w/v) containing ampholines with a pH range of 3.5–10 (Pharmacia Diagnostics, Saint Quentin Yvelines, France). Migration was performed at 500 V overnight in a LKB 2117 Multiphor apparatus (Pharmacia Diagnostics, Uppsala, Sweden). The β-lactamase bands were visualised in the gels by the iodine starch procedure [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>]. β-Lactamases of known pI (TEM-1, pI 5.4; TEM-2, pI 5.6; TEM-3, pI 6.3; TEM-24, pI 6.5; SHV-1, pI 7.6; SHV-4, pI 7.8; CTX-M-1, pI 8.4) were used for gel calibration.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Analysis of genomic DNA by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">Genomic DNA was digested with XbaI and analysed by PFGE for 20 h at 180 V with a pulse ramping from 15 to 5 s using a CHEF-DR2 apparatus (Bio-Rad, Marnes la Coquette, France) as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>]. PFGE patterns were interpreted according to the criteria of Tenover <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">DNA isolation, amplification and sequencing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">DNA from strain PA2345 was extracted as described by Sambrook <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. Plasmids were prepared using a Plasmid Midi kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Purified DNA samples were digested with <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI, SacI or <ce:italic>Hin</ce:italic>dIII (Roche, Meylan, France), and the resulting fragments were separated by electrophoresis in agarose 0.8% w/v gels [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">The <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic>, gyrB, <ce:italic>parE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDRs) of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic>, as well as the coding sequence of the <ce:italic>mexZ</ce:italic> repressor gene and the <ce:italic>mexZ-mexXY</ce:italic> intergenic region, were amplified by PCR with the primers listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>. Cloned fragments and PCR products were sequenced on both strands by Genome Express (Roche) on a capillary autosequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). GenBank searches were performed using the BLASTN Align algorithm (<ce:inter-ref id="caeref30" xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov" xlink:type="simple">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</ce:inter-ref>). Deduced protein sequences were edited with BLASTP (<ce:inter-ref id="caeref40" xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov" xlink:type="simple">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</ce:inter-ref>) and aligned with top hits by using Clustal W v.1.8 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec80" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">Southern hybridisation experiments</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">A digoxigenin-labelled probe for the detection of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> was obtained by adding DIG-11-dUTP to the PCR mix with the Per-1/Per-2 primers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) as recommended by the manufacturer (Roche). DNA was extracted from bacteria by the method of Kieser [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>] and subjected to electrophoresis in a horizontal agarose 0.8% w/v gel, calibrated with reference plasmids from <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> V517 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>]. The DNA was then capillary blotted [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>] to a Hybond-N+ nylon membrane (Amersham, Orsay, France). Southern blot hybridisation was performed under highly stringent conditions, with a DIG DNA detection kit (Roche) used for colourimetric detection of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> on the membrane.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">Cloning of the integron</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">A <ce:italic>Sac</ce:italic>I genomic library of strain PA2345 was constructed in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> DH5α with the plasmid vector pK18 (Kan<ce:sup loc="post">r</ce:sup>). The resulting transformants were screened for aminoglycoside resistance on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA; Bio-Rad, Ivry-sur-Seine, France) containing gentamicin 4 mg/L. Two gentamicin-resistant clones were selected for further analysis, both of which were found to contain the same recombinant plasmid, named pAD1, containing a 5.8-kb SacI fragment. The aadA11 gene was subcloned into vector pUC18, previously cleaved with <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI and <ce:italic>Hin</ce:italic>dIII, by ligating a 914-bp PCR product generated with primers SubE1 (which created a <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI restriction site) and SubE2 (which created a <ce:italic>Hin</ce:italic>dIII site) designed from the sequence of the pAD1 insert. The DH5a clones containing the new construct, designated pADD, were selected on MHA containing streptomycin 32 mg/L.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Characterisation of the transposon carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>
                     </ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">Sequencing of the entire transposon in strain PA2345 was achieved by a genome walking strategy with a two-step semi-degenerate PCR, essentially as described by Jacobs <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>]. Partially overlapping DNA sequences of c. 200-400 bp, upstream of and downstream of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> gene, were amplified with a set of specific primers anchored in the sequences surrounding <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>, and with semi-degenerate primers with a defined tail (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). In the first round of PCR, amplifications were performed in a final volume of 20 μL with c. 100 ng template DNA, 0.4 U RedTaq DNA polymerase (Sigma), 0.2 mM dNTPs, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 μM of a specific primer designed to anneal to the 5′- or 3′-end of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>, and 0.34 μM each of the semi-degenerate primers CEKG2A, CEKG2B and CEKG2C (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Amplification involved 5 min at 94°C, six cycles of 30 s at 94°C, 40 s at 40°C (1°C decreasing at each cycle) and 3 min at 72°C, 25 cycles of 30 s at 94°C, 40 s at 60°C and 3 min at 72°C, and 7 min at 72°C. Each first-round amplicon (1 μL) was used to prime a second-round PCR under the same conditions with 0.5 μM of primer CEKG4 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>), complementary to the tail sequence shared by the three random primers, and 0.5 μM of a nested primer overlapping the end of the anchored primer. PCR conditions comprised 5 min at 94°C, 30 cycles of 30 s at 94°C, 40 s at 60°C and 2 min at 72°C, and 7 min at 72°C. The amplicons were separated by electrophoresis in an agarose 1% w/v gel, and then extracted (QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit; Qiagen) and sequenced. New specific primers were then designed (data not shown) for subsequent PCR amplifications in order to walk the chromosome.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Quantitative real-time PCR</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Quantitative data for <ce:italic>mexX</ce:italic> transcripts were obtained in triplicate and normalised to <ce:italic>rpsL</ce:italic> gene expression as an internal control, as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>]. The expression levels of <ce:italic>mexX</ce:italic> in strain PA2345 were then divided by those found in wild-type strain PAO1 in order to yield relative results.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">RESULTS AND DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">A new ESBL-producing strain of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic>
                     </ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Strain PA2345 was detected initially because of its high resistance to many anti-pseudomonal antibiotics, especially ceftazidime (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). Production of an ESBL by this isolate was first suspected from a standard antibiogram, and was then confirmed with a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>-specific PCR, in accordance with the finding of several PER-1-producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates in Europe [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib10 bib13 bib14 bib15">10,13–15</ce:cross-refs>] and Turkey [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib11 bib33">11,33</ce:cross-refs>] during the past decade. A single 925-bp amplicon was obtained, with a sequence that was 100% identical with those generated from two other <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf>-carrying <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates from France in 1991 (strain RNL-1, serotype O:2) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>] and Belgium in 1998 (strain PABG, serotype O:12) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. Genotyping analysis of the three strains by PFGE showed very distinct profiles, which indicates that this ESBL gene may spread among isolates of different geographical origins. In contrast to other PER-1-producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> strains involved in hospital outbreaks in Italy [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>], Turkey [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>] and Belgium [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>], strain PA2345 did not disseminate and remained confined to a single patient in this institution.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">The isoelectric point for this enzyme (pI 5.2) was close to that reported by Nordmann <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>] (pI 5.4). In addition, strain PA2345 produced basal levels of chromosomally-encoded AmpC β-lactamase (pI 8.2).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">Characterisation of the genetic element carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> in PA2345</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para180" view="all">Unlike the PER-1-producing strains of <ce:italic>Salmonella</ce:italic> Typhimurium described by Vahaboglu <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>] and Casin <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>], PA2345 did not appear to harbour a plasmid after DNA extraction, and failed to transfer ceftazidime resistance by conjugation to other bacterial hosts (results not shown). Consistent with these findings, Southern blot experiments with a specific <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> probe revealed that the gene was located on the chromosome of this strain (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>), as also reported previously for <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> RNL-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>], PABG [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>] and a variety of epidemic strains isolated in Italy [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">In order to learn more about the genetic environment of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> gene, the sequence of the flanking region in PA2345 was established using a PCR chromosome walking strategy. The sequence of the 4872-bp genetic element found in this strain contained the 1899-bp fragment described by Pagani <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>] (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>a). Database searches revealed that the ORF (1266 bp) located upstream of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> gene codes for a putative product of 421 amino-acids that has 37% amino-acid homology with the transposase of an insertion sequence (IS) element from <ce:italic>Aeromonas punctata</ce:italic> (accession number YP067861) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>]. This ORF was arbitrarily named <ce:italic>tnpA1.</ce:italic> The product of the second ORF, designated <ce:italic>tnpA2</ce:italic> (1272 bp), which is transcribed downstream of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> in the same direction as <ce:italic>tnpA1</ce:italic>, was found to have 37% identity with the transposase of ISSB1 from the marine psychotrophic bacterium Mst37 (accession number CAC84124.1). Both <ce:italic>tnpA1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>tnpA2</ce:italic>, which are bordered by almost identical 16-bp terminal inverted repeats (IR) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="fig2">Fig. 2b</ce:cross-ref>), display the typical structure of IS elements. They have been called ISPa23 and IS<ce:italic>Pa24</ce:italic> (<ce:inter-ref id="caeref40g" xlink:href="http://www-is.biotoul.fr/" xlink:type="simple">http://www-is.biotoul.fr/</ce:inter-ref>). Moreover, the presence of two 6-bp directly repeated sequences (DRs) flanking IR1L and IR2R (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig2">Figs 2a and b</ce:cross-ref>), generated in the target DNA as a consequence of transposon insertion, strongly evokes the genetic organisation of composite transposons.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para200" view="all">Several composite transposons are known to disseminate resistance to aminoglycosides (Tn<ce:italic>4001</ce:italic>, Tn<ce:italic>5</ce:italic>) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>], chloramphenicol (Tn<ce:italic>9</ce:italic>) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>], tetracycline (Tn<ce:italic>10</ce:italic>) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>] and β-lactams [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>]; their IS modules contain transposase genes coding for either strictly identical, functional enzymes (Tn<ce:italic>9</ce:italic>) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>], or closely related enzymes, one of which is functional and the other inactive (Tn<ce:italic>10</ce:italic>, Tn<ce:italic>5</ce:italic>) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib41 bib42">41,42</ce:cross-refs>]. Interestingly, TnpA1 and TnpA2 demonstrated limited amino-acid sequence homology (45.1% overall identity; 57% overall similarity). To our knowledge, this is the first report of a composite transposon with such dissimilar putative transposases. This new transposon was named Tn<ce:italic>2345.</ce:italic> Amplification of PA2345, RNL-1 and PABG genomic DNA with specific primers targeting <ce:italic>tnpA1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>tnpA2</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) generated PCR products of the same length, suggesting very similar, if not identical, structures. Sequencing of the DNA regions upstream and downstream of Tn<ce:italic>2345</ce:italic> revealed that this element was inserted into <ce:italic>insAB</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="fig2">Fig. 2A</ce:cross-ref>), inactivating the gene coding for the putative transposase of IS<ce:italic>1012</ce:italic> (GenBank accession number CAD31055).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec150" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle170">Class I integron</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para210" view="all">The high-level resistance of strain PA2345 to all clinically-significant aminoglycosides (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>) suggested the production of one or more aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme(s). In order to characterise the genetic determinants of this resistance, a 5.8-kb <ce:italic>Sac</ce:italic>I-<ce:italic>Sac</ce:italic>I genomic fragment from PA2345 was cloned into <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> DH5α. The recombinant plasmid pAD1 conferred resistance to kanamycin, gentamicin and tobramycin, but not to amikacin and netilmicin. Sequencing of the pAD1 insert indicated the presence of a class I integron exhibiting a 5′-conserved segment (5′-CS) carrying the integrase gene intI1, and a 3′-CS located downstream of three integrated gene cassettes, namely <ce:italic>aadB, orfE</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>a). The 3′-CS itself contained a typical set of three genes, namely <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic>, sul1 and <ce:italic>orf5</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib43">43</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para220" view="all">The first gene cassette of the integron, <ce:italic>aadB</ce:italic>, showed a sequence 100% identical to that of the prototype [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>] (GenBank accession number L06418), codes for aminoglycoside adenylyltransferase AAD(2″), also designated ANT(2″)-I, and confers high-level resistance to kanamycin, gentamicin and tobramycin. This gene has already been reported to occur in a <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> strain as a cassette carried by a class I integron, along with an ESBL-determining gene, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">VEB-1</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>]. In strain PA2345, no <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic> gene was found in the vicinity of <ce:italic>aadB.</ce:italic>
                     </ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para230" view="all">Downstream of <ce:italic>aadB</ce:italic>, an ‘<ce:italic>orfE</ce:italic>-like’ gene was identified that was 90% homologous to various <ce:italic>orfE</ce:italic> genes reported previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>]. This ‘<ce:italic>orfE</ce:italic>-like’ gene flanks an ORF of 846 bp that encodes a novel aminoglycoside resistance gene, named <ce:italic>aadA11.</ce:italic> The <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> sequence showed eight and two nucleotide differences compared with <ce:italic>aadA6</ce:italic> and the first 804-bp of gene fusion <ce:italic>ant(3″)Iiaac(6′)-Iid</ce:italic>, respectively [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib47 bib48">47,48</ce:cross-refs>]. Sequence analysis of the <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> flanking regions identified elements typical of gene cassettes. Indeed, immediately adjacent to the gene is an integrase-specific recombination site, <ce:italic>attC</ce:italic>, belonging to the family of ‘59-base elements’ [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="bib49">49</ce:cross-ref>], with an inverse core site (GTCTAAC) identical to those flanking <ce:italic>aadA6</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA7</ce:italic>, and a recombinational core site (GTT-AGAT) identical to that of <ce:italic>aadA7</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="fig3">Figs 3b,c</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para240" view="all">AadA11 activity was investigated in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> DH5α transformed with recombinant plasmid pADD, in which the <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> gene was sub-cloned. The recombinant clones showed high resistance to streptomycin and spectinomycin (MICs 1024 mg/L; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>), confirming that AadA11 has the same substrate specificity as other AadA enzymes characterised to date, which are referred to as ANT(3″) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="bib50">50</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec160" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle180">Aminoglycoside efflux and quinolone resistance in PA2345</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para250" view="all">Products of the <ce:italic>aadB</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> genes do not confer resistance to amikacin and cannot account for the reduced susceptibility of PA2345 to this agent [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib7 bib44">7,44</ce:cross-refs>]. As stable overproduction of the MexXY-OprM efflux system may impair the susceptibility of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> to virtually all aminoglycosides, including amikacin [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib51 bib52">51,52</ce:cross-refs>], the expression levels of <ce:italic>mexX</ce:italic> were measured by quantitative real-time PCR. PA2345 expressed <ce:italic>mexX</ce:italic> 34-fold more than PAO1, and two-fold more than MutGr1, a MexXY-overproducing mutant derived from reference strain PAO1, which is altered in repressor gene <ce:italic>mexZ</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>]. Sequence analysis of <ce:italic>mexZ</ce:italic> revealed only silent mutations, suggesting that PA2345 is an <ce:italic>agrW</ce:italic> mutant defective in an unknown regulator gene [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="bib53">53</ce:cross-ref>]. Non-cystic fibrosis strains of <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> that overproduce MexXY constitutively and are highly resistant to amikacin (MIC ≥ 64 mg/L) have been reported previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="bib54">54</ce:cross-ref>]. However, inactivation of <ce:italic>mexXY</ce:italic> in these latter strains left residual resistance to aminoglycosides. Similarly, disruption of <ce:italic>mexY</ce:italic> by homologous recombination with a central fragment of the gene cloned in suicide vector pUC18 decreased the MIC of amikacin from 64 to 16 mg/L in strain PA2345, and from 4 to 1 mg/L in PAO1 (data not shown). This result indicates that additional unknown mechanisms contribute to the low susceptibility of that strain to amikacin in a synergic interaction with efflux.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para260" view="all">Mutations in the QRDRs of <ce:italic>gyrA</ce:italic> (Thr83 → Ile) and <ce:italic>parC</ce:italic> (Ser80 → Leu), together with MexXY efflux, accounted for the high MICs of fluoroquinolones for strain PA2345.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec170" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle190">CONCLUSIONS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para270" view="all">This study reinforces the concept that multidrug resistance in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> may arise from sequential acquisition of a variety of mechanisms provided by multiple genetic exchanges (integrons, transposons) and mutations in chromosomal genes (target alterations, up-regulation of efflux pumps) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. The observation that genotypically and geographically diverse <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> strains have acquired the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">PER-1</ce:inf> transposon [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>] since the initial isolation of RNL-1 in 1991 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>], suggests that contacts with still unknown bacterial reservoirs contribute to the evolution of this pathogen towards multiresistance in Europe and Turkey.</ce:para>
                  <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle200">Nucleotide sequences accession numbers</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para280" view="all">The nucleotide sequences reported in this paper have been submitted to the EMBL and GenBank databases. The accession numbers for the class I integron and <ce:italic>aadA11</ce:italic> gene are AY758206 and AY144590, respectively; the accession number for the putative composite transposon Tn<ce:italic>2345</ce:italic> is AY866517.</ce:para>
