                     Background: The Staphylococcus aureus collagen-binding protein Cna mediates bacterial adherence to collagen. The primary sequence of Cna has a non-repetitive collagen-binding A region, followed by the repetitive B region. The B region has one to four 23 kDa repeat units (B1–B4), depending on the strain of origin. The affinity of the A region for collagen is independent of the B region. However, the B repeat units have been suggested to serve as a ‘stalk’ that projects the A region from the bacterial surface and thus facilitate bacterial adherence to collagen. To understand the biological role of these B-region repeats we determined their three-dimensional structure.
                  
                     Results: B1 has two domains (D1 and D2) placed side-by-side. D1 and D2 have similar secondary structure and exhibit a unique fold that resembles but is the inverse of the immunoglobulin-like (IgG-like) domains. Comparison with similar immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) structures shows novel packing arrangements between the D1 and D2 domains. In the B1B2 crystal structure, an omission of a single glycine residue in the D2–D3 linker loop, compared to the D1–D2 and D3–D4 linker loops, resulted in projection of the D3 and D4 in a spatially new orientation. We also present a model for B1B2B3B4.
                  
                     Conclusions: The B region of the Cna collagen adhesin has a novel fold that is reminiscent of but is inverse in nature to the IgG fold. This B region assembly could effectively provide the needed flexibility and stability for presenting the ligand binding A region away from the bacterial cell surface.

            <ce:abstract class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">
                     <ce:bold>Background:</ce:bold> The <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> collagen-binding protein Cna mediates bacterial adherence to collagen. The primary sequence of Cna has a non-repetitive collagen-binding A region, followed by the repetitive B region. The B region has one to four 23 kDa repeat units (B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>–B<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>), depending on the strain of origin. The affinity of the A region for collagen is independent of the B region. However, the B repeat units have been suggested to serve as a ‘stalk’ that projects the A region from the bacterial surface and thus facilitate bacterial adherence to collagen. To understand the biological role of these B-region repeats we determined their three-dimensional structure.</ce:simple-para>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">
                     <ce:bold>Results:</ce:bold> B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> has two domains (D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>) placed side-by-side. D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> have similar secondary structure and exhibit a unique fold that resembles but is the inverse of the immunoglobulin-like (IgG-like) domains. Comparison with similar immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) structures shows novel packing arrangements between the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> domains. In the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> crystal structure, an omission of a single glycine residue in the D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>–D<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf> linker loop, compared to the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>–D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>–D<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf> linker loops, resulted in projection of the D<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf> in a spatially new orientation. We also present a model for B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>.</ce:simple-para>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">
                     <ce:bold>Conclusions</ce:bold>: The B region of the Cna collagen adhesin has a novel fold that is reminiscent of but is inverse in nature to the IgG fold. This B region assembly could effectively provide the needed flexibility and stability for presenting the ligand binding A region away from the bacterial cell surface.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para view="all">Bacterial binding to extracellular matrix proteins such as fibrinogen <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB1">[1]</ce:cross-ref>, fibronectin <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB2">[2]</ce:cross-ref>, vitronectin <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB3">[3]</ce:cross-ref> and collagen <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB4">[4]</ce:cross-ref> is an important mechanism of host-tissue adherence and is considered to be the first step in the pathogenic process of many infections <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB5">[5]</ce:cross-ref>. Many MSCRAMMs (microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules) have been identified in the past decade (for reviews see <ce:cross-refs refid="BIB5 BIB6 BIB7">[5–7]</ce:cross-refs>). Sequence analyses of the MSCRAMMs revealed a common structural theme composed of unique and repeated sequences. A multidomain organization was proposed for these proteins, with a specific ligand-binding activity being ascribed to some subdomains. The <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> collagen-binding MSCRAMM, Cna, binds to a variety of collagens with moderate affinity <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB8">[8]</ce:cross-ref>. Molecular characterization of the <ce:italic>Cna</ce:italic> gene revealed the presence of a 55 kDa nonrepetitive A region (previously denoted as the A domain <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB9">[9]</ce:cross-ref>) and the repetitive B region that consists of multiple 23 kDa B-repeat units (previously denoted as the B domain <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB9">[9]</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR1">Figure 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="FIGGR1"/>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">Cna mediates bacterial adherence to collagenous tissues such as cartilage, a process that is important in the pathogenesis of septic arthritis caused by <ce:italic>Staphylococci</ce:italic>. An isogenic mutant in which the <ce:italic>Cna</ce:italic> gene was inactivated was considerably less virulent than the wild-type Cna-expressing bacteria in a mouse model of septic arthritis <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB10">[10]</ce:cross-ref>. In addition, an <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic> strain that normally lacked the <ce:italic>Cna</ce:italic> gene became more virulent in this model when the <ce:italic>Cna</ce:italic> gene was introduced. The collagen-binding activity was attributed to the Cna A region. A 19 kDa portion of the A region, Cna151–318 (previously denoted as CBD19 [collagen-binding domain 19] <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB11">[11]</ce:cross-ref>), with measurable collagen-binding activity, was used in both biochemical and X-ray crystallographic studies to illustrate the protein’s mode of binding to collagen. The crystal structure of Cna151–318 was determined in our laboratory, and consisted of two parallel β sheets connected by two short α helices <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB11">[11]</ce:cross-ref>. One of the sheets had a noticeable trench traversing its surface. Molecular modeling studies were helpful in docking a collagen triple-helical probe [(Gly-Hyp-Pro)<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>]<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf> (where Hyp-Pro is hydroxy-proline) into this trench. Analyses of single point mutations performed in and around the trench confirmed it to be the binding site for collagen. Recently, a similar binding scheme has also been suggested for other collagen-binding proteins <ce:cross-refs refid="BIB12 BIB13 BIB14 BIB15 BIB16">[12–16]</ce:cross-refs>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">The B region of Cna in <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic> strain FDA574 is a triplet of 23 kDa repeat units B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>, B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> and B<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>. Gillaspy <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>. reported that the B region is composed of one to four nearly identical 23 kDa repeats, depending on the <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic> strain from which the Cna was isolated <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB17">[17]</ce:cross-ref>. Earlier studies indicated that the B region neither binds collagen nor influences the A region’s collagen-binding activity <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB18">[18]</ce:cross-ref>. These B region repeat units have been proposed to serve as a ‘stalk’ that projects the A region away from the bacterial cell surface and positions it for binding to collagen <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB18">[18]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">In our quest to understand the significance of the repetitive B region in the architecture of the <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic> collagen adhesin, in this paper we describe the crystal structures of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and the covalently linked B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> dimer, contrast their unique fold to the immunoglobulin (IgG) domains, and compare their structures with similar multidomain crystal structures. In addition, we present modeling studies of multiple B region repeat units based on the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> crystal structures, and highlight some of the important structural features.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Crystal structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>
                     </ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The crystal structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> displays two similar domains (D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>) placed side by side and related by an approximate twofold screw axis (2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>; <ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR2">Figure 2a</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="FIGGR2"/>). Each D domain exhibits a four- and three-strand arrangement similar to that observed in the IgG domains but differs in topology from all the previously described V, C, H and I types (V, variable; C, conserved; H, hybrid; I, intermediate; respectively) in the immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) <ce:cross-refs refid="BIB19 BIB20">[19,20]</ce:cross-refs>. The order of the β strands and their linking regions are different in comparison with all the described IgSF-like domains. We designate this fold as the inverse IgG (inv-IgG) fold. The interface between D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> is marked by several hydrogen bonds between the two domains. There are only three hydrophobic residues (Leu564, Met710 and Ile714) in the interface and the remaining interactions are via either charged or polar amino acids. A combined surface area of 782 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> is buried in the interface between the two domains (see the Materials and methods section for a calculation of the buried surface area).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para view="all">There is 41% sequence identity between the two domains, and the 89-residue D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> domain and the 97-residue D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> domain superpose with a root mean square deviation (rmsd; on C<ce:inf loc="post">α</ce:inf> atoms) of 1.28 Å (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR2">Figure 2b,c</ce:cross-ref>). The existence of two domains and their significant sequence identity in a single B region repeat unit was identified upon solving the crystal structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>. The AB loop regions in these two domains share a striking sequence identity (67%) and have similar structures (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR2">Figure 2c</ce:cross-ref>). These AB loops in D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> have the sequences DKDNQDGKRPEK (single-letter amino acid code) and DNNNQDGKRPTE, respectively, where eight out of twelve residues are conserved and resemble the consensus calcium-binding motif of the form DxxNxDxKxxE (where X is any amino acid) <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB21">[21]</ce:cross-ref> that is present in the <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic> surface protein SdrD B domain <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB22">[22]</ce:cross-ref>. The observed coordination geometry of the water molecule present in this loop suggested a possible metal binding site. However, structural analyses of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> crystallized in the presence of calcium did not reveal any calcium ions in this location (data not shown). Moreover, no change in structure upon addition of Ca<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> was observed in the circular dichroism (CD) and fluorescence emission spectra of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> or B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (data not shown). Further comparison of the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> structures revealed that the FG loop is the only segment that differs significantly in length and composition; D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> has five additional residues including Asp709 and Met710, and it exhibits a resultant negative charge on the molecular surface.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para view="all">The two independent copies of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> in the asymmetric unit have distinct crystal-packing interactions but exhibit nearly identical structures, superposing well with an rmsd (C<ce:inf loc="post">α</ce:inf> atoms) of 0.68 Å. The presence of an identical second copy of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> in the asymmetric unit suggests that the ‘folding pattern’ of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> (and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>), and the ‘domain packing’ between D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> could be intrinsic in nature.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title>Comparison with the IgG domain</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The IgG constant domain generally has a four-stranded β sheet, ABED, on one side of the barrel and a three-stranded β sheet, CFG, on the other side (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3a</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="FIGGR3"/>). The D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> domains also have a four-(DAGF) and three-stranded (EBC) β-sheet arrangement but display a different topology (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3b</ce:cross-ref>). When overlaying the four strands of D1 (DAGF) on IgG (ABED) the directions of the first two strands D and A of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> run in opposite directions to the A and B strands of IgG. Whereas the strands A and G of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> are parallel, the positional equivalent strands B and E of IgG are antiparallel.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para view="all">The IgG and D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> folds seem to be the inverse of one another. Keeping the C-terminal G strand common, the inverse relationship between the two antiparallel β-sandwich structures starting from the N-terminal end of the IgG is as follows: (IgG/D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>) A/F, B/E, C/D, D/C, E/B, and F/A. It seems that the evolution of the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> fold has relied on tail-to-head rather than head-to-tail folding, if directionality could be assigned to the folding. This inverse nature is observed in several other aspects of the fold. For example, in the rainbow-colored topology diagrams (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3a,b</ce:cross-ref>), if any one of the strand colors on the IgG and D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> are aligned an adjacent strand of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> (to the right or left) is observed to be on the opposite side in IgG. Also, viewing the four-stranded β sheet as the frontal plane of the molecule, the CD loop region is on the right side for IgG, whereas in D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> it is on the left side (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3a,b</ce:cross-ref>). The N termini of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and IgG originate from nearly identical positions. The C terminus of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> is on the right side of the frontal plane (four strands) whereas in IgG it is on the left side of the back plane (three strands) of the module (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3c</ce:cross-ref>). Thus, the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and D<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> domains have a four- and three-stranded arrangement similar to that observed in IgG domains, but they exhibit a novel inverse fold.</ce:para>

The newly observed fold for the D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> domain has allowed the presentation of the LDV motif at the exposed extremity of the molecule.</ce:para>

               <ce:section view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para view="all">The IgSF motif is evolutionarily ancient, as exemplified by both intracellular and extracellular IgSF domains in <ce:italic>Caenorrhabditis</ce:italic> elegans <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB37">[37]</ce:cross-ref>. On the human leukocyte surface about 40% of the proteins are predicted to contain one or more IgSF domains <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB38">[38]</ce:cross-ref>. The crystal structure of the bacterial periplasmic chaperone (PapD) has a topology identical to that of an IgG fold <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB39">[39]</ce:cross-ref>. Here we report the structure of the B region of the bacterial surface protein Cna, which has a β sandwich of novel architecture that resembles the IgG fold. The novel inverse nature of this fold compared with that of IgG is observed in: the reversal of directions in the placement of adjacent strands in the topology diagrams compared with IgG (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3a,b</ce:cross-ref>); the presence of the CD strand on the opposite side of the molecule compared with the IgG domain (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR3">Figure 3c</ce:cross-ref>); and the clockwise pattern of packing of domains in the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> structure compared with the counter-clockwise packing pattern observed in hemolin (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR6">Figure 6</ce:cross-ref>). The secondary structure predictions based on the sequence of D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> proposed an IgSF fold; however, the observed inverse fold could not be predicted. The preservation of structural homology between the D domains is observed not only in the β-sheet regions but also in the loop regions. The conservation of sequence and tertiary structure by lower-level organisms like bacteria indicates that this might be an essential structural element for a specific, but as yet unidentified, biological function.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">It is interesting to observe both the integrin-binding LDV and ETxxK motifs in the CD loop region of the first domain (D<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>) of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>; they closely resemble those of the ICAMs and VCAMs. The LDV motifs in B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> are well exposed at the extremities of the molecule and are separated by a distance of 60 Å. It is very tempting to speculate and suggest that each B repeat unit binds to one integrin molecule. However, cell binding experiments with α<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>β<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> integrin, which recognizes the LDV motif <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB40">[40]</ce:cross-ref> did not show any positive binding to the recombinant construct of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (data not shown).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">Domain duplication by genetic evolution has led to interesting combinations of events in both sequence and structure, and divergent evolution could lead to a difference in the sequence of the duplicated domain <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB41">[41]</ce:cross-ref>. Domain arrangements are also crucial during the initial assembly of proteins to optimally adapt to their function. There are several examples of tandem-duplicated crystal structures (e.g. chymotrypsin, zinc-finger motifs found in transcription factor IIIA, and fibronectin type III repeats found in the muscle protein titin; for a review see <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB41">[41]</ce:cross-ref>). However, these duplicated domains vary in amino acid composition and in some cases in their secondary structure. The B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> repeat units have nearly identical sequence and secondary structure. Further, the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> structure has less buried surface area between the domains when compared with the other similar four-domain structures of fibronectin and hemolin. This could mean that the interactions between B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> are more susceptible to changes by the external forces in comparison with the other four-domain IgSF structures.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">The observation of the omission of a residue resulting in a different orientation of the next B repeat unit is similar to that suggested by Eisenberg and coworkers <ce:cross-refs refid="BIB42 BIB43">[42,43]</ce:cross-refs>. They proposed that a hinge-loop mutation/deletion lead to domain swapping, a phenomena observed in the packing of multiple copies of the protein molecule. In the case of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> we do not observe any domain-swapping characteristics, however, we do observe an arrangement that is equivalent to domain swiveling. The structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> resembles the linear (open-ended) 3D domain-swapped oligomer, which was proposed for multiple aggregation of monomers. Interestingly, Pokkuluri <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>. <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB44">[44]</ce:cross-ref> reported domain flipping resulting from the substitution of one amino acid (Glu38) in the crystal structure of modified human immunoglobulin light-chain variable domains (V<ce:inf loc="post">L</ce:inf>). However, in this structure the ‘conventional dimer’ has flipped by 180° around an axis perpendicular to the original twofold axis. It is important to note that a single glycine residue produces a radically different conformation in a sequence (KYTPET, KYTPGET) of high homology. Homology modeling and threading analyses tend to conclude that the presence of a glycine in a sequence of this kind would not produce major conformational change; in the case of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> this conclusion would obviously be incorrect.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para view="all">Cna was proposed to be a mosaic protein, where one region/domain functions independently of another <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB18">[18]</ce:cross-ref>. In our modeling studies the accordion model of the B-repeat units could project the A region from the cell surface and aid in binding to collagen. If these repeat units were not serving as a stalk, but instead have an unknown function, then flexibility of the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf> model would be needed for these repeat units to move and function independently. The B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf> accordion model seems to possess the flexibility and stability needed for holding out the larger A region (55 kDa) for collagen binding. The necessary trigger or force to activate them to move in conjunction (stretching/retracting) could come from any of the many known and unknown cell events.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Biological implications</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para view="all">The collagen-binding surface protein of <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> is composed of a non-repetitive A region and a repetitive B region. In our previous study of this protein, the A region was shown to bind to collagen. The crystal structure was solved for a portion of the A region, Cna151–318, that contains the collagen-binding activity. Subsequently, docking studies implicated a trench observed in the structure as the collagen-binding site, and mutations in and around this trench supported this hypothesis. The presence of one to four B-repeat units has been observed in different strains of <ce:italic>S. aureus</ce:italic>. The importance of the presence of the B-repeat region and the functions of these repeats are not yet known. However, it has been suggested that the B-repeat units might serve as a ‘stalk’ that projects the A region away from the bacterial surface, thereby facilitating bacterial adherence to collagen. Analyses of the crystal structure of the single B-repeat unit (B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>), revealed two immunoglobulin-like (IgG-like) domains with a novel fold. This new fold has a four- and three-strand arrangement, and is the inverse of the fold found in the IgG-like domains. Notably, the B-repeat units include the integrin-binding LDV and ETxxK motifs in the CD loop regions of their first domains, although the significance of these domains remains unclear. The crystal structure of two B-repeat units (B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>) has a packing arrangement where a single missing glycine residue in the linker region between B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> and B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> has resulted in a new orientation of the domains. Our modeling studies on the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">3</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf> repeats shows that these domains pack in a zig-zag fashion, like an accordion; they might stretch and contract from the bacterial cell wall and thus aid in the projection of the A region away from the cell surface. Perhaps in the event of proteolytic loss of a Cna A region resulting from a specific cleavage by the host’s extracellular defensive apparatus, the B-repeat units could be pressed into an adhesive process or any other function essential for bacterial survival. The repeating number of these domains might reflect the added stability the bacteria would achieve by multiple anchoring.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title>Crystallization and data collection</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The expression, purification and crystallization of the recombinant protein B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> (22.6 kDa) were reported previously <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB45">[45]</ce:cross-ref>. Crystals were grown by the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method. A droplet containing 2 μl of protein, 1.6 μl of well solution (1 ml of 4M (NH<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>)<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>SO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, 50 mM succinic acid, pH 4.6) and 0.4 μl of 10% (w/v) octyl β-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glucopyranoside was equilibrated against the reservoir well solution. Crystals grew at room temperature in 4–5 days to a size of 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.2 mm. Data were collected on the Raxis IIc image plate detector mounted on a Rigaku RU-200 rotating anode operating at 100 mA and 40 kV. A complete data set to 2.5 Å resolution was collected at room temperature. The crystallographic data statistics are presented in <ce:cross-ref refid="TBL2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="TBL2"/>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para view="all">In the case of crystallization of the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (43.8 kDa) recombinant protein, a droplet containing 1 μl of protein, 1 μl of 5 mM CaCl<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> and 7 μl of well solution (1 ml of 16% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG6000), 100 mM sodium cacodylic acid, pH 7.5) was equilibrated against the reservoir well solution <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB46">[46]</ce:cross-ref>. Diffraction-quality crystals grew at room temperature in 5–6 days to a size of 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.15 mm. A complete data set was collected on a Raxis IV image plate mounted on a Rigaku RU-H3R rotating anode operating at 100 mA and 40 kV to 2.3 Å resolution at room temperature. Crystallographic data statistics are presented in <ce:cross-ref refid="TBL2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Structure determination and refinement</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The structure of the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> protein was solved by the MIR (multiple isomorphous replacement) method. Three derivatives (trimethyl lead acetate [TMLA], potassium platinum tetrachloride and samarium nitrate) were identified by difference Patterson analysis suitable for MIR phasing (using the program XTALVIEW <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB46">[46]</ce:cross-ref>). The details of the heavy-atom derivative statistics are presented in <ce:cross-ref refid="TBL3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="TBL3"/>. Each derivative had one major site and one minor site. Refinement of heavy-atom derivatives and subsequent phase calculations were carried out using the program PHASES <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB47">[47]</ce:cross-ref>. An initial 3.0 Å electron-density map revealed clearly defined solvent boundaries and most of the sidechain densities were visible. The initial C<ce:inf loc="post">α</ce:inf> trace was made using the program CHAIN <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB48">[48]</ce:cross-ref>. Sidechains were incorporated into the model and further refinements were performed with X-PLOR <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB49">[49]</ce:cross-ref>. All reflections (≥ 2σ) from 8.0 to 2.5 Å were used in the refinement, with 10% partitioned in a test set for monitoring the refinement process <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB50">[50]</ce:cross-ref>. The initial R<ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf>/R factor of the fully built model with sidechains was 40.1%/35.7%. The molecular model building was performed with the graphics program O <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB51">[51]</ce:cross-ref>, in conjunction with OOPS <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB52">[52]</ce:cross-ref>. Solvent molecules were added based on geometry and electron density. Model convergence was achieved after several cycles of model building and refinement. Maximum-likelihood refinement and bulk-solvent correction were carried out with CNS_0.4 <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB53">[53]</ce:cross-ref> in the last few cycles, resulting in 84 solvent molecules in the asymmetric unit with the final R<ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf>/R factor being 24.5%/19.1%. The average B factor for B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> was 38.8 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>. Although the B factors are on the high side, they are still comparable with the calculated B value from the Wilson plot of 47.6 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>. The average B factors for IgG-like domains vary between 13.0 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> (for fibronectin <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB34">[34]</ce:cross-ref>) and 50.4 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> (for ICAM-2 <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB25">[25]</ce:cross-ref>). The model has 99.1% of the residues in the allowed regions of the Ramachandran plot, two residues in the generously allowed regions, and one residue in the disallowed region. These latter three residues appear in loop region where the electron density is not clearly defined.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para view="all">The crystal structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>B<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> was solved by the molecular-replacement method. The crystal structure of B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> was used as the initial model. A good rotation solution was obtained using the program AMORE <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB54">[54]</ce:cross-ref>. Using the first rotation solution we obtained two good peaks for the translation search. Fixing the properly rotated and translated molecule we could obtain the rotation and translation values for the second molecule. Following rigid-body refinement in AMORE, the R factor was 31.1% and correlation coefficient of 0.51 was obtained for each individual solution. The model was further refined by X-PLOR using simulated annealing. The R<ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf>/R factor dropped from 42.8%/35.6% to 37.7%/27.7%. Further refitting was carried out with the program O in conjunction with OOPS. Maximum-likelihood refinement and bulk-solvent correction were performed with CNS_0.4 in the last few cycles. With 72 solvent molecules in the asymmetric unit the model had a final R<ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf>/R factor of 24.44%/18.29%. This model had an average B factor of 41.2 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>, which was comparable with that of the B<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> model and had all the residues within the allowed regions of the Ramachandran plot.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Orientation and superposition of IgSF-like domains</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The comparison of the different IgSF domains requires a common coordinate system and here we describe a new method for comparing them. The first and last C<ce:inf loc="post">α</ce:inf> atoms of the seven core strands of the barrel each defined a strand vector and their average, a strand center. The average of the sheet centers was taken as the origin. The seven normalized vectors (negated if necessary to point in the same direction as the first strand) were averaged and normalized to define a y axis. The average direction of the vectors between the first three sheet centers on each side of the barrel defined an intermediate vector, x′. Then the z axis was defined as the cross product of x′ and y. The x axis was then defined as the cross product of y and z. The y axis gives the average direction of sheets, with the strand centers roughly parallel to the x axis. This orientation is used for all figures and superpositions presented in this paper. All the figures in this paper were made using the program RIBBONS <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB55">[55]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Elbow, twist and swivel angles</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para view="all">The relationship between the two domains is based on the coordinate system described earlier to align domains. We define an elbow angle as the complement of the angle between the y axes of the two domains, a twist angle as the angle between the x axes of the two domains, and a swivel angle as the projection of the second domain’s y axis on the xz plane of the first domain (<ce:cross-ref refid="FIGGR4">Figure 4</ce:cross-ref>). The original method used in calculating the elbow angles for antibodies was based on the intersection of the dyads <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB56">[56]</ce:cross-ref>. In the structure of hemolin it was defined as the angle between the long axes of adjacent domains, approximated by the ellipsoids <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB35">[35]</ce:cross-ref>, and in fibronectin by connecting the C<ce:inf loc="post">α</ce:inf> positions of residues at the midpoints of the B–C and E–F loops <ce:cross-ref refid="BIB34">[34]</ce:cross-ref>. The rotation angles required to superimpose adjacent domains described for fibronectin closely resemble our calculated values for the twist angles. The swivel angle is a new definition and to our knowledge has not been described yet for comparing the IgSF-like domains. (The program used to calculate these angles is available on request from MC).</ce:para>
