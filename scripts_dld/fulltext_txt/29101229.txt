Stephen</dc:creator><dc:description>
                  Cationic antimicrobial peptides (CAMPs), such as polymyxins, are used as a last-line defense in treatment of many bacterial infections. However, some bacteria have developed resistance mechanisms to survive these compounds. Current pandemic O1 Vibrio cholerae biotype El Tor is resistant to polymyxins, whereas a previous pandemic strain of the biotype Classical is polymyxin-sensitive. The almEFG operon found in El Tor V. cholerae confers &gt;100-fold resistance to antimicrobial peptides through aminoacylation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), expected to decrease the negatively charged surface of the V. cholerae outer membrane. This Gram-negative system bears striking resemblance to a related Gram-positive cell-wall remodeling strategy that also promotes CAMP resistance. Mutants defective in AlmEF-dependent LPS modification exhibit reduced fitness in vivo. Here, we present investigation of AlmG, the hitherto uncharacterized member of the AlmEFG pathway. Evidence for AlmG glycyl to lipid substrate transferase activity is demonstrated in vivo by heterologous expression of V. cholerae pathway enzymes in a specially engineered Escherichia coli strain. Development of a minimal keto-deoxyoctulosonate (Kdo)-lipid A domain in E. coli was necessary to facilitate chemical structure analysis and to produce a mimetic Kdo-lipid A domain AlmG substrate to that synthesized by V. cholerae. Our biochemical studies support a uniquely nuanced pathway of Gram-negative CAMPs resistance and provide a more detailed description of an enzyme of the pharmacologically relevant lysophosphospholipid acyltransferase (LPLAT) superfamily.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara90" view="all">Cationic antimicrobial peptides (CAMPs), such as polymyxins, are used as a last-line defense in treatment of many bacterial infections. However, some bacteria have developed resistance mechanisms to survive these compounds. Current pandemic O1 <ce:italic>Vibrio cholerae</ce:italic> biotype El Tor is resistant to polymyxins, whereas a previous pandemic strain of the biotype Classical is polymyxin-sensitive. The <ce:italic>almEFG</ce:italic> operon found in El Tor <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> confers &gt;100-fold resistance to antimicrobial peptides through aminoacylation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), expected to decrease the negatively charged surface of the <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> outer membrane. This Gram-negative system bears striking resemblance to a related Gram-positive cell-wall remodeling strategy that also promotes CAMP resistance. Mutants defective in AlmEF-dependent LPS modification exhibit reduced fitness <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic>. Here, we present investigation of AlmG, the hitherto uncharacterized member of the AlmEFG pathway. Evidence for AlmG glycyl to lipid substrate transferase activity is demonstrated <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> by heterologous expression of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> pathway enzymes in a specially engineered <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> strain. Development of a minimal keto-deoxyoctulosonate (Kdo)-lipid A domain in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> was necessary to facilitate chemical structure analysis and to produce a mimetic Kdo-lipid A domain AlmG substrate to that synthesized by <ce:italic>V. cholerae.</ce:italic> Our biochemical studies support a uniquely nuanced pathway of Gram-negative CAMPs resistance and provide a more detailed description of an enzyme of the pharmacologically relevant lysophosphospholipid acyltransferase (LPLAT) superfamily.</ce:simple-para>

                     </ce:footnote> superfamily is a massive collection of enzymes present throughout all domains of life, where most genomes encode multiple superfamily homologs. Foundational LPLAT enzymes generally catalyze transfer of acyl groups from donor molecules, like carrier proteins or coenzyme A, onto acceptor lipids, such as lyso-glycerophospholipids or lipid A, or glycerophospholipid precursors, such as dihydroxyacetonephosphate or glycerophosphate. Research on bacterial LPLATs has not only shown their impact on the chemical composition of membrane lipids, and how variation in composition contributes to organismal fitness, but has also demonstrated how specific environmental factors trigger remodeling of bacterial membranes through sensory pathways that coordinate regulation of LPLAT activities (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib2">1–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref70" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Lipid A biosynthesis late acyltransferases (LABLAT) compose a characteristic subgroup within the LPLAT superfamily and participate in secondary acylation of bacterial keto-deoxyoctulosonate (Kdo)-lipid A domains during the final steps of canonical biosynthesis. Biochemical characterization of LABLAT members was first performed with source material from <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic>, where secondary acyltransferases esterify acyl groups to primary hydroxylacyl chains of nascently synthesized Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid IV<ce:inf loc="post">A</ce:inf> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref80" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref90" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>). In <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> these activities are encoded by <ce:italic>lpxL</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref100" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>), which transfer lauroyl (C12:0, 12 carbons) or myristoyl (C14:0, 14 carbons) fatty acids from acyl carrier proteins to hydroxyacyl fatty acids at the 2′- and 3′-positions of the Kdo-lipid A di-glucosamine backbone, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref110" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref120" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>). Subsequent investigations of LABLAT homologs across numerous bacterial species show nuanced heterogeneity in acyl chain selectivity and variance in positional specificity in hydrocarbon transfer to target substrates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref130" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib7 bib8">–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref140" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para110" view="all">LpxN is a recently described member of the LABLAT subgroup found in the human intestinal pathogen <ce:italic>Vibrio cholerae</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref150" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>), which annually infects millions of people around the world, especially in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref160" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref170" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>). Subsequent elucidation of LpxN activity showed it transfers a 3-hydroxylaurate residue to the primary 3′-hydroxylacyl chain of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> Kdo-lipid A domains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>). LpxN deletion mutants are sensitive to polymyxin B by &gt;100-fold relative to wild type, current pandemic <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> El Tor strains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>). Inactivating mutants of distantly related LABLAT members from other organisms such as <ce:italic>Klebsiella, Escherichia, Salmonella,</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> also display sensitivity to CAMPs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>) but not to the same degree as LpxN inactivation, which produces an order of magnitude more sensitivity to CAMPS (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Mass spectrometric structural analysis of Kdo-lipid A domains isolated from <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> biotype El Tor showed evidence for attachment of glycine or diglycine residues to the 3-hydroxyl of LpxN-transferred 3-hydroxylauryl groups (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). A multiprotein pathway, AlmEFG (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>), was identified as responsible for the observed Kdo-lipid A glycine modification. Discovery of the AlmEFG pathway resolved a decades long mystery in clinical surveillance of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> as to why current pandemic biotype El Tor is resistant to polymyxin B, but the previous pandemic biotype “Classical” is sensitive. Classical strains lack a functional AlmEFG pathway, due to an inactivating mutation in AlmF (Vc1578; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>), later determined to be a glycine-carrier protein (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>). Further biochemical characterization of AlmE (Vc1579), a dual aminoacyl-adenyltransferase/carrier protein ligase, implicated its role in transfer of an activated glycine to the phosphopantetheinyl prosthetic group of holo-AlmF carrier molecules (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>). AlmEFG glycine modification to <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> surface lipids revealed an emergent paradigm in membrane remodeling between Gram-negatives and Gram-positives with respect to CAMPs defense, as Gram-positives similarly decorate their surface teichoic acids with aminoacyl groups (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib14 bib15">14–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para130" view="all">In this report the biochemistry of a distant LABLAT homolog, AlmG (Vc1577), is explored, providing evidence to support its hitherto putative role as a glycyltransferase in the AlmEFG pathway. Evidence for AlmG glycyl to lipid substrate transferase activity is demonstrated <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> by heterologous expression of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> pathway enzymes in a specially engineered <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strain. Development of a minimal Kdo-lipid A domain in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> was necessary to facilitate chemical structure analysis and to produce a mimetic Kdo-lipid A domain AlmG substrate to that synthesized by <ce:italic>V. cholerae.</ce:italic> With this report AlmG becomes the only characterized aminoacyl transferase in the LPLAT superfamily. Further mutational analysis of putative catalytic residues reveals an active site unique to AlmG, which indicates a chemical mechanism that may be distinct from other characterized LABLAT subgroups and LPLAT superfamily members.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">Rationale, genetic construction, and LPS analysis of E. coli that produce simplified Kdo-lipid A domains</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">It remains possible that AlmG might require idiosyncratic features of the Kdo-lipid A domain biosynthesized by <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic>, similar to the specific requirement of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> LpxL for a phosphorylated Kdo domain (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). To better investigate AlmG activity and any potential substrate requirements, we sought to engineer an <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strain that produced a simplified Kdo-lipid A domain containing a 3″-hydroxylauryl. This would allow definition of the minimum factors for Kdo-lipid A glycine modification in a non-native organism.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strain design started with deletion of the LpxM acyltransferase to produce a penta-acylated Kdo-lipid A domain, lacking the secondary myristoyl acyl chain. Our previous study showed <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> LpxN can replace LpxM in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>), with a final lipid A product that contains a 3-hydroxylauoryl group at the 3″-position (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>), the substrate hydroxyl group for putative AlmG glycine transfer. Deletion of <ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> was also performed, which encodes a phosphotransferase dispensable for growth in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>), not found in <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic>, and complicates downstream analysis of <ce:sup loc="post">32</ce:sup>P-radiolabeled lipids by thin-layer chromatography (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>). Finally, mutants in the Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid A heptosyltransferase pathway of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. S2</ce:cross-ref>), responsible for initiating LPS inner core oligosaccharide synthesis, were also targeted. Others have successfully disrupted the <ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> (also known as <ce:italic>waaDFC</ce:italic>) operon in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> with no apparent effect on viability (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>). Our rationale for the heptosyltransferase disruption step was multifold: (i) produce a minimal Kdo-lipid A domain; (ii) slow core-lipid A transport across the inner membrane to potentially increase efficiency of the predicted cytoplasmic glycine modification; and (iii) simplify downstream isolation of Kdo-lipid A material.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">In the <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> mutant, we were able to cleanly delete the entire <ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> operon (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. S2</ce:cross-ref>) using a recombineering approach (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>). Verification that core-oligosaccharide truncated Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid A species are produced by the engineered <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> was assessed by ProQ Emerald detection of isolated LPS separated by SDS-PAGE (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). Consistent with disruption of the heptosyl transfer reactions, and core-oligosaccharide synthesis, a smaller molecular weight species was readily apparent in isolated LPS from the Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> mutant <ce:italic>versus</ce:italic> parental strain <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>; compare <ce:italic>lanes 5 versus 4</ce:italic>). For reasons discussed below, further mutations to <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> were performed, and they maintained the core-oligosaccharide truncated phenotype (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:italic>lanes 6–8</ce:italic>).</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Analysis of lipids from E. coli-expressing V. cholerae enzymes shows requirement for AlmG in production of glycine-modified Kdo-lipid A domains</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">Combinations of AlmEFG and LpxN were co-expressed in the simplified Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid A-producing <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strain, to determine whether glycine modification was possible in an organism other than <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). Combinatorial, co-expression constructs of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> proteins were created using the pQlink plasmid system, which enables IPTG-inducible expression of multiple, simultaneous gene products (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). Co-expression of AlmEFG and LpxN in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>eptA</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>eptB</ce:italic> resulted in expected TLC migration shifts for Kdo-lipid A material modified with glycine (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:italic>last two lanes</ce:italic>). Glycine results in the addition of a positively charged residue, which in this TLC solvent system results in migration with lower mobile phase retention factors (spot shifts downward); similar to phosphoethanolamine-modified species (<ce:italic>e.g.</ce:italic> 
                        <ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>). The shift in phosphoethanolamine-modified species is more dramatic than glycine modification, as might be expected, due to the zwitterionic nature of phosphoethanolamine. Expression of the trio of AlmEFG proteins, without LpxN, did not affect migration of radiolabeled lipid species consistent with LpxN providing the appropriate 3-hydroxylauryl acyl chain as the site of glycine esterification (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>). Similarly AlmG is indispensable for glycine modification, where co-expression of LpxN, AlmF, and AlmE does not show evidence for glycine modification, consistent with the role of AlmG as a glycyltransferase. As Gly-AlmF is a co-substrate, the law of mass action would predict that increasing its intracellular concentration may push the AlmG-based glycine transfer reaction forward. Indeed, co-expression of Vc2457, which we previously identified as an AlmF-selective phosphopantetheinyltransferase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>), improved overall pathway efficiency as an increase in glycine modification was evident by TLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>, compare <ce:italic>last two lanes</ce:italic>). Vc2457 expression increases the available pool of holo-AlmF available for AlmE glycinylation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para200" view="all">Predicted TLC structure assignments were supported by MALDI-MS (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>). Molecular ions consistent with 3″-hydroxylauryl containing lipid A and/or Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid A species were highly abundant upon expression of LpxN in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxM</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>lpxT</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>eptA</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>eptB</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:italic>m/z</ce:italic> 1784.2 and 2224.5). Co-expression of LpxN, AlmEFG, and Vc2457 reveals prominent spectral peaks indicative of glycine-modified (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:italic>m/z</ce:italic> 1841.1 or 2281.3) and diglycine-modified (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>; <ce:italic>m</ce:italic>/<ce:italic>z</ce:italic> 1898.2 and 2338.2) lipid material, congruent with our TLC result (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>). Our previous structural analysis of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> biotype El Tor lipid A using more informative MS<ce:italic>
                           <ce:sup loc="post">n</ce:sup>
                        </ce:italic> also showed a mixed population of mono- and di-glycine-containing species (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). Efficiency of modification as observed by MALDI appears more robust than what was observed by TLC. Either di-glycinylated or mono-glycinylated lipid material might co-migrate with unmodified lipid in this solvent system, which complicates the use of TLC in determining overall modification efficiency. In this report MALDI and TLC data cohesively provide structural analysis in non-native <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> that details the minimal requirements for glycine modification of Kdo-lipid A domains and the absolute requirement of AlmG in the final addition of glycine to target lipids. With our successful synthetic biology approach to characterization of the glycine modification pathway, we turned our attention to a more direct biochemical investigation of AlmG-based glycine transfer.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Activity assessment of single-site AlmG alanine variants reveals an unusual catalytic domain within the LABLAT subgroup</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para210" view="all">AlmG is distantly related to other members of the LABLAT subgroup, yet LABLAT members are the closest homologs in public databases of the apparently unique AlmG protein (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig6"/>; <ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. S4</ce:cross-ref>). A recently described clade within the LABLAT subgroup, LpxJ, responsible for transfer of acyl chains to secondary positions along associated Kdo-lipid A domains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>), is even more distantly related than AlmG (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>). Members of the expansive LPLAT superfamily typically feature an H<ce:italic>X</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>(D/E) catalytic dyad proposed to be essential for activating hydroxyl nucleophiles for attack on thioester-linked acyl groups in transferase activity (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>). AlmG contains two such candidate active-site dyads (<ce:sup loc="post">106</ce:sup>H<ce:italic>X</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>D<ce:sup loc="post">111</ce:sup> and <ce:sup loc="post">215</ce:sup>H<ce:italic>X</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>E<ce:sup loc="post">220</ce:sup>). This putative catalytic dyad is often positioned within a predicted hydrophobic cleft region, reasoned to be important for positioning of target lipid A acyl chain substrates (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib26 bib27">26–</ce:cross-refs>
                        <ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). Based on current convention, the dyad at position His-106/Asp-111 is the most likely active-site dyad.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para220" view="all">To assess the role of predicted catalytic residues, single residue alanine variants of AlmG were assessed. Glycine transfer activity was assessed via polymyxin B resistance in a <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> Δ<ce:italic>almG</ce:italic> mutant, an assay described in multiple studies that provides a rapid, physiologically relevant detection method for Kdo-lipid A chemical glycine modification in <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). Strains of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> El Tor Δ<ce:italic>almG</ce:italic> were complemented with a pQlink vector, alone, expressing a wild-type copy of <ce:italic>almG</ce:italic> or single-site alanine variants (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="fig7">Fig. 7</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig7"/>). No obvious growth phenotypes were observed in any strains tested. Contrary to other LABLATs, the His-106/Asp-111 dyad was not absolutely required for activity. Shockingly, the His-215/Glu-220 dyad was implicated as absolutely necessary for Kdo-lipid A domain glycine modification (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="fig7">Fig. 7</ce:cross-ref>). This site is not present in any other sequenced LABLAT member. Lys-148 was surprisingly dispensable for AlmG-based glycine transfer, a notable departure from the best biochemically characterized LABLAT subgroup member, LpxM from <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii,</ce:italic> which has a spatially, chemically conserved arginine implicated in acyl transfer activity (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>). D180A mutation also had no effect on AlmG glycine transferase activity. In sum, our AlmG functional assays, informed by the state of field biochemical data of conserved LABLAT catalytic motifs, conflate with its phylogeny (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>) to place AlmG as an unusual, potentially foundational clade within the LABLAT subgroup of the LPLAT superfamily. AlmG very likely uses His-215/Glu-220 for its apparent glycyltransferase activity, a catalytic element consistent with other LPLATs, albeit at a different site. A more detailed description of AlmG activity awaits further elucidation.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>

               <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">AlmG does not contain predicted transmembrane helical regions characteristic of bacterial integral membrane proteins (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>), and it is most likely a peripheral membrane protein. The general hydropathy of AlmG is relatively high with multiple hydrophobic clusters predicted (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>). Combined with a very basic theoretical pI (9.49), these features hint at favorable interactions with acidic, hydrophobic phospholipids of the inner bacterial membrane. As glycinylated AlmF is restricted to the cytosol (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>), the substrate donor molecule for glycine transfer, AlmG's residence on the cytoplasmic face of the inner membrane bilayer is most sensible. MALDI-MS and TLC analysis of lipid material from <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> hosts expressing Alm, and other necessary enzymes, support the assignment of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> AlmG as a <ce:italic>bona fide</ce:italic> glycyltransferase of distant relation to LABLAT subgroup members, yet it is no more distant than the LpxJ clade (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">A recent report elegantly providing the first LPLAT structure, LpxM from <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, put forth compelling evidence for bifunctional activity of a LABLAT subgroup member (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>). This secondary acyltransferase homolog catalyzes not only the transfer of hydrocarbon acyl chains to substrate Kdo-lipid A domains but also displays a thioesterase activity against co-substrate acyl-ACPs. Thioesterase activity in this context would release covalently bound hydrocarbon acyl chains on associated carrier proteins. Albeit tendentious, this indicates that LPLAT members, with <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> LpxM bifunctionality, can liberate fatty acids for redirection into catabolic pathways, because acyl-ACPs are generally fated for anabolic reactions during growth. The signal for metabolic redirection would be lack of sufficient Kdo-lipid A substrate as might occur during metabolic starvation, and as reported <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> LpxM thioesterase activity occurs when lipid co-substrates are in absentia (<ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> only acyl-ACP is bound). Interestingly, LpxM from <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> presents the closest related member of the LABLAT family to AlmG, still quite a distant relation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>). For the above reasons, an alternative mechanism of AlmG glycyl transfer is likely, where direct transfer to Kdo-lipid A domains may not occur. In combination with the dual active-site hypothesis for LpxM of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, we present an alternative, plausible route for glycine modification of Kdo-lipid A domains. Glycine may be transferred to 3-hydroxylauryl ACPs, where LpxN transfers a resultant glycinylated acyl chain to Kdo-lipid A domains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="fig8">Fig. 8</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig8"/>). Isolation of a glycinylated 3-hydroxylauryl intermediate would provide convincing evidence for this mechanism.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">Our laboratory recently reported that <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> El Tor has maintained a second LPS modification to promote polymyxin resistance through the addition of phosphoethanolamine residues to lipid A phosphate groups (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>). The <ce:italic>Vibrio</ce:italic> EptA homolog is similar to pEtN transferases found in other pathogens, including <ce:italic>Neisseria meningitidis</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Salmonella enterica</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Campylobacter jejuni</ce:italic>, and pathogenic <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>. Phosphoethanolamine transferase activity occurs in the periplasmic compartment. Substrate competition between lipid A modification enzymes has been reported (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>). During development of the <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> synthetic biology approach to study glycine modification in this report, EptA and EptB were capable of phosphoethanolamine transfer onto glycinylated Kdo-lipid A domains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 5 and 6</ce:cross-ref>). Similarly <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> EptA is capable of phosphoethanolamine transfer to glycinylated substrates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>). Thus phosphoethanolamine and glycine transfer does not occur at the level of enzymatic substrate specificity. However, there is coordinated control of LPS glycine and phosphoethanolamine modifications in <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> at the transcriptional level. The responsible regulatory network has not been sorted out, but we do know that expression of <ce:italic>eptA</ce:italic> is not controlled by the AlmEFG transcriptional regulator VprA (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">Aminoacylation of bacterial surface membranes is an emergent paradigm in microbial resistance to cationic antimicrobial peptides. Gram-positive bacteria neutralize their cell walls by transferring <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-alanine to surface teichoic acids, poly-phosphoribitol, or poly-phosphoglycerol chains linked to glycerophospholipids or <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-acetylmuramic acid of surface-exposed peptidoglycan (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib37 bib38">37–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>). Although the genes <ce:italic>dltBD</ce:italic> of the <ce:italic>dltDABC</ce:italic> operon (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1010" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>) have long been identified as responsible for the final chemical transfer step, from <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-alanine-linked carrier proteins (DltC) to surface molecules, the precise molecular details of the DltBD reaction remain to be elucidated. Recent data have clarified that DltC, the carrier molecule of <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-alanine, analogous to Gly-AlmF, is not exported, consistent with aminoacyl transfer activity being localized to the cytoplasm (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1020" refid="bib41">41</ce:cross-ref>). Continued exploration of this system in Gram-positives will hopefully clarify these important steps, in a modification with tremendous physiological importance, particularly in pathogenic <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic> and other Firmicutes like <ce:italic>Listeria</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Bacillus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Enterococcus,</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>Streptococcus</ce:italic> spp. (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1030" refid="bib41">41</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para270" view="all">Kdo-lipid A glycinylation is a unique Gram-negative strategy necessary for resistance to CAMPs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1040" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). AlmEFG catalyzes the adenylation of an amino acid (like DltA), subsequent transfer to a carrier protein (like DltA to DltC), and final transfer to a surface component by dedicated transferase machinery (as DltBD). An important consideration regarding the activity of AlmG is apparent specificity for Gly-AlmF. Detailed molecular investigation of wild-type <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> AlmE, demonstrated that its substrate holo-AlmF could, in addition to glycine, carry <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-alanine as evidenced upon <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> characterization (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1050" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>). Currently, there is insufficient evidence to support the existence of <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Ala-AlmF <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1060" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>). AlmG certainly does not use <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Ala-AlmF to decorate Kdo-lipid A domains as <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>
                     <ce:bold>-</ce:bold>Ala-Kdo-lipid A is not apparent either in MALDI-MS analysis of isolated lipid material from <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> (<ce:italic>e.g.</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="fig3 fig7">Figs. 3 and 7</ce:cross-refs> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1070" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1080" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). Finally, as diglycine-modified Kdo-lipid A species are apparent in both <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1090" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1100" refid="fig7">7</ce:cross-ref>, and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1110" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1120" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>), it would appear that if any flexibility exists in AlmG specificity, it involves chemistry for at least two rounds of glycine to lipid substrate transfer. Multiple rounds of amino acid transfer to the same molecule have not been reported with DltBD-like enzymes. As a final note, glycine-modified sugars of core-oligosaccharide LPS have also been reported in multiple organisms, including <ce:italic>C. jejuni, Haemophilus influenzae,</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Shigella flexneri</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib42 bib43">42–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1130" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>); however, enzymes responsible for this observed activity await identification. A recent study on the chemical composition of <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> exopolysaccharide also showed its decorated with glycine residues, catalyzed by a hitherto unidentified enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1140" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para280" view="all">Evidence in this report supports the hypothesis that AlmG functions as a glycyltransferase and characterizes a potential founding member of an outlying clade within the LABLAT subgroup of the LPLAT superfamily. However, this distinction awaits deposition of more closely related sequences within public databases. The paucity of terrestrial bacterial sequences, with high similarity to AlmG, is remarkable. Genomes of marine bacteria are difficult to obtain as a result of sampling difficulties (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib46 bib47">46–</ce:cross-refs>
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1150" refid="bib48">48</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>Vibrio</ce:italic> is one of the most facile groups of marine microorganisms to culture and is an important oceanic model organism. How fitting that an organism with dual lifestyles between the oceanic hydrosphere and centuries long evolution as a human pathogen provides our first glimpse of this newly described glycyl to lipid transfer activity.</ce:para>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">General procedure for co-isolation of glycerophospholipid and Kdo<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>-lipid A species</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para350" view="all">A single colony of bacteria was used to inoculate 5 ml of media (LB or G56 media, supplemented with 100 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> IPTG or antibiotic) and grown overnight at 37 or at 30 °C for Δ<ce:italic>rfaDFC</ce:italic> strains. The next day, a 10-ml culture was inoculated to a starting <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">600</ce:inf> of ∼0.05, grown in a 20 × 150-mm disposable glass culture tube. Growth media for TLC analysis was supplemented with 10 μCi/ml of <ce:sup loc="post">32</ce:sup>P. Cells were grown to mid-exponential phase, to an <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">600</ce:inf> of 0.8–1.0. Cells were then harvested in 16 × 125-mm glass centrifuge tubes with PTFE-lined cap using a fixed angle clinical centrifuge at 1,500 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 12 min. Supernatant was removed into an appropriate radioactive waste container. Cell pellets were washed with 5 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged again for 10 min at 1,500 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic>. Supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in 5 ml of single-phase Bligh-Dyer mixture consisting of chloroform/methanol/water (1:2:0.8, v/v/v), mixed by vortex, and incubated at room temperature for 20–30 min to ensure complete chemical cell lysis. These mixtures were centrifuged in a clinical centrifuge at 1,500 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 15 min. Supernatant, which contains glycerophospholipids, isoprenyl lipids, free lipid A, and Kdo-lipid A domains, was then transferred into clean 16 × 125-mm glass centrifuge tubes and converted into a two-phase Bligh-Dyer mixture by adding 1 ml of chloroform and 1 ml of methanol, yielding a chloroform/methanol/aqueous (2:2:1.8 v/v) mixture. This mixture was vortexed for ∼30 s and then centrifuged for 12 min in a clinical centrifuge at 1,500 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> to separate the organic and aqueous phases. The lower organic phase was removed into a clean glass centrifuge tube using a glass Pasteur pipette. A second extraction was performed by adding 2 ml of pre-equilibrated two-phase Bligh-Dyer lower phase (2:2:1.8; v/v/v; chloroform/methanol/PBS, pH 7.5) to the remaining upper phase. This material was vortexed and centrifuged as before. The lower phase was combined with the previous lower phase, and 7.6 ml of pre-equilibrated upper phase was added to yield a final two-phase Bligh-Dyer solution (chloroform/methanol/water; 2:2:1.8, v/v/v). Material was vortexed and centrifuged at 1,500 × <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> for 12 min, and the lower phase was transferred to a clean glass tube where isolated lipid material was dried using a nitrogen stream dryer. Dried samples were used right away or were transferred to smaller containers by resuspension in 4:1 v/v chloroform/methanol, then re-dried, and/or routinely stored as dried material at −20 °C until further use.</ce:para>

                     <ce:para id="para360" view="all">TLC mobile phase solvent system was prepared in a clean TLC tank with ∼40 cm of chromatography paper to accommodate a 10 × 20- or 20 × 20-cm Silica Gel 60 plate.                      <ce:section-title id="cestitle170">Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry to structurally characterize isolated phospholipid species</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para370" view="all">Typically, the dried lipid A samples were transferred in microvials, where material was resuspended in ∼50 μl of chloroform/methanol (4:1, v/v) and vortexed/sonicated to obtain an ∼1–5 μg/μl lipid A solution when extracted from <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>. Azothiothymine matrix was prepared by mixing a saturated 6-aza-2-thiothymine in 50% acetonitrile solution with saturated tribasic ammonium citrate (20:1, v/v). Mixtures were vortexed and centrifuged, before 0.5 μl was spotted on MALDI plate. 0.5 μl of lipid sample was typically deposited onto the previously spotted ATT matrix and mixed on plate. MALDI-MS data were calibrated using calibration 1 and calibration 2 mixtures from Anaspec peptide mass standards in negative mode. Spectra were acquired in the linear negative mode on a Voyager-DE (AB Biosystems) by scanning for optimal ion signals, with a minimum of 300 laser shots per spectra. Samples were diluted or concentrated as needed to generate high-quality spectra. Typical voltage parameters included 20,000 V, 95% grid voltage, 0.002 guide wire voltage, and a delay time of 150 ns. A low mass gate at 500 Da was also employed.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle180">Polymyxin B MIC determination</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para380" view="all">Mid-exponential <ce:italic>V. cholerae</ce:italic> strains were diluted (1:50) in fresh media and sterilely applied to LB agar plates supplemented with 1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> IPTG. Polymyxin B E-strip® tests were added to the plate and allowed to incubate overnight, where confluent bacterial growth occurs at antibiotic levels below the MIC.</ce:para>
