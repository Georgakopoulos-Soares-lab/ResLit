                  The crystal structure of the W47A/W242A mutant of phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC) from Bacillus thuringiensis has been solved to 1.8Å resolution. The W47A/W242A mutant is an interfacially challenged enzyme, and it has been proposed that one or both tryptophan side chains serve as membrane interfacial anchors (Feng, J., Wehbi, H., and Roberts, M. F. (2002) J. Biol. Chem. 277, 19867-19875). The crystal structure supports this hypothesis. Relative to the crystal structure of the closely related (97% identity) wild-type PI-PLC from Bacillus cereus, significant conformational differences occur at the membrane-binding interfacial region rather than the active site. The Trp → Ala mutations not only remove the membrane-partitioning aromatic side chains but also perturb the conformations of the so-called helix B and rim loop regions, both of which are implicated in interfacial binding. The crystal structure also reveals a homodimer, the first such observation for a bacterial PI-PLC, with pseudo-2-fold symmetry. The symmetric dimer interface is stabilized by hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions, contributed primarily by a central swath of aromatic residues arranged in a quasiherringbone pattern. Evidence that interfacially active wild-type PI-PLC enzymes may dimerize in the presence of phosphatidylcholine vesicles is provided by fluorescence quenching of PI-PLC mutants with pyrene-labeled cysteine residues. The combined data suggest that wild-type PI-PLC can form similar homodimers, anchored to the interface by the tryptophan and neighboring membrane-partitioning residues.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" xml:lang="en" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara100" view="all">The crystal structure of the W47A/W242A mutant of phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC) from <ce:italic>Bacillus thuringiensis</ce:italic> has been solved to 1.8Å resolution. The W47A/W242A mutant is an interfacially challenged enzyme, and it has been proposed that one or both tryptophan side chains serve as membrane interfacial anchors (Feng, J., Wehbi, H., and Roberts, M. F. (2002) <ce:italic>J. Biol. Chem.</ce:italic> 277, 19867-19875). The crystal structure supports this hypothesis. Relative to the crystal structure of the closely related (97% identity) wild-type PI-PLC from <ce:italic>Bacillus cereus</ce:italic>, significant conformational differences occur at the membrane-binding interfacial region rather than the active site. The Trp → Ala mutations not only remove the membrane-partitioning aromatic side chains but also perturb the conformations of the so-called helix B and rim loop regions, both of which are implicated in interfacial binding. The crystal structure also reveals a homodimer, the first such observation for a bacterial PI-PLC, with pseudo-2-fold symmetry. The symmetric dimer interface is stabilized by hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions, contributed primarily by a central swath of aromatic residues arranged in a quasiherringbone pattern. Evidence that interfacially active wild-type PI-PLC enzymes may dimerize in the presence of phosphatidylcholine vesicles is provided by fluorescence quenching of PI-PLC mutants with pyrene-labeled cysteine residues. The combined data suggest that wild-type PI-PLC can form similar homodimers, anchored to the interface by the tryptophan and neighboring membrane-partitioning residues.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC)<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="fn2">2</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:footnote id="fn2">
                     <ce:label>
                        <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>
                     </ce:label>
                     <ce:note-para id="cenpara10" view="all">The abbreviations used are: PI-PLC, phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C; PI, phosphatidylinositol; PC, phosphatidylcholine; POPC, 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-PC; diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC, diheptanoyl-PC; cIP, 1,2-cyclic inositol phosphate; r.m.s., root mean square.</ce:note-para>
                  </ce:footnote> enzymes catalyze the specific cleavage of the <ce:italic>sn</ce:italic>-3-phosphodiester bond in phosphatidylinositol (PI) and phosphoinositides. PI hydrolysis occurs in two sequential reactions (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib2 bib3">1-3</ce:cross-refs>): (i) an intramolecular phosphotransferase reaction at a phospholipid/aggregate surface to produce diacylglycerol and water-soluble 1,2-cyclic inositol phosphate (cIP) (or cIP<ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">x</ce:inf>
                  </ce:italic> in the case of a phosphoinositide substrate), followed by (ii) a phosphodiesterase reaction where water-soluble cIP is hydrolyzed to inositol-1-phosphate. Since their substrate is presented in an interface, their activity can often be modulated by the composition of the interface. Eukaryotic PI-PLCs, as critical components of phosphatidylinositol-mediated signaling pathways (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>), have multiple domains for regulation of enzyme activity both by regulating partitioning to membranes and by allosteric effects on catalysis, and these are often hard to separate. However, the much smaller bacterial PI-PLC enzymes are secreted, single domain proteins whose crystal structures (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib6 bib7 bib8">6-8</ce:cross-refs>) resemble the catalytic domain of PLCδ<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>, the only mammalian PI-PLC for which there is a structure (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib9 bib10 bib11">9-11</ce:cross-refs>). The molecular topology is that of a distorted TIM barrel. Around the barrel rim, there are regions where hydrophobic side chains are exposed to solvent. The structural similarity of the bacterial PI-PLC to the mammalian catalytic domain and similar kinetic features (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib12 bib13 bib14">12-14</ce:cross-refs>) suggest that the smaller enzyme can provide a simple model for understanding how different interfaces modulate catalysis.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Previous work has shown that the PI-PLC from <ce:italic>Bacillus cereus</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>Bacillus thuringiensis</ce:italic> not only prefers micellar to monomeric substrate (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>) but is also activated by binding to phosphatidylcholine (PC) interfaces (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). The binding has an allosteric effect on the enzyme, since it increases <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                  <ce:inf loc="post">cat</ce:inf> and decreases <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                  </ce:italic> for hydrolysis of the soluble substrate cIP. PC also alters kinetic parameters for PI cleavage (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>). Hendrickson and Hendrickson (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib19">19</ce:cross-ref>) showed that resonance energy transfer occurs between one (or more) of the seven Trp residues in <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> PI-PLC and a dansyl group at the terminus of the <ce:italic>sn</ce:italic>-1 alkyl chain of PC dispersed in a hexadecylphosphorylcholine aggregate. This indicates that there must be some direct partitioning of the protein into the membrane, although it does not indicate the segment(s) of the protein involved. The rim of the (βα)-barrel above the active site of bacterial PI-PLC has a short helix B and one particular loop (<ce:sup loc="post">236</ce:sup>SSGGTAWNS<ce:sup loc="post">244</ce:sup>) that show an unusual clustering of hydrophobic amino acids that are fully exposed to solvent. These two structural units contribute to the binding of the PC activator with both Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> important for the enzyme to bind to interfaces (both activating zwitterionic (PC) and substrate anionic surfaces) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>). The W47A/W242A mutant showed minimal activation of cIP, dramatically reduced PI cleavage, and virtually no binding to PC interfaces, vesicles, or micelles.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">In this work, we report the crystal structure of the W47A/W242A mutant (double mutant of W47A and W242A) PI-PLC. Although the overall structure and the active site of this mutant are very similar to that in the structure of the PI-PLC from <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>), helix B has been disrupted, and the orientation of the loop 236-244 has shifted. More unexpectedly, this mutant protein crystallized as a dimer in the asymmetric unit, the first time a bacterial PI-PLC oligomer has been observed. Complementary binding components and intermolecular interactions (both hydrophobic and hydrophilic) at the dimer interface suggest that the dimer is as stable as those observed in other, naturally occurring protein-protein complexes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>). Fluorescence quenching assays of various PI-PLC constructs with fluorophores introduced in the region of the dimer interface strongly support the idea that binding to PC interfaces promotes formation of the dimer.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle10">EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>PI-PLC Expression, Purification, and Modification</ce:italic>—A plasmid containing the <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC gene was transformed into <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> BL21-Codonplus (DE3)-RIL cells (Stratagene). Overexpression and purification of W47A/W242A followed procedures described previously (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Several cysteine mutants (Y57C, W242C, S250C, and W280C) of PI-PLC were prepared (using QuikChange™ mutagenesis) to introduce extrinsic fluorophores into the protein; all mutated genes were sequenced to confirm that the desired mutation was introduced. These mutants expressed well in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>; purification to &gt;90% as monitored by SDS-PAGE was achieved by chromatography on a Q-Sepharose fast flow column followed by a phenyl-Sepharose column as described for the recombinant enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). The phosphotransferase reaction of PI-PLC cysteine mutants was monitored by following cIP production with <ce:sup loc="post">31</ce:sup>P NMR spectroscopy as described previously (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Assay conditions included 8 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> PI solubilized in 32 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles in 50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES, pH 7.5, at 28 °C. Most of the PI-PLC cysteine mutants had phosphotransferase-specific activities comparable with that for recombinant PI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The one exception was W280C, which was 30% that of the control. The Cys mutants, 1.5-3.0 mg/ml (50-100 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>) in 50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris-HCl, pH 7.3, were derivatized at room temperature with <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-(1-pyrene) iodoacetamide added dropwise from a 15 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> stock solution in dimethylformamide made immediately prior to use and protected from light. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. When the reaction was completed, an excess of mercaptoethanol was added to consume excess thiol-reactive reagent. The reaction product was then extensively dialyzed at 4 °C in 50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris, pH 7.5. Less than 10% Cys remained as monitored by Ellman's reagent (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>). Protein solutions were concentrated using Millipore Centraplus 10 filters; protein concentrations were determined by Lowry or Bradford assays.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para50" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>X-ray Crystallography</ce:italic>—Crystals of PI-PLC W47A/W242A mutant were grown at 17 °C by vapor diffusion in hanging drop against a reservoir solution containing 15% 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol, 16% polyethylene glycol 400, and 0.1 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> citrate buffer at pH 5.4. Crystallographic data were collected at the Brookhaven National Laboratory Synchrotron X8C beam line. Crystals were directly cooled to 80-100 K by vapor nitrogen stream prior to collection. Data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the DENZO and SCALEPACK software packages (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para60" view="all">Phases were provided by molecular replacement using the program EPMR (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>) and previous structures of wild type PI-PLC from <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> (Protein Data Bank entry 1PTD) as a starting model (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>). Model-building and refinement steps were carried out using O (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>) and CNS (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>), respectively. Individual B-factors were refined for all atoms. Data collection and refinement statistics of the crystallization work are presented in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>. The PDB code for this structure is 2OR2. Crystallographic figures were all made through Pymol (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para70" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>POPC Vesicle Binding Assay</ce:italic>—A simple binding assay based on centrifugation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>) was used to characterize the binding of W242C and pyrene-modified W242C and other Cys mutants to sonicated vesicles of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids. Vesicle-bound enzyme (<ce:italic>E<ce:inf loc="post">B</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>) was separated from free enzyme (<ce:italic>E<ce:inf loc="post">F</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>) using an Amicon Centricon-100 filter. The filtrate, containing free enzyme, was lyophilized and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Binding was analyzed using a Langmuir adsorption isotherm (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>), where <ce:italic>n</ce:italic> is the number of lipid molecules in the surface to which each enzyme molecule is assumed to bind, and <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> is the dissociation constant for the binding site composed of <ce:italic>n</ce:italic> lipids. The product “<ce:italic>nK<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>” provides an apparent dissociation constant for comparison of the different PI-PLC proteins.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para80" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Fluorescence Spectroscopy</ce:italic>—The accessibility of a given pyrene-labeled PI-PLC to the water-soluble quenching agent acrylamide was studied in the absence and presence of POPC vesicles prepared by sonication. For quenching experiments, conditions included 0.05 mg/ml (1.4 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>) protein and 1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> POPC vesicles prepared by sonication in 50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> Tris-HCl, pH 7.0. Labeled PI-PLC fluorescence spectra as a function of added acrylamide (from a 2 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> stock solution) were obtained with either a Shimadzu RF5000U spectrofluorometer or a Fluorolog R-3 spectrofluorometer with excitation at 334 nm and emission monitored at 383 nm.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle20">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para90" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Crystal Structure of the W47A/W242A Mutant of PI-PLC</ce:italic>—The <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC W47A/W242A mutant crystallized in the <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf>2<ce:inf loc="post">1</ce:inf> space group with two monomers in the asymmetric unit (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>). The two monomers are structurally very similar to each other, with r.m.s. deviations of 0.291 Å (main chain) and 0.549 Å (overall). Each monomer folds as an incomplete (βα)<ce:inf loc="post">8</ce:inf>-barrel in which the inner layer of the barrel is composed of eight β-strands, surrounded by the six α-helices of the outer layer (the two helices paired with strands IV and V are absent in the bacterial PI-PLC enzymes). In the W47A/W242A crystal structure, the two C-terminal residues of PI-PLC, Lys<ce:sup loc="post">297</ce:sup> and Glu<ce:sup loc="post">298</ce:sup>, are omitted due to lack of electron density. The subunit interface of the pseudo-2-fold symmetric dimer is composed of the 27 residues (9.1% of the total number of residues) from one monomer and the same 27 residues from another. These residues are mostly contributed from helix G, helix B, and the rim loop. The dimer interface is ∼27 Å in length and 26 Å in width, with an accessible surface area of 884 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>, covering 7.5% of the surface area of each monomer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). The contacts between the two monomers are primarily hydrophobic, with 64.3% contributed by nonpolar interacting atoms. These are stabilized further by 10 intermolecular hydrogen bonds (Tables <ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="tbl4">4</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl4"/> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="tbl5">5</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl5"/>). The dimer interface reveals a striking surface complementarity in which the most significant interactions are contributed by a cluster of aromatic residues. The aromatic rings of Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup> from helix G and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup> pack into a quasiherringbone pattern forming a hydrophobic core within the interface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="fig1">Fig. 1<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). The rings of Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">246</ce:sup>, Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">247</ce:sup>, and Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">251</ce:sup> make strong hydrophobic interactions with residues from the other monomer. Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">248</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup> remain confined to their own respective monomers and provide stabilization for the hydrophobic core boundary.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para100" view="all">The effects of the W47A/W242A mutation on PI-PLC structure and function can be seen by comparison with other PI-PLC crystal structures. The amino acid sequence of <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC is similar to that of the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> enzyme, with 97% sequence identity. The only significant differences between the crystal structures of the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild type (Protein Data Bank entry 1PTD) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> W47A/W242A mutant enzymes are localized to the so-called helix B (Ile<ce:sup loc="post">43</ce:sup>-Gly<ce:sup loc="post">48</ce:sup>) and rim loop (Ser<ce:sup loc="post">236</ce:sup>-Ser<ce:sup loc="post">244</ce:sup>) regions (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). These regions, and their Trp residues in particular, have been implicated in binding of the protein to PC bilayers, since replacement of either Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> or Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> with alanine dramatically reduces the affinity of the protein for PC bilayers by an amount comparable with what would be expected if a membrane-soluble Trp side chain were replaced by the much less hydrophobic methyl group of alanine (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>). The “helix B” region of the W47A/W242A mutant adopts an irregular loop conformation rather than the α-helix observed in wild type PI-PLC. The overall shape of the rim loop is similar in the two enzymes, but in the mutant, the loop shifts positionally closer to the lower center of the (βα)-barrel, which changes the relative orientation between the rim loop and helix B. In the wild-type enzyme, the closest Cα-Cα distance between the rim loop and B helix is 5.1 Å from Val<ce:sup loc="post">46</ce:sup> to Gly<ce:sup loc="post">239</ce:sup>, whereas the distance between Val<ce:sup loc="post">46</ce:sup> and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">239</ce:sup> is changed to 13.5 Å in the mutant enzyme. When residues from the helix B and rim loop are omitted from the superposition calculation, the mainchain r.m.s. deviation falls from 1.305 to 0.514 Å, confirming that significant structural differences are confined to these two regions. Comparison of the crystal structures of <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC mutants W47A/W242A and R69D (Protein Data Bank entry 1T6M) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>), which contains a metal ion site introduced into the active site, produces similar results: main-chain r.m.s. deviation values fall from 1.352 Å (all residues) to 0.586 Å with residues at helix B and the rim loop omitted. On the other hand, the main-chain r.m.s. deviation between <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild-type and <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> R69D mutant PI-PLC structures is 0.653 Å for all residues, indicating that despite the species and sequence differences, they retain highly similar conformations in the absence of the W47A/W242A mutation. Of particular note is the observation that the W47A/W242A mutation has no observable effect on active site geometry, which in the mutant aligns closely with the active sites of <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild-type PI-PLC, with or without inhibitors. The r.m.s. deviation for the 13 active site residues (as defined in Ref. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>) is 0.564 Å (all atoms). These comparisons reveal that significant structural effects of the W47A/W242A mutation are limited to the helix B and rim loop regions, leaving the active site geometry unaltered and capable of catalysis.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para110" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Acrylamide Quenching of PI-PLC Mutants</ce:italic>—To evaluate whether the PI-PLC dimer can be detected in an interfacial environment, we initially considered chemical cross-linking (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>); however, a dearth of reactive functionalities in the vicinity of the dimer interface proved to be a serious drawback for this approach. We have chosen to use instead an extrinsic fluorescence approach to evaluate solvent accessibility at selected residue positions in the presence or absence of a PC interface. Since cysteine residues do not occur naturally in PI-PLC, we introduced cysteine residues at positions near the observed dimer interface. Each cysteine-containing mutant was derivatized with <ce:italic>N</ce:italic>-(1-pyrene)iodoacetamide to introduce a pyrene moiety that served as a probe to evaluate accessibility to acrylamide at that position. Acrylamide has no tendency to partition into the interior of the bilayer or the protein core; nor does it concentrate at the bilayer surface. Acrylamide quenching thus reports on surface exposure of fluorescence-labeled residues. Cysteine residues were substituted through mutagenesis for Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> (a loop rim residue that has been implicated in PC membrane partitioning), Ser<ce:sup loc="post">250</ce:sup> (located centrally at the dimer interface and proximal to the rim loop), and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup> (located in the dimer interface but distant from the barrel rim). Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">57</ce:sup>, a surface residue that is not situated at the putative dimer or membrane interface, was included as a control. As shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>, all Cys mutants but W242C exhibited <ce:italic>nK<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> values (for POPC small unilamellar vesicles) comparable with the 64 ± 3 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> previously obtained for recombinant PI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). Previous work showed that W242A lost significant affinity for PC with an apparent dissociation constant of 8600 ± 600 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Interestingly, the W242C <ce:italic>nK<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> value, 200 ± 15 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>, was about 40-fold lower than for W242A and only about 3-fold higher than recombinant PI-PLC. Introduction of the pyrene moiety at this position enhanced its affinity for PC vesicles with <ce:italic>nK<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> reduced to 125 ± 20 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> (2-fold higher than wild type). Thus, all pyrene-labeled PI-PLC enzymes bound effectively to PC vesicles. They also exhibited specific activities toward PI solubilized in diC<ce:inf loc="post">7</ce:inf>PC micelles comparable with recombinant wild-type PI-PLC and much higher than for W47A/W242A (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para120" view="all">In a noninterfacial environment, the pyrene probes attached to Y57C, W242C, S250C, and W280C could be quenched by acrylamide, consistent with substantial solvent exposure for these pyrene labels (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="fig3">Fig. 3<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>). It was further observed that all of the pyrene-labeled PI-PLC mutants except Y57C exhibited nonlinear Stern-Volmer plots. Assuming collisional quenching, this nonlinearity could be explained by two pools of the pyrene: one accessible to quencher (<ce:italic>e.g.</ce:italic> monomer) and the other inaccessible (<ce:italic>e.g.</ce:italic> dimer). If one uses a modified Stern-Volmer equation (<ce:italic>F<ce:inf loc="post">o</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>/Δ<ce:italic>F</ce:italic> = 1/(<ce:italic>f<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>K<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>[Q]) + 1/<ce:italic>f<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>, where <ce:italic>f<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> is the fraction of fluorophore accessible to quenching and <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> is a quenching constant) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>) to fit the data, <ce:italic>f<ce:inf loc="post">a</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> values were 0.56, 0.37, and 0.29 for pyrene attached to W242C, S250C, and W280C in the absence of an interface, and the Stern-Volmer quenching constants (<ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>) for the accessible pyrene-labeled proteins were &gt;3 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Previous studies have shown that wild-type PI-PLC exists predominantly as a monomer in solution (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). However, it is possible that introducing the large pyrene probe makes the dimer interface region more hydrophobic in solution, thus shifting the monomer-dimer equilibrium for the derivatized proteins and giving rise to two fluorophore populations. In contrast, the presence of a phospholipid interface (POPC vesicles) provided nearly complete protection from acrylamide quenching (values of <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> with the Stern-Volmer equation yielded values of &lt;0.5 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>
                     <ce:sup loc="post">-1</ce:sup> for all mutants but Y57C) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="fig3">Fig. 3<ce:italic>B</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">v</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> only decreased a small amount for Y57C-pyrene in the presence of vesicles, indicating that this residue is exposed to solvent whether or not the protein is bound to a vesicle.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para130" view="all">None of the pyrene-labeled proteins showed any excimer bands either free in solution or when bound to PC surfaces, even those attached to residues near the dimer interface. The stacked orientation of two pyrene rings is critical for excimer formation. If each pyrene ring in the dimer adopts a conformation compatible with the herringbone pattern observed at the interface, there would not be efficient excimer formation.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Previous kinetic studies have established that the <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC W47A/W242A mutant is an interfacially impaired enzyme that no longer binds well to activating PC interfaces (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Removal of these two tryptophan residues, singly or together, dramatically increases the apparent <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">D</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> (or <ce:italic>nK<ce:inf loc="post">d</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> in a Langmuir adsorption model for binding (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>)) for partitioning of the enzyme onto PC vesicles, and the magnitude of the change is consistent with the change in free energy for removing a group that partitions into the PC membrane. X-ray crystallography was used to investigate protein structural changes that might be associated with the W47A/W242A mutation and to determine whether such changes might occur in the active site, resulting in a lower <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">cat</ce:inf>. The crystal structure demonstrates that the <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> PI-PLC W47A/W242A mutant retains the overall (βα)-barrel structure (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>) observed in the crystal structure of <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild-type PI-PLC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>). Crystal structures of wild-type PI-PLC complexes with inositol have established that the active site is located inside the β-barrel, and the lower barrel rim allows the substrate entry from the membrane bilayer. Comparison of the wild-type and W47A/W242A mutant structures indicates no significant structural differences between the active sites. However, the W47A/W242A mutant reveals a major conformational change in two regions that have been implicated in membrane binding (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>). The W47A/W242A mutation perturbs the helix B region, which in wild-type PI-PLC forms a short α-helix (residues Ile<ce:sup loc="post">43</ce:sup>-Gly<ce:sup loc="post">48</ce:sup>) that has been proposed to partially insert into membranes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). In wild-type PI-PLC, this helix orients the side chains of Ile<ce:sup loc="post">43</ce:sup>, Val<ce:sup loc="post">46</ce:sup>, and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> so that they pack together and form a plug that extends outwards toward solvent, thus forming a hydrophobic protrusion from the protein surface. This hydrophobic protrusion occurs near the lower rim entrance, where the protrusion could act to stabilize membrane binding and perhaps encourage the proper orientation of the β-barrel with respect to the interfacial surface. The W47A mutation disrupts the helical secondary structure, which in turn alters the relative orientation of these side chains. The Ile<ce:sup loc="post">43</ce:sup> side chain becomes redirected into the protein core, and the Cα-Cα distance between residues 43 and 46 increases from 5.3 to 8.3 Å. These data are consistent with the premise that the decreased catalytic activity of the mutant is due primarily to the loss of its membrane binding capacity. Deficient membrane binding could alter the enzyme activity in various ways (<ce:italic>e.g.</ce:italic> reduction of enzyme-ligand contact or failure to induce an active site environment that promotes catalysis).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para150" view="all">An unexpected feature to emerge from the present studies is the first crystallographic observation of a PI-PLC dimer. Dimers have been observed crystallographically for three other phospholipases (A, A<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>, and the C-terminal tail of phospholipase C-β); however, the first two of these are structurally and functionally distinct from PI-PLC enzymes, and the last is an isolated domain. The outer membrane phospholipase A of Gramnegative bacteria is an integral β-barrel membrane protein that is active <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> as a dimer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>). Dimerization depends on substrate and Ca<ce:sup loc="post">2+</ce:sup> binding, and the complete active site requires dimer formation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>). The anion-assisted dimer of secreted phospholipase A<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>) forms a catalytically inactive enzyme. Avian phospholipase C-β, which possesses a fold unique to these enzymes, forms dimers that mediate interactions with G-proteins (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>). A recent report shows that an isoform of mammalian PI-PLC can also form homodimers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>). Although crystal structures of mammalian PLC-β have yet to be determined, the report implicated the involvement of the catalytic domain in the dimerization. Precedent thus exists for homodimerization in other phospholipases, despite the diversity in structural motifs and functional properties.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para160" view="all">The dimer interface of <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> W47A/W242A PI-PLC exhibits high complementarity, with a striking pseudo-2-fold symmetry in which aromatic side chains form an interdigitating core involving the same residues from each monomer. Such symmetry is observed frequently in macromolecular assemblies. In the W47A/W242A dimer, the monomers are oriented parallel to each other, such that the pseudo-2-fold axis of the dimer interface parallels the axes passing through the two β-barrels. This orientation for the monomers would preserve the interfacial active site accessibility for both. The 2-fold symmetry creates some curvature in the dimer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="fig1">Fig. 1<ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                     </ce:cross-ref>), which may be consistent with the strong preference exhibited for small, highly curved unilamellar vesicles of PC compared with large, more planar bilayer vesicles (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). It is also possible that the dimer may reorient slightly to adopt a more planar shape on a membrane surface. Such a reorientation could be accommodated with only minor adjustments of the aromatic residues at the dimer interface.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para170" view="all">The combined data suggest that PI-PLC from <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> share an inherent ability to form dimers. The residues that comprise the dimer interface are conserved in PI-PLC from both species. In the wild-type PI-PLC crystal structure, these side chains are surface-accessible and available to participate in protein-protein interactions. Given the similarities between the enzymes, it is intriguing that the crystallographic dimer has been observed solely for the W47A/W242A mutant. The data herein suggest that the crystallization behavior is critically influenced by the local conformation of the B helix and barrel rim regions, particularly the Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> side chains. In the conformation observed in the <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild-type PI-PLC crystal structure, the hydrophobic protrusion from the B-helix extends outwards in an orientation that interferes with formation of the lower boundary of the dimer interface; the bulky tryptophan side chains prevent the requisite close intermolecular contacts observed in the dimer structure. However, these regions are conformationally mobile, and partitioning of the protrusion into the membrane could relieve the steric hindrance and permit dimerization. Indeed, in the W47A/W242A mutant, such a conformational transition is effected by the substitution of alanine for the bulky side chains of tryptophans 47 and 242. The double mutation brings the Cα-Cα distance between residues 47 and 242 in the same monomer 2.3 Å closer to each other. The distance between the Ala<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup>/Ala<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> pair from one monomer to the other is also close, ∼5Å(Cα-Cα). These close distances are compatible with dimerization. One possible model for the dimer interface of wild-type PI-PLC, based on the W47A/W242A crystal structure, is shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para180" view="all">The fluorescence and crystallographic data are in agreement and together support a model in which dimer formation is promoted by membrane binding. When PI-PLC is free in solution and not membrane-associated, the corresponding fluorescence data demonstrate that all of the pyrene-labeled residues are exposed to solvent. The crystal structure of <ce:italic>B. cereus</ce:italic> wild-type PI-PLC shows that these same four residues are located at the protein surface. Since dimer formation, as seen in the crystal structure of <ce:italic>B. thuringiensis</ce:italic> W47A/W242A PI-PLC, would be expected to bury Ser<ce:sup loc="post">250</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup>, their significant solvent accessibility in solution is consistent with the monomeric state. However, when PI-PLC is bound to a PC interface, three of the four residues become solventshielded. This shielding could occur if the residues become buried within the dimer interface or by partitioning into the membrane. The crystallographic data for wild type PI-PLC shows that Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> forms part of the barrel rim loop abutting the entrance to the active site. The pyrene derivative of W242C, as part of the rim loop, occupies a position where the Trp side chain has been proposed to insert into the bilayer (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>), helping to anchor the enzyme to the membrane surface. Hence, protection of pyrene at this position from acrylamide quenching in the presence of PC vesicles would be expected. Unlike Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup>, the side chains of Ser<ce:sup loc="post">250</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">280</ce:sup> could not contact the PC interface unless PI-PLC underwent a molecular reorientation on the bilayer surface. However, this would lead to misalignment of the active site entrance, and the catalytic competence of the S250C and W280C mutants argues against this potentiality. The more plausible explanation is that these residues are buried at the protein-protein interface, as depicted in the crystal structure of the PI-PLC W47A/W242A dimer. Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">57</ce:sup>, which is distant from the dimer interface and membrane-binding region, is freely accessible to solvent in both monomer and dimer crystal structures, consistent with the fluorescence data. Presumably, all of these Cys derivatives of PI-PLC are interfacially active because they have both Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> (or the pyrene in the case of the W242C) present as membrane anchors.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para190" view="all">We propose a model in which the dimer interface is substantially stabilized when wild-type PI-PLC is bound to membranes. Partitioning of Trp<ce:sup loc="post">47</ce:sup> and Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> into a PC membrane provides the primary driving force for changes in the rim loop that promote dimer formation. The rim loop is highly mobile in both mutant and wild type structures with much higher B factors than the rest of the structure, and its conformation or position can be easily changed. The proposed dimerization is a dynamic process that occurs in several steps. (i) In the absence of a membrane surface, the Trp side chains (Trp<ce:sup loc="post">242</ce:sup> in its flexible loop as well as the helix B hydrophobic plug) sterically hinder dimer formation. (ii) At the PC membrane surface, the Trp residues partition into the membrane. (iii) This attachment of the Trp “plug” to the PC membrane allows adjacent PI-PLC molecules to approach one another while bound to the membrane interface. (iv) The tyrosine herringbone pattern forms, bringing the two monomers together on the PC interface. Dimer formation should increase the residence time of the protein on a vesicle surface and would lead to enhanced catalysis. There are likely to be other more subtle changes as well, since the enzyme when bound to PC surfaces is much more active toward water-soluble substrates (both diC<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>PI and cIP) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
