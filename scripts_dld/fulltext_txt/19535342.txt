                     Bacillus anthracis elaborates a poly-γ-d-glutamic acid capsule that protects bacilli from phagocytic killing during infection. The enzyme CapD generates amide bonds with peptidoglycan cross-bridges to anchor capsular material within the cell wall envelope of B. anthracis. The capsular biosynthetic pathway is essential for virulence during anthrax infections and can be targeted for anti-infective inhibition with small molecules. Here, we present the crystal structures of the γ-glutamyltranspeptidase CapD with and without α-l-Glu-l-Glu dipeptide, a non-hydrolyzable analog of poly-γ-d-glutamic acid, in the active site. Purified CapD displays transpeptidation activity in vitro, and its structure reveals an active site broadly accessible for poly-γ-glutamate binding and processing. Using structural and biochemical information, we derive a mechanistic model for CapD catalysis whereby Pro427, Gly428, and Gly429 activate the catalytic residue of the enzyme, Thr352, and stabilize an oxyanion hole via main chain amide hydrogen bonds.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara80" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Bacillus anthracis</ce:italic> elaborates a poly-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamic acid capsule that protects bacilli from phagocytic killing during infection. The enzyme CapD generates amide bonds with peptidoglycan cross-bridges to anchor capsular material within the cell wall envelope of <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic>. The capsular biosynthetic pathway is essential for virulence during anthrax infections and can be targeted for anti-infective inhibition with small molecules. Here, we present the crystal structures of the γ-glutamyltranspeptidase CapD with and without α-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu dipeptide, a non-hydrolyzable analog of poly-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamic acid, in the active site. Purified CapD displays transpeptidation activity <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic>, and its structure reveals an active site broadly accessible for poly-γ-glutamate binding and processing. Using structural and biochemical information, we derive a mechanistic model for CapD catalysis whereby Pro<ce:sup loc="post">427</ce:sup>, Gly<ce:sup loc="post">428</ce:sup>, and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup> activate the catalytic residue of the enzyme, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, and stabilize an oxyanion hole via main chain amide hydrogen bonds.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Spores of <ce:italic>Bacillus anthracis</ce:italic> are the causative agents of anthrax disease (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>). Upon entry into their hosts, spores germinate and replicate as vegetative bacilli (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>). The formation of a thick capsule encasing vegetative forms enables bacilli to escape granulocyte0 and macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, and the pathogen eventually disseminates throughout all tissues of an infected host (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>). Bacilli secrete lethal and edema toxins, which cause macrophage necrosis and precipitate anthrax death (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib4 bib5 bib6 bib7">4–7</ce:cross-refs>). The genes providing for toxin and capsule formation are carried on two large virulence plasmids, pXO1 and pXO2, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). Loss of any one plasmid leads to virulence attenuation, a feature that has been exploited for the generation of vaccine-type strains (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib10 bib11 bib12 bib13 bib14">10–14</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Unlike polysaccharide-based capsules that are commonly found in bacterial pathogens, the capsular material of <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic> is composed of poly-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamic acid (PDGA)<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="FN4">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>

All the genes necessary for capsule biogenesis are located in the <ce:italic>capBCADE</ce:italic> gene cluster on plasmid pXO2 (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib15 bib16 bib17 bib18 bib19">15–19</ce:cross-refs>). CapD is the only protein of this cluster that is located on the bacterial surface (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>). CapD shares sequence similarity with bacterial and mammalian γ-glutamyl transpeptidases (GGTs; EC 2.3.2.2) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). GGTs belong to the N-terminal nucleophile hydrolases (Ntn) family (Protein Structure Classification (Class (C), Architecture (A), Topology (T) and Homologous superfamily (H)) (CATH) id 3.60.60.10) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). These enzymes assemble as a single polypeptide chain and acquire activity by undergoing autocatalytic processing to heterodimer.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para130" view="all">Bacterial GGTs catalyze the first step in glutathione degradation. For example, <ce:italic>Helicobacter pylori</ce:italic> GGT removes glutamate from glutathione tripeptide via the formation of a γ-glutamyl acyl enzyme. This intermediate is resolved by the nucleophilic attack of a water molecule, causing the release of γ-glutamate (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>). Mammalian enzymes transfer the γ-glutamyl intermediate to the amino group of a peptide, thereby completing a transpeptidation reaction (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>). The <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic> CapD precursor is also programmed for autocatalytic cleavage (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>). Similar to mammalian GGTs, CapD also catalyzes a transpeptidation reaction; however, this reaction promotes the covalent linkage of PDGA to the bacterial envelope (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). We have recently demonstrated the cell wall anchor structure of capsule filaments in the envelope of <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic>, identifying an amide bond between the terminal carboxyl group of PDGA and the side amino group of <ce:italic>m</ce:italic>-diaminopimelic acid cross-bridges within muropeptides (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). The CapD-catalyzed transpeptidation reaction could be recapitulated <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> using purified recombinant CapD, γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu<ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">n</ce:inf>
                  </ce:italic> peptide, and muropeptide substrates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). In the absence of the physiological nucleophile (muropeptides), CapD acyl intermediates can be resolved by the nucleophilic attack of water to generate hydrolysis products.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Here, we report the high resolution crystal structure of CapD in the absence and presence of a glutamate dipeptide and compare it with the known structures of <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> GGTs. By combining structural, genetic, and biochemical approaches, we identify the unique features of CapD that distinguish the protein from GGTs and detect several residues that are important for CapD autocatalytic cleavage and PDGA processing. This structural information will further the development of small molecule inhibitors that disrupt CapD activity and that may be useful as anti-infective therapies for anthrax.</ce:para>

                           <ce:para id="para160" view="all">DNA from <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic> Ames was isolated using the Wizard Genomic DNA purification kit (Promega, Madison, WI). The DNA sequence of plasmid pXO2 (GenBank accession <ce:inter-ref id="interrefs80" xlink:href="ncbi-n:NC_007323" xlink:type="simple">NC_007323</ce:inter-ref>) was used to design primers to amplify the CapD open reading frame by PCR with the following primer pair: 5′-TACTTCCAATCCAATGCG<ce:underline>TTCAACA</ce:underline>
                              <ce:underline>AAATAAAAGACAGT</ce:underline>-3′ and 5′-TTATCCACTTCCAATGTCA<ce:underline>TTTATTTGATTTCCAAGTTCC</ce:underline>-3′. The amplified product was directly cloned in the ligation-independent cloning vector pMCSG19 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>). This primer design avoids cloning of the putative signal peptide of 28 residues (predicted by the SignalP 3.0 server). pMCSG19 vector encodes the maltose-binding protein (MBP) sequence followed in order by: (i) tobacco vein mottling virus protease cleavage site, (ii) hexa-histine tag (His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf> tag), (iii) TEV protease cleavage site, and (iv) target protein. Upon cleavage with TEV protease, three residues (Ser-Asn-Ala) are added at the N terminus of the target protein.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para170" view="all">For site-directed mutagenesis, the QuikChange site-directed strategy from Stratagene was employed. The pMCSG19-<ce:italic>capD</ce:italic> plasmid was used as a template for amplification of DNA with pairs of primers that introducing a discrete change of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">368</ce:sup>, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup>, Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup>, or Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> to Ala. No other change was introduced during the mutagenesis procedure. All clones were verified by DNA sequencing.</ce:para>

                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">Protein Production and Purification</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para180" view="all">Wild-type and variant CapD proteins were produced as a fusion protein MBP-His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>-CapD produced in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> BL21 (DE3) carrying plasmids pMAGIC that encode three rare <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> tRNAs: Arg (AGG/AGA), Ile (ATA), and pRK1037 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>) (Scientific Reagents, Inc.). Cells were grown in the presence of ampicillin and kanamycin, 100 and 30 μg/ml, respectively, to an absorbance at 600 nm of 0.6, and isopropyl-α-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-thiogalactoside (1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>) was added to induce the expression of the fusion protein. MBP was removed <ce:italic>in situ</ce:italic> upon production of the TVMV protease encoded on plasmid pRK1037. 4 h after the addition of isopropyl-α-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-thiogalactoside, cells were harvested, suspended in 5 volumes of lysis buffer (50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-KOH, pH 8.0, 500 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> NaCl, 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> imidazole, 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> β-mercaptoethanol, and 5% v/v glycerol), and stored at −20 °C. Cells were thawed and lysed by sonication after the addition of protease inhibitors (Sigma, P8849) and lysozyme (1 mg/ml). Cell lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 30,000 <ce:italic>g</ce:italic> (RC5C-Plus centrifuge, Sorval) for 20 min and filtered through 0.45- and 0.22-mm in-line filters (Gelman). His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>-CapD was purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC-I) using a 5-ml HiTrap chelating HP column charged with Ni<ce:sup loc="post">+2</ce:sup> ions and buffer-exchange chromatography on a HiPrep 26/10 desalting column and AKTA<ce:sup loc="post">TM</ce:sup> Xpress (General Electric Health Systems). The His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf> tag was cleaved using recombinant His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>-TEV protease expressed from vector pRK508 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>) (a gift from Dr. D. Waugh, NCI, National Institutes of Health). The protease over target protein ratio was 1:30, and incubation was performed at 4 °C for 72 h, after which the protease was removed using a 3-ml nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid chelating column (Qiagen). The CapD protein was concentrated using a Centricon Plus-20 centrifugal concentrator (Millipore). The buffer was exchanged during this procedure to crystallization Buffer E (20 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-KOH, pH 8.0, 250 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> NaCl, 2 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> dithiothreitol).</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para190" view="all">The selenomethionine (SeMet) derivative of CapD was prepared as described (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). Briefly <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> BL21 (DE3) cells producing MBP-His<ce:inf loc="post">6</ce:inf>-CapD were grown at 37 °C in enriched M9 medium (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>) supplemented with 0.4% glucose, 8.5 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> NaCl, 0.1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> CaCl<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>, 2 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> MgSO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, and 1% thiamine. After the <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>
                              <ce:inf loc="post">600</ce:inf> reached 1.2, 0.01% (w/v) each of leucine, isoleucine, lysine, phenylalanine, threonine, and valine were added to inhibit the metabolic pathway of methionine and promote SeMet incorporation. 60 mg of SeMet was added to 1 liter of culture, and 15 min later, protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> isopropyl-α-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-thiogalactoside. Cells were incubated at 20 °C overnight, harvested, suspended in lysis Buffer D (500 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> NaCL, 50 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-Na, pH 8.0, 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> imidazole, 10 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> β-mercaptoethanol, and 5% v/v glycerol, 50 mg of protease inhibitor mixture), and stored at −80 °C. The SeMet derivative was purified as described earlier.</ce:para>

The assay was performed in 25 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-KOH buffer containing 1% Tween 20 at pH 7.5 and 21 °C. Increase in fluorescence was measured in a FluoroMax-2 (Horiba Jobin Yvon Inc., Edison, NJ).</ce:para>

                           <ce:para id="para230" view="all">CapD crystallization conditions were established with the help of the Mosquito robot (TTP LabTech) using the sitting drop vapor diffusion technique in a 96-well CrystalQuick plate (Greiner Bio-One). Crystallization screens included Index, Crystal Screen (Hampton Research), Wizard (Emerald Biosystems), and SM4 and SM5 (NeXtal from Qiagen). For structure determination, the complete data set was collected from orthorhombic crystals grown in a crystallization solution containing PEG 3350 12.5%, 0.1 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> LiSO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, and 0.1 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-Na, pH 7.0. Prior to data collection, the concentration of the PEG 3350 in the crystallization solution was increased to 32% for cryoprotection, and the crystals were flash cryo-cooled in liquid nitrogen. Three segments of a single, long crystal were used to collect a complete data set for structure determination. In the course of crystal optimization, the monoclinic crystal form was obtained from PEG 3350 15%, PEG 400 8%, 0.05 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> LiSO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, and 0.1 <ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> HEPES-Na, pH 7.0. These crystals diffract to a high resolution of 1.80 Å. Prior to data collection, these crystals were cryoprotected using 15% glycerol, and crystals were flash cryo-cooled in liquid nitrogen.</ce:para>

                        <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">Structure Determination and Refinement</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para250" view="all">The CapD crystal structure was first determined using single-wavelength anomalous diffraction phasing and data obtained from an orthorhombic crystal. 13 selenium sites were located using the program SHELEXD (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>). These selenium sites were used for phasing to 2.30 Å with the program MLPHARE (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>). After density modification (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>), a partial model of 369 residues without side chains was obtained from an automatic model building trial using the program RESOLVE (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>). All the above referenced programs are integrated within the program suite HKL3000 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). Based on the initial partial model, most parts of the CapD monomer were built manually using COOT (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>). The electron density for most of the CapD was of high quality. After several cycles of manual model adjustment and improvement using COOT, the model was refined using REFMAC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>) (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The high resolution structure was obtained using data collected from a monoclinic crystal. The structure was determined using the CapD 2.30 Å structure as a starting model and MOLREP of the CCP4 program suite (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>). The model was refined using REFMAC (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>) (see <ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>).</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>

                           <ce:para id="para260" view="all">The model of CapD with the physiological substrate di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu was created using the Dundee PRODRG server and refined with the monomer library sketcher. The normalized structure of di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu was modeled into the omit electron density map, and real space refinement was performed to the maximum fitness of the ligand to the map. Two orientations of the ligand between enzyme and substrate were considered. Based on the geometry analysis and density fit analysis, the best fitted one was shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                     <ce:section id="cesec150" view="all">
                        <ce:section id="cesec160" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Structure Determination</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para270" view="all">Recombinant CapD lacking the N-terminal signal peptide within the first 28 amino acids (residues 29–528) was produced in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>. The recombinant protein underwent autocatalytic processing to yield a stable heterodimer with large L and small S chains, residues 29–351 and 352–528, respectively. The N-terminal amino acid sequence of the small subunit was verified by Edman degradation. Orthorhombic and monoclinic crystals of the CapD heterodimer were obtained. The structure of CapD was determined by single-wavelength anomalous diffraction phasing of the orthorhombic crystal form and SeMet-labeled protein using HKL3000 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>) and refined to 2.30 Å (<ce:italic>R</ce:italic> = 19.7%, <ce:italic>R</ce:italic>
                              <ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf> = 25.2%) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). This crystal form contained one CapD molecule with bound <ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu dipeptide (di-α-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu), a non-hydrolyzable analog of poly-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamic acid, in the asymmetric unit. The peptide was acquired by CapD during protein expression in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>. The structure of CapD in monoclinic crystal was determined by molecular replacement, using CapD as a starting model, and refined to 1.80 Å (<ce:italic>R</ce:italic> = 19.7%, <ce:italic>R</ce:italic>
                              <ce:inf loc="post">free</ce:inf> = 24.2%). This crystal form contained two CapD molecules in the asymmetric unit, one with bound di-α-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu and one unliganded (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>). Electron densities in both crystals were of high quality and generally continuous. Nevertheless, electron densities for residues 29–45 and 337–351, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> the first 17 and the last 15 residues of the L chain, and residue 528, the last residue of the S chain, were poorly defined, and these residues could not be modeled into the structure. At 1.80 Å, the heterodimer has overall dimensions of 60 × 45 × 35 Å, and the crystal structure confirmed the discontinuity of the peptide backbone between residues 351 and 352, liberating the N terminus of the S chain at Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>. The L and S chains are highly intertwined throughout the structure with a 9,192 Å<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup> surface area buried between them.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec170" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Overall Structure</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para280" view="all">CapD contains two central β-sheets stacked with clusters of 8 and 10 α-helices above and below, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). Together, these secondary structures form a six-layer sandwich (α/α/β/β/α/α), a variation of the four-layer sandwich (α/β/β/α) found in the structure of Ntn hydrolases. In the central β-sheet sandwich, strands are arranged in an antiparallel fashion, and both L and S chains contribute strands to the nearly flat β-sheets. The N-terminal portion of the L chain sequence provides the entire domain folded below the β-sheet sandwich, which is composed of 10 helices arranged in two layers (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). The upper helical domain with eight helices is composed of elements that are derived from both the L and the S chain, whereas a three-stranded mixed β-sheet is provided by the S chain (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>).</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para290" view="all">A large, solvent-accessible groove is located in the middle of the molecule between the lower and upper domains. Three areas of strongly positive electrostatic potential on the surface of CapD form a Y-shape and converge within the deep cleft in the middle of the molecule (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). The active site nucleophile, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, is positioned at the bottom of this cleft. Comparison of the CapD structure with other protein structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) was performed with the SSM server (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>) and identified GGT of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>Thermoplasma acidophilum</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). These proteins display 30–33% overall sequence identity with CapD. An alignment of the primary sequences of CapD and GGT homologues is shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>. Similar to CapD, GGTs adopt the Ntn fold (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref770" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>), which enables members of this family of enzymes to engage in autocatalytic processing. The side chain of the newly liberated N-terminal residue within the S chain is thereby incorporated into a β-sheet, where it can function as a nucleophile during catalysis. Hence, autocatalytic processing is an absolute prerequisite for enzymatic activity, where the nucleophilic attack of an N-terminal cysteine, serine, or threonine residue enables cleavage of scissile amide bonds and the formation of acyl intermediates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref780" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref790" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref800" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>). CapD is most similar to GGTs, where the catalytic residue is the first Thr of the newly processed S chains (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref810" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>). This amino acid is Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> in CapD and Thr<ce:sup loc="post">380</ce:sup> in <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> GGT.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Topology of Ligands in the Binding Pocket</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para300" view="all">A well defined electron density was found in the active site of CapD and could be revealed as di-α-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref820" refid="fig1">Figs. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref830" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic> and <ce:cross-ref id="cecref840" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>). This compound represents a non-hydrolyzable analogue of γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu<ce:italic>
                                 <ce:inf loc="post">n</ce:inf>
                              </ce:italic>. Its presence in the active site is a serendipitous discovery. The γ-carboxylate group of the dipeptide forms a strong salt bridge with Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> and maintains hydrogen bonds with the amide of Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup> and amine of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>. Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> also forms a strong hydrogen bond with the α-carboxylate of glutamate (2.67 Å) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref850" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). In turn, the α-carboxylate of di-α-<ce:small-caps>l</ce:small-caps>-Glu engages additional hydrogen bonds with the amide of Gly<ce:sup loc="post">430</ce:sup> and through a water molecule with the amide of Asn<ce:sup loc="post">373</ce:sup>. Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup>–Gly<ce:sup loc="post">430</ce:sup> are located in a highly conserved sequence segment, GSPGG (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref860" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>); the corresponding glycine pair in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> GGTs has been shown to interact with the substrate, glutathione (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref870" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref880" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>). Two residues are unique to CapD, Phe<ce:sup loc="post">374</ce:sup> and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">172</ce:sup>. The guanidine group interactions with the benzene ring (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref890" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>) may limit the access of water to the active site of CapD and could be responsible for the catalytic attributes of the enzyme toward capsule anchoring rather than PDGA hydrolysis.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para310" view="all">Based on the binding attributes of di-α-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu, the physiological substrate, the di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu peptide, was modeled into the active site of CapD by exploring two possible orientations. The best fit orientation between enzyme and substrate is shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref900" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>. A salt bridge could be formed between the α-carboxylate group of the first glutamate within the substrate and the guanidinium group of Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup>. Additional hydrogen bonds with the amides of Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup>, Gly<ce:sup loc="post">430</ce:sup>, and Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> may allow the catalytic center, the side chain hydroxyl of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, to contact with the α-amino group of the substrate, subsequently forming a covalent bond with the liberated γ-carboxylate of <ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamate. Two additional possible enzyme substrate interactions, between the dipeptide carbonyl and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">432</ce:sup> as well as the second γ-carboxylate and potentially Asn<ce:sup loc="post">431</ce:sup>, may further stabilize the substrate for CapD catalysis. Assuming that the physiological CapD substrate is composed of secreted poly-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu, the modeled interaction with the enzyme can be extended at the C-terminal end of di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu. In contrast, no more than one glutamate residue may be added at the N terminus of the substrate unless the enzyme undergoes substantial conformational changes.</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para320" view="all">Surface elements of CapD were inspected for a possible binding site that could accommodate the second substrate of the enzyme, the amino group of cell wall cross-bridges within muropeptides. A potential ligand binding pocket of CapD with Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup> at its center is located in close proximity to Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> and in immediate vicinity to the α-carboxylate of di-Glu. Although this has not yet been tested experimentally, the surface pocket of CapD could serve as an acceptor site and accommodate the <ce:italic>meso</ce:italic>-diaminopimelate cross-bridge of muropeptides, the acceptor group of the CapD-catalyzed transpeptidation reaction.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec190" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">Comparison of CapD Structures with and without Ligand</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para330" view="all">The CapD structure in monoclinic crystal contained one molecule with ligand and one without, suggesting conformational changes that can be associated with substrate binding (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref910" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). Several α-helices in both the L and the S chains were shifted toward the positively charged surface groove, resulting in a more compact appearance of the enzyme's complex with di-Glu. In addition, the loop region of Pro<ce:sup loc="post">494</ce:sup>–Phe<ce:sup loc="post">497</ce:sup> near the active site became more structured and formed a single-turn helix (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref920" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). Several side chains that contact the ligand and/or control access to the active site also showed significant conformational changes. Notably, Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> assumes a different conformation in the absence of ligand (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref930" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>), which is also observed at low substrate occupancy in one of the crystal forms with ligand (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref940" refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). The conformational changes of Arg<ce:sup loc="post">113</ce:sup>, Phe<ce:sup loc="post">374</ce:sup>, Arg<ce:sup loc="post">408</ce:sup>, Phe<ce:sup loc="post">410</ce:sup> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref950" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>), Asn<ce:sup loc="post">431</ce:sup>, Arg<ce:sup loc="post">432</ce:sup>, Pro<ce:sup loc="post">494</ce:sup>, and Ala<ce:sup loc="post">495</ce:sup> all contribute to substrate binding in the active site (whereas catalytic residues conserved between CapD and GGTs, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup>, Pro<ce:sup loc="post">428</ce:sup>, Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup>, and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">430</ce:sup>, remained unchanged in the presence and absence of ligand (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref960" refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>)).</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec200" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">CapD Active Site Residues That Are Required for Catalysis</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para340" view="all">The structure of unliganded CapD as well as modeling of di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu peptide binding suggested that Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup> and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> may be important for the catalysis of the transpeptidation reaction with PDGA substrate. To test this prediction, Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup> and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> were individually replaced with alanine. Two additional variants introduced alanine substitutions for amino acids Thr<ce:sup loc="post">368</ce:sup> or Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup> as these residues, together with Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> and Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup>, are conserved and positioned in close proximity to the active sites of CapD and GGTs (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref970" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). Edman degradation of the small subunits of each purified variant revealed the sequence TTHFVIID, <ce:italic>i.e.</ce:italic> the expected N-terminal amino acid residues of the S chain. These results demonstrate further that none of the listed mutants abolished the autocatalytic properties of CapD. CapD cleavage of purified PDGA from <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic> was measured by separating reaction products by electrophoresis on a 2.5% agarose gel and staining capsular material with methylene blue. As CapD degradation products cannot be retained in the gel, the absence of capsule staining indicates enzyme cleavage of PDGA. With the exception of the S372A variant, all other CapD mutants displayed defects in their ability to cleave capsular material when compared with the wild-type enzyme (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref980" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>
                              <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>). These findings could be corroborated with a second assay using a γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-glutamate pentamer with the fluorescence quencher ortho-aminobenzoyl (Abz) at the N terminus and the 2,4-dinitrophenyl (Dpn) fluorophore at the γ-carboxyl group (Abz-[γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu]<ce:inf loc="post">5</ce:inf>-Dpn). A strong increase in fluorescence, indicating substrate cleavage, was observed upon incubation of substrate with wild-type CapD. Here again, the S372A variant retained most of the activity of wild-type CapD, whereas T368A, T370A, and R520A displayed no activity (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref990" refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). Thus, although Thr<ce:sup loc="post">368</ce:sup>, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup>, and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> are not required for autocatalytic processing of CapD, these residues proved to be essential for enzyme catalysis. Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup>, on the other hand, appears to be required for full cleavage activity of CapD but is otherwise dispensable for catalysis. Thr<ce:sup loc="post">398</ce:sup> of <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> GGT, a residue that occupies the analogous position to Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup> of CapD, is also dispensable for autoprocessing but necessary for glutathione hydrolysis. Boanca <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1000" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>) proposed that Thr<ce:sup loc="post">398</ce:sup> may assume the important function of orienting the catalytic residue, Thr<ce:sup loc="post">380</ce:sup>, and thereby enhance its reactivity. A similar function could apply to Thr<ce:sup loc="post">370</ce:sup> of CapD.</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="cesec210" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle170">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para350" view="all">We report here for the first time the crystal structure of <ce:italic>B. anthracis</ce:italic> CapD, which shares with other GGT hydrolases the overall Ntn fold as well as conserved amino acid features of their active site. Nevertheless, the CapD structure also displays unique features that likely contribute to the ability of the enzyme to perform a transpeptidation reaction that immobilizes PDGA capsular material in the bacterial cell wall envelope. We presume that the Ntn fold and active site nucleophile at the N terminus of the S chain account for the ability of these enzymes to undergo autocatalytic processing. In Ntn hydrolases, self-cleavage involves the formation of an acyl enzyme intermediate with key catalytic residues and a properly positioned water molecule to resolve the intermediate. Such a water molecule (H<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>O<ce:sup loc="post">41</ce:sup>) is also positioned within the active site of CapD. Cleavage of the Ser<ce:sup loc="post">351</ce:sup>–Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> peptide bond liberates the N-terminal amino group of Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, a prerequisite for subsequent enzyme activation. The P-segment, Cys<ce:sup loc="post">336</ce:sup>–Ser<ce:sup loc="post">351</ce:sup>, is disordered within the structure of CapD, as occurs with other Ntn hydrolases (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1010" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref1020" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>). For example, the crystal structure of a T391A variant of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> GGT, which cannot undergo autocatalytic processing, revealed that the P-segment of the precursor reaches over the active site (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1030" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>). If such a model is transferred to CapD, upon autocatalytic processing, the Cys<ce:sup loc="post">336</ce:sup>–Ser<ce:sup loc="post">351</ce:sup> P-segment moves away from the active site, thereby permitting enzyme access to substrate and catalysis.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para360" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> GGTs display promiscuity for several different small glutamyl substrates but cannot exert transpeptidase activities with a large substrate such as PDGA. Residues surrounding Tyr<ce:sup loc="post">433</ce:sup> form a loop on the surface of <ce:italic>H. pylori</ce:italic> GGT that restricts access of PDGA and other large substrates to the substrate binding cleft (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1040" refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>). In CapD, the bound substrate di-α-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu assumes a similar position to the loop of GGTs; please note that in GGTs, there is an insert of 10 residues between residues 399 and 400 in CapD (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1050" refid="ecomp10">supplemental Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>), resulting in a significantly longer loop that constrains access to the active site in GGTs. Although its active site is widely open in CapD, this enzyme cannot hydrolyze glutathione and cannot be inhibited by known GGT inhibitors (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1060" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). We presume that the remarkable, extended Y-shaped positive electrostatic potential on the surface of CapD contributes to substrate recognition that is polymeric in nature. <ce:italic>In vivo</ce:italic>, CapD transfers poly-γ-glutamate chains to the cell wall, a reaction that can be recapitulated <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> by using muropeptides (transpeptidation reaction) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref1070" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>). Based on sequence and structure comparison, we propose that Ser<ce:sup loc="post">372</ce:sup>, Pro<ce:sup loc="post">427</ce:sup>, Gly<ce:sup loc="post">428</ce:sup>, and Gly<ce:sup loc="post">429</ce:sup> contribute to the CapD transpeptidation reaction by activating Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> and stabilizing an oxyanion hole with classical main chain amide hydrogen bonds. Modeling of di-γ-<ce:small-caps>d</ce:small-caps>-Glu in the active site places the free amino group of the peptide near Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup>, mimicking a cleaved substrate. We further propose that Asn<ce:sup loc="post">431</ce:sup>, Arg<ce:sup loc="post">432</ce:sup>, and Arg<ce:sup loc="post">520</ce:sup> are important in positioning PDGA substrate for catalysis. Thr<ce:sup loc="post">352</ce:sup> is thought to form an acyl enzyme intermediate with the γ-carboxylate of poly-γ-glutamate chains. If the intermediate were resolved by a water molecule, depolymerization of poly-γ-glutamate would occur. If the intermediate were resolved by the cross-bridge amino group from peptidoglycan, transpeptidation of poly-γ-glutamate occurs with covalent attachment of the capsule to the cell wall envelope.</ce:para>
