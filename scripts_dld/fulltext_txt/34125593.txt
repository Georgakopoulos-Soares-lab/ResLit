ABSTRACT 

The mechanisms underlying an in vivo switch in the resistance phenotype of P. aeruginosa after ceftazidime-avibactam treatment was investigated. The initial isolate (a blood culture) was resistant to meropenem but remained susceptible to antipseudomonal cephalosporins and combinations with β-lactamase inhibitors. One week after ceftazidime-avibactam therapy, a subsequent isolate (a rectal swab) recovered from the same patient showed the opposite phenotype. Whole-genome sequence analysis revealed a single SNP difference between both (ST235) isolates, leading to a P162S change in bla GES-5 , creating bla GES-15 . Thus, bla GES-1 , bla GES-5 , and bla GES-15 were cloned and expressed in the wild-type strain PAO1. Susceptibility profiles confirmed the P162S substitution reverted the carbapenemase phenotype determined by the G170S change of GES-5 back into the ESBL phenotype of GES-1. 

KEYWORDS: Pseudomonas aeruginosa , beta-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenemases, drug resistance evolution 

INTRODUCTION 

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a major nosocomial pathogen characterized by an extraordinary capacity for developing resistance by the selection of mutations in chromosomal genes and the expression of horizontally acquired determinants, frequently leading to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR)/extensively drug-resistant (XDR) phenotypes ( 1 ). Moreover, the growing prevalence of nosocomial infections produced by MDR/XDR P. aeruginosa strains is associated with significantly increased morbidity and mortality, since it compromises the selection of effective therapies ( 2 ). Over the past few years, some novel antipseudomonal agents, such ceftolozane-tazobactam and ceftazidime-avibactam, have been introduced in the clinical practice and have helped to mitigate classical β-lactam resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa , including the overexpression of the chromosomal β-lactamase AmpC and/or efflux pumps such as MexAB-OprM ( 3 , 4 ). This enhanced activity compared with other antipseudomonal β-lactams relies on ceftolozane being a poorer substrate for AmpC hydrolysis and efflux pump extrusion or in the potent inhibition of avibactam of class A and C β-lactamases. However, in vitro and in vivo emergence of resistance to these drugs has been demonstrated to be frequently caused by the selection of mutations leading to increased catalytic efficiency of AmpC against cephalosporins and/or reduced inhibition by avibactam ( 5 – 11 ). Here, we show that resistance to novel cephalosporin β-lactamase inhibitor combinations may also emerge in vivo through the selection of mutations leading to the modification of the substrate specificity of horizontally acquired GES β-lactamases. 

A 57-year-old male patient admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of a Spanish hospital (Hospital Central de Asturias) in February 2020 with a diagnosis of hemorrhagic stroke developed a catheter-related bloodstream infection by MDR P. aeruginosa that was treated with ceftazidime-avibactam plus colistin. The infection was cleared but a rectal swab collected 1 week after the initiation of treatment was still positive for MDR P. aeruginosa . The susceptibility profiles of the initial bloodstream and subsequent rectal swab isolates to ticarcillin, piperacillin-tazobactam, aztreonam, ceftazidime, cefepime, ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, imipenem, meropenem, imipenem/relebactam, ciprofloxacin, tobramycin, amikacin, and colistin were determined by broth microdilution using customized Sensititre plates (ref. FRCNRP, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and EUCAST (eucast.org) breakpoints were applied. As shown in Table 1 , the initial bloodstream isolate (as well as a rectal swab isolate obtained at the same time, not shown) was resistant to carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides, but remained susceptible to aztreonam, antipseudomonal cephalosporins, and β-lactam plus β-lactamase inhibitors. The subsequent isolate recovered from a rectal swab 1 week later showed as well resistance to ciprofloxacin and aminoglycoside, but the susceptibility profile to β-lactams was significantly different; the isolate was susceptible to meropenem but resistant to ceftazidime, cefepime, and ceftolozane-tazobactam, and showed increased MICs (from 1 to 8 mg/liter) for ceftazidime-avibactam ( Table 1 ). Thus, in order to analyze clonal relatedness between both isolates and the involved resistance mechanisms, whole-genome sequencing was performed following previously described protocols using Illumina (Miseq) technology ( 12 , 13 ). The presence of horizontally acquired resistance determinants and the sequence type (ST) was determined using online databases ( http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/CGE-1.0/ ). The presence of mutations in a panel of 164 chromosomal genes related to antibiotic resistance (mutational resistome) was investigated as previously described ( 13 ).

In silico multilocus sequence type (MLST) evidence indicated that both isolates belonged to the high-risk clone ST235. Moreover, the analysis of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (variant calling) using the PAO1 core genome for mapping demonstrated their clonal origin, since their sequences were 100% identical, sharing 34.037 SNPs with respect to the reference sequence. The resistome analysis of the initial bloodstream isolate revealed the presence of mutations in oprD (nt1Δ3), mexZ (nt292Δ11), gyrA (T83I), and parC (S87L), along with two horizontally acquired aminoglycoside resistance genes (aa d6 and aac [ 6′ ] -Ib3 ) and a bla GES-5 . The latter codes for the class A carbapenemase GES-5, which differs from the ESBL GES-1 by a single amino acid replacement (G170S) involved in carbapenem resistance ( 14 ). On the other hand, the resistome analysis of the subsequent isolate recovered from a rectal swab 1 week later revealed a single difference with the preceding bloodstream isolate, resulting in a P162S amino acid substitution in bla GES-5 , leading to the recently described bla GES-15 ( 15 ). Thus, in order to demonstrate the implication of this single amino acid replacement in the modified β-lactam resistance profile, bla GES-5 and bla GES-15 were cloned using the respective clinical isolates as the template, along with bla GES-1 (obtained from our laboratory collection). PCR products obtained with upstream (GES-F-EcoRI TCGAATTCGATAATTTCCATCTCAAGGGATC ) and downstream (GES-R-HinDIII, TCAAGCTTCTATTTGTCCGTGCTCAGGAT ) primers were digested with EcoRI or HinDIII, ligated to pUCP24, and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α made competent by CaCl 2 . Transformants were selected in 5 mg/liter gentamicin Xgal-IPTG (isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside) LB agar plates. The cloned genes obtained from two independent experiments were fully sequenced to ascertain the absence of mutations introduced during PCR amplification. Resulting plasmids were electroporated into PAO1 and plated in 30 mg/liter gentamicin on LB agar plates. MICs of a panel of antipseudomonal β-lactams for PAO1 derivatives producing GES-1, GES-5, and GES-15 variants are shown in Table 1 . As expected according to previous works ( 15 , 16 ), the G170S amino acid substitution of bla GES-5 , compared with bla GES-1 , resulted in significantly increased MICs of meropenem (from 1 to 8 mg/liter) and significantly decreased MICs of ceftazidime (from >64 to 2 mg/liter) and ceftolozone/tazobactam (>32 to 2 mg/liter), as well as a modest decrease in the MIC of ceftazidime-avibactam (from 4 to 1 mg/liter). On the other hand, remarkably, the acquisition of the additional P162S change by bla GES-5 (leading to bla GES-15 ) fully restored the GES-1 initial phenotype, significantly decreasing meropenem MICs and significantly increasing ceftazidime and ceftolozane-tazobactam MICs ( Table 1 ). Therefore, this work highlights the major impact of novel cephalosporin β-lactamase inhibitor combinations in the evolution of the spectrum of β-lactamases, adding GES to the growing list of P. aeruginosa AmpC ( 5 , 6 ), OXA ( 17 , 18 ), and KPC ( 19 , 20 ) enzymes. The increasing prevalence of GES enzymes both in P. aeruginosa and Enterobacterales ( 21 – 23 ) enhances the implications of our findings. Moreover, this emerging scenario, in which development of resistance to novel cephalosporin β-lactamase inhibitor combinations restores carbapenem susceptibility and vice versa, offers a unique opportunity for innovative treatment strategies such as antibiotic rotations and highlights the importance of antimicrobial stewardship measures to combat infections by MDR Gram-negative pathogens.
