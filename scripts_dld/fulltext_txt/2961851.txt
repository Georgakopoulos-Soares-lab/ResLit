                  A prospective study of the medical care utilization experience of 205 severely-disabled independently-living adults in Eastern Massachusetts shows that there was a mean of 0.83 ± 1.26 hospital admissions, 9.9 ± 22.7 hospital days, 1.5 ± 2.31 emergency room (ER) visits, and 26.88 ± 44.4 outpatient contacts per person per year. Among those hospitalized, the mean experience was 16.2 ± 27.1 days per person per year; mean length-of-stay was 9.3 ± 14.7 days per admission.
                  Regression analysis indicates that those with spinal cord injuries as major disabling conditions were significantly more likely to be hospitalized. So were those with lower self-assessments of health, higher levels of depressions, and more baseline ER visits. Self-assessment of health is a significant predictor of hospital days for the total cohort (including those with no admissions); so are age at onset of disability (greater age; higher risk), and bed disability days in the month before the baseline survey (more disability days; higher risk). Among those hospitalized, the total number of days hospitalized is significantly related to both age at onset of disability (later onset; more days) and baseline days hospitalized (greater number; more days). Lengths-of-stay are significantly related to two factors; age and age at onset of disability (in both cases, greater age associated with longer stays).
                  Prior ER visits are a significant predictor of subsequent ER visits (more baseline; more subsequent); so are respondents' reported satisfaction with their participation in their medical care (lower reported satisfaction; more ER visits), organizational affiliations, and frequencies of contacts with friends or relatives. Higher levels of social interaction (.e. organizational affiliation and more frequent social contacts) were associated with more ER visits.
                  Prior contacts with physicians, nurse-practitioners, or physician-assistants was the most powerful predictor of subsequent outpatient contacts (more baseline;,more subsequent). There were also significant relationships between subsequent contacts and respondents' assessments of their health relative to others with similar disabilities (relatively worse health; more contacts), age (greater age; more contacts), and baseline ER visits (more visits; more contacts).

(Received in revised form 5 June 1987)    Abstract-A prospective study of the medical care utilization experience of 205 severely-disabled independently-living adults in Eastern Massachusetts shows that there was a mean of 0.83 t 1.26 hospital admissions, 9.9 t 22.7 hospital days, 1.5 t 2.31 emergency room (ER) visits, and 26.88 t 44.4 outpatient contacts per person per year. Among those hospitalized, the mean experience was 16.2 t 27.1 days per person per year; mean length-of-stay was 9.3 f 14.7 days per admission.   Regression analysis indicates that those with spinal cord injuries as major disabling conditions were significantly more likely to be hospitalized. So were those with lower self-assessments of health, higher levels of depressions, and more baseline ER visits. Self-assessment of health is a significant predictor of hospital days for the total cohort (including those with no admissions); so are age at onset of disability (greater age; higher risk), and bed disability days in the month before the baseline survey (more disability days; higher risk). Among those hospitalized, the total number of days hospitalized is significantly related to both age at onset of disability (later onset; more days) and baseline days hospitalized (greater number; mote days). Lengths-of-stay are significantly related to two factors; age and age at onset of disability (in both cases, greater age associated with longer stays).   Prior ER visits are a significant predictor of subsequent ER visits (more baseline; more subsequent); so are respondents' reported satisfaction with their participation in their medical care (lower reported satisfaction; more ER visits), organizational affiliations, and frequencies of contacts with friends or relatives. Higher levels of social interaction (.e. organizational atiation and more frequent social contacts) were associated with more ER visits.   Prior contacts with physicians, nurse-practitioners, or physician-assistants was the most powerful predictor of subsequent outpatient contacts (more baseline;, more subsequent). There were also significant relationships between subsequent contacts and respondents' assessments of their health relative to others with similar disabilities (relatively worse health; more contacts), age (greater age; more contacts), and baseline ER visits (more visits; more contacts). The establishment of independent living (IL) programs in the early 1970's has enabled many disabled people to live autonomously in community settings with only a minimum of professional and paraprofessional care [1].   Nevertheless, there continue to be a number of barriers to independent living -[l, 2] among which disabled persons' risk of frequent and prolonged hospitalizations most seriously threatens IL program goals. Hospitalization is  164    costly, with most of the cost borne by Medicaid and Medicare. It is also disruptive of work, education, and personal relationships. Therefore, for both economic and philosophic reasons there is considerable interest in reducing both numbers of admissions and lengths-of-stay (LOS). Prepayment and managed care are possible means to these ends. Recent changes in both Medicaid and Medicare regulations encourage the enrollment of aged, poor, and disabled people in prepaid managed medical care programs, to promote independence and to reduce utilization and costs [3-5].   Prepaid managed care programs-most notably health maintenance organizations (HMOs)-have reduced costs of medical care in relatively young and healthy working populations, mainly by reducing hospital admissions [6]. There is some evidence that they have also done so for certain-mainly younger and healthier-older adults [7-10], and that they may be able to do so for other higher-risk populations, including those in IL programs [11, 12]. However, with few data on the risk factors associated with hospitalization of people in IL programs and so many methodological problems which limit their use, it is difficult to develop capitation formulae, managed care protocols, or other programs, to prevent some proportion of hospitalizations. It is difficult even to know whether prevention is even a feasible goal. If excess utilization is a result mainly of intrinsic factors related to the pathophysiology or psychology of disability, it is less likely that managed care will substantially reduce hospital utilization. If, on the other hand, excess utilization reflects system factors-for example, reimbursement, organization, and financing of services-it is more likely that managed care programs could reduce utilization and, in that way, reduce costs.   The current data are derived from a prospective evaluation of an experimental program of medical care delivery to people with major disabilities. A previous analysis of these data suggests that people in the experimental group (N = 59 volunteers) with higher baseline ER utilization had significantly fewer hospital admissions than controls (N = 146; p = 0.0055). Experimentals with better self-assessments of health experienced significantly fewer hospital days per person (p = 0.0075) and days per person hospitalized (p = 0.0056), and experimentals who reported that they had organizational affiliations reported significantly    ALLAN R. The analysis presented in this paper addresses the social, economic, medical, and psychological risk factors associated with the use of inpatient, outpatient, and emergency care.    REVIEW     The epidemiological literature suggests that IL program participants, even more than most disabled people, experience relatively frequent hospitalizations and prolonged hospital stays [1, 2]. Young and Northrup, for example, found that in the second and third years following spinal cord injuries (SCI), their sample of 383 people experienced means of 17 and 11 hospital days/person/year, respectively [13]. DeJong, in a secondary analysis of data on 108 SCI survivors who were between 2 and 4 years post-injury, found that 54% had been hospitalized at least once during the year before the analysis, and that the mean experience was 29.5 hospital days/person/year [1].   Young and Northrup found that higher risk was associated with both level of injury (quadriplegia higher than paraplegia) and elapsed time since SCI (longer time associated with lesser risk) [13]. DeJong, too, found that risk was associated with degree of disability, with quadriplegics' experiencing more admissions and longer stays [1]. Zook and Moore reached a similar conclusion in their analysis of 86 records from a Veteran Aministration SCI facility: 70% of admissions were repeat admissions for complications of SCI [14].   Anderson and Anderberg have suggested that people with SCIs who have unmet needs for help with skin care are more likely to develop pressure sores, a common reason for hospitalization; so are those who are unemployed [15]. Lamid and El Ghatit, in a study of 38 hospitalized veterans with SCIs found a significant correlation between tobacco smoking and the severity of pressure sores [16]. There are also suggestions from other studies that personality factors, locus-of-control, use of alcohol and cannabis, and "sedentary" life-styles may also play major roles [1, 17].   Suggestive as these studies are, they have a number of methodological limitations. Their results are inconsistent and sometimes contradictory. They refer mainly to people with SCIs, whose experiences may differ from those with other disabling conditions [1]. They are often based upon small and non-random  Epidemiology of Medical Care Utilization    samples. They are mainly retrospective studies. They rely heavily on data from a small number of specialized facilities, mainly VA hospitals or specialized rehabilitation or SCI centers, whose patients or whose treatment protocols may not be representative of the care available to most disabled people,, and they rely mainly upon data from medical records, so there is little ability to control for the effects of such factors as household composition, employment status, morale, or compliance with medical regimens.   The current study tries to address some of these problems and determine factors which affect the utilization of medical services by a cohort of severely-disabled independently-living adults.    METHODS AND MATERIALS     Study subjects were recruited from Independent Living programs in the Boston and Worcester, Massachusetts areas. The Boston Center had been instrumental in the development of an experimental care program [18]. The Worcester Center was selected because of its proximity to Boston and because its administrators were supportive of the research. No other program was asked to participate in the research. All participants agreed to a baseline interview, a series of 18 monthly telephone contacts, exit interviews, and a medical record review.    Enrollment and interviews     There were contacts with all of the clients of both Independent Living centers who met the criterion of eligibility for personal care services (N = 272). Power calculations indicated that this cohort size was sufficiently large to show the hypothesized effect on utilization. Of the 272, 234 (86%) agreed to participate.   Interviews averaged 60 minutes (range: 35-90 minutes). Comparisons between the first 210 participants' and non-participants' ages and genders, the only data about non-participants which were, available to the researchers, indicated no systematic differences between those who agreed to interviews and those who refused. Data analysis     To minimize the recall bias, patients reported their utilization experiences on a monthly basis. Each individual's data were converted to annualized rates (for hospital admissions and number of days hospitalized) or average monthly rates (for doctor-nurse contacts). Length of stay was computed for each study subject who was hospitalized by dividing the annualized number of days by the annualized number of hospital admissions. Respondents' reported reasons for admissions and ER visits were categorized.   Weighted averages were computed for each utilization measure, weighting by the numbers of monthly contacts during the follow-up period, to account for the varying numbers of observations for each person. Comparisons of the changes in utilization (subsequent to baseline) were computed by weighted repeated measure analysis of variance techniques [21], analogous to a weighted paired t-test.   Multiple regression analyses were used to examine predictors  utilization, weighting by the numbers of contacts during the follow-up period [23]. DISCUSSION     An earlier analysis of these data has shown that the managed care program reduced the utilization of certain kinds of medical care services for some sub-sets of the participants in the    managed care program, but not for the program participants as a group [18]. The program's effects on these sub-sets may have attenuated the effects of other variables upon utilization. This potential confounding, in turn, may limit the generalizability of these findings to clients of other Independent Living programs not involved with managed care, or to clients of other managed care programs not affiliated with Independent Living groups.   Two other confounders may also affect the ability to generalize from these data. First, in October 1982, Massachusetts enacted Chapter 372, the state's hospital cost containment law, a waivered alternative to Medicare DRG reimbursement. Following its enactment, both hospital admissions and lengths-of-stay declined in Massachusetts, as they did for the cohort. It is unclear how Chapter 372 affected thle use of emergency services, but it may have contributed to the cohort's dramatic and significant increase in ER visits: i.e. people who might previously have been admitted through the ER and, therefore, treated as admissions, are now treated in the ER, discharged, and recorded as ER visits. Though the data must be interpreted with considerable caution, they confirm some established ideas about the factors which predict the    171    use of medical care services by high-risk populations and they introduce others which are new.  Medical care utilization by the severely disabled adults in this cohort reflects a combination of    seven kinds of factors (cf. Tables 1 and 2): demographic (age, which is related to three outcomes, and age at onset of disability); med-  172    ical (SCI as a major disabling condition); health status (self-assessment of health, which is related to three outcomes and bed disability days); social interaction (frequency of contacts and organizational affiliations); satisfaction with medical treatment; prior medical care utilization (baseline ER experience), and emotional (i.e. depression). Together, these factors explain between 20% and 37% of the variance in subsequent medical care utilization among the cohort (Table 3). Anecdotes and conventional wisdoms notwithstanding, there were no statistically significant relationships among ADL, unmet ADL needs, IADL problems, utilization of formal IL services, alcohol/tobacco/drug use, orientation to independent living, and any of the outcome measures.   While some of the factors which affect utilization are fixed and immutable (e.g. age, age at onset of disability, and the nature of one's disabling condition), others are, at least in principle, amenable to change. An earlier analysis of these data suggests that managed medical care, including home care and continuity of care between ambulatory and inpatient settings, affects utilization by some subgroups of independently-living people with major disabilities [18]. This analysis suggests that other kinds of changes in the organization and financing of services may have similar effects. Depression, for example, which is associated with more hospital admissions, the most costly health service, is amenable to treatment. If mental health services were readily accessible and available to IL populations, numbers of hospital admissions may decline. While such services may represent additional costs, especially if the services are truly accessible (i.e. in barrier-free environments with the possibility of home visits), they might also lead to substantial savings of expenditures for hospital services. These savings could, in turn, either be directed to the development of additional IL services or to the reduction of total care costs.   Even if there were no savings, guarantees of meaningful access to health care services by people with major disabling conditions would be consistent with our societal commitment to those with chronic health care needs.             <ce:abstract class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">A prospective study of the medical care utilization experience of 205 severely-disabled independently-living adults in Eastern Massachusetts shows that there was a mean of 0.83 ± 1.26 hospital admissions, 9.9 ± 22.7 hospital days, 1.5 ± 2.31 emergency room (ER) visits, and 26.88 ± 44.4 outpatient contacts per person per year. Among those hospitalized, the mean experience was 16.2 ± 27.1 days per person per year; mean length-of-stay was 9.3 ± 14.7 days per admission.</ce:simple-para>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">Regression analysis indicates that those with spinal cord injuries as major disabling conditions were significantly more likely to be hospitalized. So were those with lower self-assessments of health, higher levels of depressions, and more baseline ER visits. Self-assessment of health is a significant predictor of hospital days for the total cohort (including those with no admissions); so are age at onset of disability (greater age; higher risk), and bed disability days in the month before the baseline survey (more disability days; higher risk). Among those hospitalized, the total number of days hospitalized is significantly related to both age at onset of disability (later onset; more days) and baseline days hospitalized (greater number; more days). Lengths-of-stay are significantly related to two factors; age and age at onset of disability (in both cases, greater age associated with longer stays).</ce:simple-para>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">Prior ER visits are a significant predictor of subsequent ER visits (more baseline; more subsequent); so are respondents' reported satisfaction with their participation in their medical care (lower reported satisfaction; more ER visits), organizational affiliations, and frequencies of contacts with friends or relatives. Higher levels of social interaction (.e. organizational affiliation and more frequent social contacts) were associated with more ER visits.</ce:simple-para>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all">Prior contacts with physicians, nurse-practitioners, or physician-assistants was the most powerful predictor of subsequent outpatient contacts (more baseline;,more subsequent). There were also significant relationships between subsequent contacts and respondents' assessments of their health relative to others with similar disabilities (relatively worse health; more contacts), age (greater age; more contacts), and baseline ER visits (more visits; more contacts).</ce:simple-para>
