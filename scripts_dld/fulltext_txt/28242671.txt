ABSTRACT 

The mcr-1 gene was detected in 5.11% (58/1136) of Escherichia coli isolates of chicken origin from 13 provinces in China. A novel mcr-1 variant, named mcr-1.3 , encoding an Ile-to-Val functional variant of MCR-1 was identified in a sequence type 155 (ST155) strain. An mcr-1.3 -containing IncI2 plasmid, pHeN867 (60,757 bp), was identified. The transfer of pHeN867 led to a 32-fold increase in the MIC of colistin in the recipient, exhibiting an effect on colistin resistance that was similar to that of mcr-1 . 

KEYWORDS: E. coli , colistin resistance, mcr-1.3 , plasmid 

TEXT 

Polymyxins (polymyxin B and colistin) are a last-resort treatment for infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria ( 1 ). In veterinary use, colistin is administered with food in pig and poultry farming to prevent infections caused by pathogens ( 2 ). The mcr-1 gene, which confers plasmid-mediated colistin resistance to Enterobacteriaceae , was identified in an IncI2 plasmid from Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae in China in 2016 ( 3 ). The mcr-1 gene found in E. coli ( 4 ), K. pneumoniae ( 5 ), and Salmonella spp. ( 6 ) has been proven to disseminate ubiquitously. The transmission of mcr-1 -mediated colistin resistance between animals and human has been a threat to human health. It has also been demonstrated that the mcr-1 gene can coexist with bla CTX-M ( 5 ), bla NDM ( 7 ), and other resistance genes ( 4 ), which threatens a return of untreatable infections worldwide. Previous reports described the unique mcr-1 gene sequence compared with that of the originally published sequence ( 3 ), which indicates that mcr-1 is relatively conserved. Recently, a point mutation of A→T at position 8 in mcr-1 was identified in K. pneumoniae ( 8 ). To investigate the epidemiology of mcr-1 and its variant, E. coli isolates collected from chickens nationwide in China were assessed. 

In total, 1,136 nonduplicate E. coli isolates were collected between 2010 and 2015 from sick chickens in 20 provinces and municipalities in China. All of these isolates were preliminarily screened on Mueller-Hinton agar medium with 2 μg/ml colistin. Because the cooccurrence of mcr-1 with bla CTX-M may accelerate the transmission of resistance to colistin and cephalosporins, the mcr-1 ( 3 ) and bla CTX-M ( 9 ) genes were detected by PCR amplification of the isolates with resistance to colistin. The corresponding primers used to amplify the whole mcr-1 gene and parts of the IS Apl1 element are listed in Table S1 in the supplemental material. For all of the positive PCR products of mcr-1 , Sanger sequencing was performed (Tsingke Biological Technology, Chengdu, China) by using a DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). We found a total of 58 (5.11%) mcr-1 -positive isolates, including one isolate harboring the mcr-1 gene with mutations not found in the originally published gene sequence ( 3 ). MICs of colistin for those isolates ranged from 4 to 16 μg/ml. Of 20 different isolate sources, 13 were mcr-1 positive ( Fig. 1 ; see also Fig. S1), which indicates the prevalence of mcr-1 in various areas in China. For the mcr-1 -positive isolates, 18 were identified in Sichuan Province, and fewer were found in Hubei, Henan, and Shandong provinces (see Table S2 in the supplemental material). China is the largest poultry and pig producer worldwide, and the extensive use of colistin in animals has resulted in a high selective pressure in the veterinary environment ( 3 ). Of 58 mcr-1 -positive isolates collected, 54 (93.1%) were multidrug-resistant (MDR) (resistant to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents) ( Fig. 1 ). Most of the MDR isolates were resistant to quinolones, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and sulfonamides. It is worth noting that among these 58 isolates, 25 (43.1%) also carried CTX-M-type extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes ( Fig. 1 ), which revealed the high frequency of mcr-1 and bla CTX-M genes coexisting in E. coli . Additionally, 36 (62.1%) carried the mcr-1 gene flanked by IS Apl1 elements ( Fig. 1 ).

The genetic relatedness of mcr-1 -positive isolates was investigated by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) using total DNA digested with XbaI (XbaI-PFGE) ( 10 ). Various PFGE patterns demonstrated that these isolates are nonclonal ( Fig. 1 ). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) for mcr-1 -positive E. coli isolates was conducted as previously described ( 11 ). MLST revealed 40 distinct sequence types (STs), which showed abundant diversity among mcr-1 -positive isolates from different geographic locations ( Fig. 1 ). Notably, ST48 (eight isolates from three different provinces), ST10 (four isolates from two provinces), ST616, and ST88 were the most common. In comparison with a recent study on the epidemiology of mcr-1 -positive E. coli isolates from humans and retail meats in Taiwan, we found ST117, ST101, and ST648 with different antimicrobial resistance profiles ( 12 ), suggesting differences between various geographical regions and that the mcr-1 -positive E. coli isolates have diverse STs. 

Interestingly, the ST155 E. coli strain HeN867, isolated on 5 January 2013 from the liver of a sick chicken with typical symptoms of colibacillosis, harbored the mcr-1 gene with point mutations. Two adjacent mutations, +111A/G and +112A/G, were confirmed in mcr-1 in HeN867 (see Fig. S2). +111A/G is a synonymous mutation; however, +112A/G will result in an amino acid change (I38V). I38V is located in the transmembrane α-helices of the MCR-1 protein but is not located in the two functional domains of the MCR-1 protein. Homology modeling of the whole protein structure with the SWISS-MODEL server ( http://swissmodel.expasy.org/ ) showed the same protein structure following the exchange of 38I→V. This sequence was named mcr-1.3 according to the reference for resistance gene naming and numbering ( 13 ). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an mcr-1 variant in E. coli . The MIC of colistin for HeN867, based on CLSI guidelines, was 8 μg/ml ( 14 ), which is similar to that for mcr-1 , which confers an MIC of colistin in the range of 4 to 8 μg/ml ( 3 ). Moreover, strain HeN867 was highly resistant to all quinolones tested (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and nalidixic acid) but remained susceptible to other antimicrobial agents tested in this study. 

Conjugation experiments, S1-PFGE ( 15 ) and typing of plasmid, ( 16 ) showed that mcr-1.3 exists in a conjugative IncI2-type plasmid, named pHeN867, that is approximately 60 kb (see Fig. S3 in the supplemental material). pHeN867 can be successfully transferred from E. coli HeN867 to E. coli J53Az r with a frequency of 2 × 10 −3 per recipient cell. The MIC of colistin in transconjugants was 8 μg/ml, exhibiting a 32-fold increase compared to that in the recipient E. coli J53Az r (see Table S3 in the supplemental material). 

Plasmid pHeN867 was extracted from transconjugants using a Qiagen (Hilden, Germany) midi kit. Plasmid sequencing was carried out using an Illumina MiSeq platform and a 350-bp paired-end library (MajorBio Company, Shanghai, China). Predicted gaps were closed by PCR and Sanger sequencing using specifically designed primers listed in Table S1. The complete sequence of the 60,757-bp plasmid was found to encode 83 open reading frames (ORFs), and the overall GC content is 42.51% ( Fig. 2 ). A BLASTn comparison was carried out between pHeN867 and previously sequenced plasmids harboring mcr-1 . A 1,331-bp insertion of the IS 2 element into pilU in pAF23 ( KX032519 ) causes the main difference between pHeN867 and pAF23 ( Fig. 2 ). Unlike pHNSHP45 ( 3 ), the first identified plasmid harboring mcr-1 , pHeN867, lacks the IS Apl1 element, which causes a 1,070-bp difference from pHNSHP45 ( Fig. 2 ). There are four currently known kinds of genetic environments for the mcr-1 gene. In the first environment, the mcr-1 gene is flanked by intact IS Apl1 elements ( 3 ). The second environment involves the mcr-1 gene being flanked by a truncated IS Apl1 element which lacks the tnpA gene ( 17 , 18 ). The mcr-1 gene may also be associated with other mobile elements ( 19 ) or exist separately ( 17 ). Moreover, our plasmid lacks an IS 679 element (2,703 bp) that exists in pHNSHP45, which is consistent with the plasmid context of pAF23. Another significant difference between pHeN867 and pHNSHP45 is the shufflon segments located between the rci and pilV genes. pHeN867 contains segment A (574 bp), segment C (499 bp), and a fusion of segments B and D (568 bp), the same components but with a different order than that of another IncI2-type plasmid, R721 ( 20 ). In contrast, the shufflon region in pHNSHP45 showed a 507-bp difference from pHeN867, which results from the lack of segment C (499 bp) and an 8-bp flanking sequence (GGAGGCCA) between segment C and the fusion region of segments D and B.

Genetic stability tests, growth curve measurements, and growth competition experiments were conducted as previously described ( 21 ). Results showed that pHeN867 existed in the host strain after continuous passage 40 times in 20 days, which suggests that pHeN867 harboring the mcr-1.3 gene can live steadily in a host. The transconjugants harboring pHeN867 showed no significant differences in growth rates compared with E. coli J53Az r (see Fig. S4a in the supplemental material), which indicated that the transconjugants attained stable transmission capacity. A fitness cost for transconjugants harboring pHeN867 was observed when they were grown together with E. coli J53Az r (see Fig. S4b), with a competitive disadvantage of ca. 4.6% per 10 generations relative to transconjugants (see Fig. S4c). These genetic stability and transmission ability characteristics of pHeN867 may help the dissemination and persistence of the mcr-1.3 gene in Enterobacteriaceae spp. 

Here, we confirmed a high frequency of coexistence of mcr-1 and bla CTX-M genes in E. coli from chickens in China. The possible prevalence of an ST10 complex harboring mcr-1 was also observed in this study. Moreover, we have reported the complete sequence of a plasmid harboring a new variant of the mcr-1 gene. Compared with the mcr-1 gene, the mcr-1.3 gene has a similar effect on colistin resistance.
