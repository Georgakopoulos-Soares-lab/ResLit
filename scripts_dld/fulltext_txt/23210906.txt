                  The aims of our study were to analyse the risk factors for colonization by Extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL)-produclng Proteus mirabilis (ESBL-PM) in rehabilitation patients and to characterize the molecular features of these strains. The study was conducted in two rehabilitation centres located in Rome, Italy (Fondazione Santa Lucia IRCCS (FSL)), and Tel-Aviv, Israel (Tel-Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TASMC)). Carriage of ESBL-PM was surveyed by rectal swabs. Strain typing was performed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE). Identification of ESBL genes was done by PCR and sequencing. Patients admitted to the same institutions without ESBL carriage were included as controls. The study group included 70 and 41 patients from FSL and TASMC, respectively. In FSL, the multivariate analysis identified severe acute brain injury (OR = 15, 95% CI = 3.2–69.5, p 0.001), decubitus ulcer (OR = 3.5, 95% CI = 1.2–9.8, p 0.018) and recent treatment with quinolones (OR = 5.7, 95% CI = 1.07–30.1, p 0.042) as independent risk factors. ESBL-PM carriers stayed longer in the hospital on average and were less likely to be discharged home. No significant risk factor was identified in TASMC. There were no similarities in PFGE types or ESBL genes between the ESBL-PM isolates from the two institutions. In both hospitals, a variety of PFGE types existed but a single ESBL type predominated, namely TEM-92 in FSL (n = 64/70; 91%) and CTX-M-2 in TASMC (n = 37/41; 90%). A new TEM ESBL variant, TEM-177 was identified in FSL. The clonal diversity and the predominance of a single ESBL type suggested that horizontal gene transfer played an important role in dissemination of resistance. The development of a population analysis tool that would allow tracing deeper genetic relationships is required.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara60" view="all">The aims of our study were to analyse the risk factors for colonization by Extended-spectrum <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamases (ESBL)-produclng <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic> (ESBL-PM) in rehabilitation patients and to characterize the molecular features of these strains. The study was conducted in two rehabilitation centres located in Rome, Italy (Fondazione Santa Lucia IRCCS (FSL)), and Tel-Aviv, Israel (Tel-Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TASMC)). Carriage of ESBL-PM was surveyed by rectal swabs. Strain typing was performed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE). Identification of ESBL genes was done by PCR and sequencing. Patients admitted to the same institutions without ESBL carriage were included as controls. The study group included 70 and 41 patients from FSL and TASMC, respectively. In FSL, the multivariate analysis identified severe acute brain injury (OR = 15, 95% CI = 3.2–69.5, p 0.001), decubitus ulcer (OR = 3.5, 95% CI = 1.2–9.8, p 0.018) and recent treatment with quinolones (OR = 5.7, 95% CI = 1.07–30.1, p 0.042) as independent risk factors. ESBL-PM carriers stayed longer in the hospital on average and were less likely to be discharged home. No significant risk factor was identified in TASMC. There were no similarities in PFGE types or ESBL genes between the ESBL-PM isolates from the two institutions. In both hospitals, a variety of PFGE types existed but a single ESBL type predominated, namely TEM-92 in FSL (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 64/70; 91%) and CTX-M-2 in TASMC (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 37/41; 90%). A new TEM ESBL variant, TEM-177 was identified in FSL. The clonal diversity and the predominance of a single ESBL type suggested that horizontal gene transfer played an important role in dissemination of resistance. The development of a population analysis tool that would allow tracing deeper genetic relationships is required.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic> is one of the most common agents of urinary tract infection, in particular among patients with urological abnormalities [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>], In addition, it is a frequent cause of other types of hospital-acquired infections, including bacteraemia [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>], meningitis, empyema and osteomyelitis [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>]. Along with sporadic infection cases, nosocomial outbreaks due to <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> have been reported since the 1970s [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib4 bib5 bib6">4–6</ce:cross-refs>]. Gastrointestinal colonization is believed to serve as a reservoir for intra-hospital spread [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>]. As with other <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic>, resistance to cephalosporins, mediated mostly by extended-spectrum <ce:italic>β-</ce:italic>lactamases (ESBLs) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib5 bib7 bib8">5,7,8</ce:cross-refs>] or acquired AmpC-type cephalosporinases [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib9 bib10">9,10</ce:cross-refs>], has been spreading in <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> populations in many parts of the world. The molecular epidemiology of ESBL-producing <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> (ESBL-PM) varies widely between different countries. In Italy and France TEM-type enzymes, especially TEM-92, have been the most common [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib2 bib7 bib8">2,7,8</ce:cross-refs>], whereas in Poland, Israel and Japan the CTX-M types have been usually observed [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib5 bib11 bib12 bib13">5,11–13</ce:cross-refs>]. In some countries, such as Greece, Italy and Poland, AmpC-producing <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> strains have disseminated along with those expressing ESBLs [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Only a single study designed to look for the specific risk factors for ESBL-PM infections has been published so far [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>]. In this study, the clinical and demographic characteristics of patients with ESBL-PM bacteraemia were compared with those of the patients with antibiotic-susceptible <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> infections in acute-care settings. Previous hospitalization in a nursing home and use of a bladder catheter were identified as risk factors. The study reported here was conducted in two rehabilitation centres in Italy and Israel, with distinct types of patient populations (see below). Rehabilitation wards (RWs) differ significantly from acute-care units, as patients are commonly hospitalized for longer periods, are more ambulant and may share joint facilities, such as physiotherapy suites or pools. Thus, the potential for direct patient-to-patient transmission of resistant bacteria may be more significant. Because patients in our study were monitored throughout their hospitalization by surveillance cultures, we were able to track the acquisition of ESBL-PM prior to evolution of clinical infection. Our aims were: (i) to comparatively assess the risk factors for ESBL-PM carriage in each of the two institutions; (ii) to reveal the clonal structure and ESBL genes of ESBL-PM populations; and (iii) to analyse the acquisition-to-admission ratio for the identified ESBL-PM clones.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Hospital settings</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para30" view="all">This study was a part of the project MOSAR (Mastering Hospital Antimicrobial Resistance and its Spread into the Community), a trans-disciplinary network funded by the European Commission and devoted to combating and controlling resistance in bacteria. The project focused on endemic and epidemic nosocomial pathogens in high-risk medical units, including ICUs, RWs and surgery wards in different European countries and Israel. Here we present a part of the results from the Work Package 5 (WP5), based on clinical trials in RWs, and Work Package 2 (WP2), comprising the laboratory work on bacterial isolates collected during the trials.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para40" view="all">The study was conducted in two hospitals. The first centre, Fondazione Santa Lucia IRCCS in Rome, Italy (FSL), consists of two wards (106 beds together), and admits patients following spinal, cranial or orthopaedic trauma and with non-traumatic neurological and orthopaedic disorders. The second centre comprises two geriatric RWs (50 beds combined) at the Tel-Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TASMC) in Tel Aviv, Israel.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para50" view="all">These wards admit elderly patients (&gt;65 year of age) for rehabilitation following acute care admissions due to orthopaedic or neurological disorders or because of general deterioration in condition. The study included patients admitted to the wards between September 2008 and November 2010 and was approved by the ethics committees of FSL and TASMC.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Design and data collection</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">This was a prospective case–control study, aimed at examining risk factors for ESBL-PM carriage among RW patients. Surveillance rectal cultures were collected from all patients at admission, 2 weeks later, then once monthly, and at discharge. According to the local infection control policy, contact isolation was not carried out for ESBL-PM carriers. The following data were recorded: patient's age and sex, admission diagnosis, medical history including underlying conditions and co-morbidities, prior hospital or long-term care facility (LTCF) stay and its duration, antibiotic treatment during the last month prior to admission, the presence of medical devices, history of surgery or other invasive procedures, and the discharge destination. ESBL-PM carriers and control patients were randomly selected from the MOSAR database. Molecular typing and identification of ESBL genes were performed on first patient-unique isolates as described below.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Detection of ESBL-PM isolates and their phenotypic characterization</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">Rectal swabs were streaked onto the Brilliance ESBL Agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK). Putative ESBL-producing <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> colonies (one from each morphotype detected) were identified according to the manufacturer's instructions. Pure cultures were frozen at −80°C and shipped to the MOSAR ESBL laboratory (National Medicines Institute in Warsaw, Poland) for definite identification and further analysis. Species identification was carried out using the Vitek 2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). ESBL production was verified using the double-disk synergy test with disks containing cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime and amoxicillin with clavulanate on Mueller-Hinton agar plates (Oxoid) that were unsupplemented and supplemented with 250 mg/L cloxacillin (Polfa Tarchomin, Warsaw, Poland) as previously described [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib14">14</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Molecular typing of ESBL-PM isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">For pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), total DNAs of the isolates were purified as described by Struelens <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>] and digested sequentially with <ce:italic>Not</ce:italic>l and <ce:italic>Sfi</ce:italic>l restriction enzymes (New England BioLabs, Beverly, MA, USA) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>]. PFGE types and subtypes were discerned by the visual analysis using the criteria of Tenover <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib17">17</ce:cross-ref>]. In order to verify the results, electrophoretic patterns were compared also with the BioNumerics Fingerprinting software (Version 6.01; Applied Maths, Sint-Martens-Latem, Belgium), using the Dice coefficient and clustering by UPGMA (unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean) with 1% tolerance in band position differences.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>

                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">ESBL-PM isolates were subjected to <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactamase profiling by isoelectric focusing as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>] by using a Model 111 Mini IEF Cell (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Identification of the ESBL <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-1</ce:inf>-, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:small-caps>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                           <ce:sup loc="post">-</ce:sup>,</ce:small-caps> 
                        <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:small-caps>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-25</ce:inf>-,</ce:small-caps> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM</ce:inf>-like genes and the AmpC <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CMY-2</ce:inf>-like genes was done by PCR as previously described [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. Sequencing of the genes was performed for representative isolates as reported [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib13 bib19">13,19</ce:cross-refs>], using sets of consecutive primers specific for each gene type. In the case of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM</ce:inf>-like genes two types of PCR products were obtained with standard primers TEM-A and TEM-B, amplifying the entire gene together with its 5′-adjacent region [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>]. Some isolates produced shorter amplicons of <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>. 950 bp alone or together with those of the expected size of <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>. 1100 bp. If both were observed for a single isolate, the two PCR products were extracted from agarose gels and sequenced separately.</ce:para>

                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Plasmid profiling was carried out by PFGE of isolates’ total DNA cut with nuclease S1 (New England Biolabs) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>]. After electrophoresis, DNA was blotted onto Hybond-N+ (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Little Chalfont, UK) and hybridized with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf> or <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:small-caps>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                        </ce:small-caps> PCR probes, using the ECL Random-Prime Labeling and Detection system (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech). Mating was done with <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>coli</ce:italic> A15 Rif* [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>]; transconjugants were selected with 128 mg/L rifampin and 2 mg/L ceftazidime or cefotaxime (Polfa). Plasmid DNA was purified with the QIAGEN Plasmid Midi Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and characterized by Pstl (New England Biolabs) fingerprinting [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>] and by PCR-based replicon typing (PBRT) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">Definitions and data analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">Risk factors were analysed by comparing the ESBL-PM carriers with the ESBL-producing <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> (ESBL-Ent)-negative group. The analysis was separated for each institution due to the big differences in the patient populations. ESBL-PM carriers were divided into ‘admission’ and ‘acquisition’ groups, according to the ESBL-PM identification time: before and after 72 h from admission, respectively. In cases when the first rectal culture was collected more than 72 h from admission, the acquisition status was not determined. Data were analysed using the univariate analysis: continuous variables were compared between the groups using an unpaired <ce:italic>t</ce:italic>-test and categorical parameters were compared using the chi-square test. p-values of ≤ 0.05 were considered as a significant difference between the groups. The multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression prediction models was constructed using forward stepwise. All variables that were identified in the univariate analysis with a p &lt;0.1 were included in the model, excluding the length of stay and discharge destination, being regarded as outcome measures. All data were analysed using the SPSS software package version 15.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Demographic and clinical characteristics of ESBL-PM carriers at FSL and TASMC</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">During the study time (from September 2008 to November 2010), 1017 and 1656 patients were admitted to the FSL and TASMC RWs, respectively. ESBL-PM carriage was identified in 93 and 113 of these patients, respectively. Of these, 70 and 41 ESBL-PM carriers (111 in total) were selected from FSL and TASMC, respectively. An additional ESBL-Ent was identified in the surveillance cultures of 61 of the 111 patients, most commonly <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>coli</ce:italic> (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 48). As a control, 70 and 40 ESBL-Entnegative patients (110 in total) were identified in FSL and TASMC, respectively. Complete clinical data were available only for 61/71 patients in the FSL ESBL-PM carrier group. Almost all the patients were admitted following a stay in an acute-care institution (FSL, 93%; TASMC, 95%). The clinical and demographic characteristics of the patient groups in the two centres are presented in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>. Overall, the FSL patients were younger than the TASMC patients [55 (95% CI 52–59) vs. 81 (95% CI 79–83) years, respectively] and had lower rates of underlying chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular, lung and renal disease and diabetes, and history of malignancy.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">In FSL, the ESBL-PM carriers were more likely to be admitted following severe acute brain injury (SABI), to have acute infection upon admission and decubitus ulcer, and to have a history of urinary catheter or other invasive devices used in the preceding month, when compared with the control group. Also they had a higher rate of recent treatment (within 1 month) with quinolones and aminoglycosides. No significant differences were found in the rates of the recent usage (1 month) of other antimicrobial agents, including penicillins, <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactam/<ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-lactams inhibitor combinations, cephalosporins, carbapenems, macrolides, glycopeptides, tetracyclines and metronidazole (data not shown). The FSL ESBL-PM carriers had a longer mean length of stay (LOS) and were less likely to be discharged home at the end of their stay. In the multivariate analysis, admission due to SABI (OR = 15, 95% CI = 3.2–69.5, p 0.001), decubitus ulcer (OR = 3.5, 95% CI = 1.2–9.8, p 0.018) and recent treatment with quinolones (OR = 5.7,95% CI = 1.07–30.1, p 0.042) were identified as significant risk factors.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">In TASMC, the ESBL-PM carriers had a higher mean age, longer LOS and were more likely to be admitted with acute infection, but these differences were not statistically significant. Four ESBL-PM carriers but none in the control group were admitted following spinal cord diseases and injuries (SDI; p 0.043).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">Molecular characteristics and acquisition of ESBL-PM in FSL and TASMC</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">The PFGE types, ESBLs and acquisition status of all isolates are presented in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/> and Fig. S1. There were no similarities in the PFGE types or ESBLs between the ESBL-PM populations from the two institutions. In both centres, a variety of PFGE types existed (FSL, 21 types; TASMC, 10 types), with a notable representation of several types. In FSL, 10 types comprised more than one isolate each, and five of these, having ≥ 5 isolates each, grouped 47/70 (67%) of the overall FSL isolates. Some types, like FSPmiH (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 16) or FSPmiB (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 10), were diversified into multiple subtypes, whereas others, like FSPmiG (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 10), were homogeneous. In TASMC, five types grouped more than one isolate and three of these, TAPmiB (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 15), TAPmiA (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 9) and TAPmiE (<ce:italic>n =</ce:italic> 8), represented 32/41 (78%) of the isolates, all being highly diverse.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">All but one of the ESBL-PM isolates in FSL produced ESBLs of the family TEM, mainly TEM-92 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>], found in 64/70 isolates (91%) of 18/21 PFGE types. The minor variants were TEM-72 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>] and TEM-177, identified in three and two isolates of specific PFGE types, respectively. TEM-177 was a new ESBL variant and when compared with TEM-1 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>] it had seven amino-acid substitutions: Q6K, Q39K, E104K, R164S, M182T, A237T and E240K. The <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> gene had an identical promoter to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>], including a 135-bp deletion and a G162→T mutation when compared with the <ce:italic>P3</ce:italic> promoter of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-1A</ce:inf> gene [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib25 bib26">25,26</ce:cross-refs>]. Apart from those causing amino-acid changes, the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> coding region contained three silent mutations compared with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-1A</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>], namely A34→ G, C436→T and T682→C. Of note, AmpC-producing <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> isolates were recovered from 30 patients in FSL, but not in TASMC. Carbapenemases were not identified in any of the ESBL-PM isolates. A single ESBL-PM isolate from FSL coproduced the CTX-M-15 ESBL [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>] and the CMY-16 AmpClike cephalosporinase [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para180" view="all">In opposition to FSL, all ESBL-PM from TASMC expressed only CTX-M-type ESBLs, mainly CTX-M-2 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>], identified in 37/41 isolates (90%) of 8/10 PFGE types. The only exceptions were four isolates having four different variants of the CTX-M-25 group, namely CTX-M-25 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>], −39 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>], −94 and −100 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>], The CTX-M-25 and −94 were produced by the PFGE type TAPmiA, which included also CTX-M-2-producing isolates.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">The plasmid analysis was performed on six TEM-92-producing isolates from FSL (PFGE types FSPmiB, FSPmiC, FSPmiD, FSPmiG, FSPmiH and FSPmiT), and four CTX-M-2-producing isolates from TASMC (types TAPmiA, TAPmiB, TAPmiC and TAPmiE), each representing a major PFGE type. In each of the isolates from FSL, the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">TEM</ce:inf> hybridization revealed the presence of the gene on a c. 50 kb plasmid. <ce:italic>Pstl</ce:italic> fingerprints of these plasmids were identical (results not shown); they were non-typeable by PBRT and did not self-transfer to <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>coli</ce:italic> cells. In TASMC, the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:small-caps>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                        </ce:small-caps>-harbouring plasmids were more diverse. Isolates of two PFGE types (TAPmiA and TAPmiC) had two copies of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:small-caps>
                           <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                        </ce:small-caps> gene, one chromosomal and one on a plasmid of c. 200 kb. The plasmids had the same Pstl fingerprint (results not shown), were of the IncA/C type and were successfully transferred to <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>coli</ce:italic>. The TAPmiE isolate had <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">CTX-M-2</ce:inf> on a c. 50 kb plasmid (results not shown), which did not self-conjugate and was probably of the IncFIA type, as suggested by PBRT performed on DNA from the clinical isolate. Finally, the TAPmiB isolate had only one chromosomal copy of the gene (results not shown).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para200" view="all">In both centres, the proportions of admission and acquisition cases were similar (24/27 and 17/19 in FSL and TASMC, respectively). A single strain, the FSPmiB PFGE type producing TEM-92, had a significantly high number of acquisition to admission cases, 8 vs. 1 (p &lt;0.05). Comparing the FSPmiBcarrying patients with the other ESBL-PM carriers in FSL, other risk factors associated with this strain were not identified (data not shown).</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para210" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> has been reported as one of the most frequent ESBL-Ent species in Italy [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib2 bib32 bib33">2,32,33</ce:cross-refs>]. Despite that, there are no data about the distinct risk factors for ESBL-PM colonization in Italy or elsewhere. In Israel, as in most of the world, the data regarding the epidemiology and molecular epidemiology of ESBL-PM are even more limited [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]. Hence, our study provides a new outlook on this important pathogen.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">The analysis was performed in unique settings of two rehabilitation centres in Italy and Israel that differed significantly in the types of medical services and their patient populations. These differences were probably responsible for the differences in risk factors for the ESBL-PM carriage identified in each centre. In Italy, ESBL-PM carriers differed in many aspects from the control group of patients. Several risk factors that were identified, including longer LOS and recent treatment with quinolones, were previously described as risk factors for other ESBL-Ent infections [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>]. The use of an invasive device was identified as a risk factor in the univariate analysis, as reported by Endimiani <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>], but previous LTCF admission was not. We also did not identify recent treatment with cephalosporins and anti-pseudomonas penicillin as risk factors, as reported in a retrospective study of nosocomial multidrug-resistant (MDR) <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> urinary tract infection [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. We identified admission following SABI as an independent risk factor. As this feature is unique to that particular institution (FSL, Rome), it has not been reported or sought in other studies. Although local spread might have been a plausible explanation for this finding, it was not supported by the polyclonal nature of the ESBL-PM isolates in this group of patients (data not shown). A unique finding in our study was the identification of decubitus ulcer as another independent risk factor. As <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> is commonly isolated from chronic wounds [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>], this finding may reflect a complex interaction between the intestinal and the wound flora in these patients. In the Israeli centre, TASMC, several risk factors were identified but, in contrast to FSL, these were statistically non-significant (0.05 &lt; p &lt;0.1). Although it is possible that a larger sampling number would have allowed us to identify these factors with better certainty, this observation also reflects the relative homogeneity of the patient populations in these wards.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">Several molecular characteristics of the ESBL-PM isolates, especially regarding the ESBL types, were consistent with the previous data from each of the countries. In Italy, TEM-92 and TEM-72 were originally reported in 2000 [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib8 bib24">8,24</ce:cross-refs>]. In the following years, TEM-92 was reported as the most common ESBL in <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> in both Italy and France [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib2 bib7 bib33 bib36">2,7,33,36</ce:cross-refs>]. Nevertheless, the predominance of the TEM-like ESBLs, mainly TEM-92, among the ESBL-PM isolates from FSL, is intriguing, considering the prevalence of CTX-Ms in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> populations in Europe, including Italy [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs110" refid="bib37 bib38 bib39">37–39</ce:cross-refs>]. Indeed, the predominance of the CTX-M enzymes (mainly CTX-M-15 and −1) was observed among <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> strains isolated in the same institution within the MOSAR project [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">Similarly, a single enzyme (CTX-M-2) was present in 90% of the isolates from Israel. Previously, CTX-M-2 was the most common enzyme found in ESBL-PM isolates in Japan [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>]. The molecular data regarding the ESBL-PM in Israel have been limited to two strains so far, one that produced CTX-M-25 and the other a novel enzyme, CTX-M-41 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib12">12</ce:cross-ref>]. CTX-M-2 was identified also in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Enterobacter</ce:italic> spp. isolates in TASMC in the early 2000s [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs120" refid="bib30 bib41">30,41</ce:cross-refs>], but in only a few <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> isolates that were recently characterized during the MOSAR project (CTXM-15 and −27 being the most prevalent) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>]. It is striking that the <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> populations concurrently present in both FSL and TASMC were so different in terms of their ESBL types and that in both places <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> had a tendency to maintain the ‘earlier’ enzyme types.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">The origin and evolution of the new gene encoding TEM-177 is rather unclear. The 5′-end of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> gene, including its promoter and the coding region just beyond the codon specifying the Q6K substitution, were identical to <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf>. However, the further region, including the Q39K substitution and three silent mutations, suggested that <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> was rather of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-2</ce:inf> lineage and not of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-1</ce:inf>, as it is in the case of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:small-caps>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">tem-92</ce:inf>
                     </ce:small-caps>. Moreover, none of the genes coding for the TEM ESBLs known so far (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs10" xlink:href="http://www.lahey.org/Studies/" xlink:type="simple">http://www.lahey.org/Studies/</ce:inter-ref>) could be proposed as a direct <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> precursor by acquisition of 1–2 mutations. Hence, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-177</ce:inf> might have arisen by a combination of recombinatorial (e.g. between <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf> and a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-2</ce:inf>-related gene) and mutational events as has been already proposed in the evolution of other <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM</ce:inf> genes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs130" refid="bib42 bib43">42,43</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">In contrast to the general uniformity of ESBL types in each of the centres, the PFGE analysis showed a heterogeneous clonal structure of their <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> populations. Similar findings were observed in France and Japan [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs140" refid="bib7 bib11">7,11</ce:cross-refs>], even in the course of a single outbreak [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. Only in one case, the TEM-92-producing FSPmiB type, was it possible to assign its higher prevalence to frequent cases of in-centre acquisition. This contrast between the ESBL uniformity and clonal heterogeneity has two possible explanations. First, it is possible that most of the isolates at each centre descended from a common ancestral strain but the PFGE approach was too discriminatory to allow tracing such deeper relatedness. Second, it is possible that the populations were heterogeneous indeed, and thus the ESBL uniformity arose from the horizontal transfer of the plasmids [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs150" refid="bib7 bib44 bib45">7,44,45</ce:cross-refs>]. Indeed, plasmid analysis showed that the main <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> FSL clones had a common <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf>-harbouring plasmid. The fact that the conjugation assay with <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> as recipient failed, may be explained by a low transfer efficiency, below the sensitivity of the assay, or by a more species-specific (<ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic>) plasmid. This might be supported also by its non-typeability by PBRT and the lack of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">TEM-92</ce:inf> in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strains collected from FSL patients during the same period [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>]. On the other hand, the analysis of the TASMC isolates had shown that the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:small-caps>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                     </ce:small-caps> gene was harboured by different plasmids, as well as in the chromosome. It is possible that the latter situations arose from transposition of a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:small-caps>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">ctx-m-2</ce:inf>
                     </ce:small-caps>-harbouring mobile element from one plasmid into other DNA replicons. Studying the clonality and evolution of <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> would be significantly facilitated upon the application of a population analysis tool that would allow tracing deeper genetic relationships among the <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> strains, such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST). Such a typing method, not currently available, would also allow comparing strains from different countries and identification of possible globally successful clones, such as the sequence type 131 ESBL-producing <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para270" view="all">In conclusion, the risk factors for ESBL-PM colonization may vary widely between different healthcare institutions. An MLST scheme for deeper <ce:italic>P</ce:italic>. <ce:italic>mirabilis</ce:italic> population studies is currently required for better understanding the clonal structure and the transmission potential of ESBL-PM clones.</ce:para>
