Abstract 

SGM-1 is a novel class A β-lactamase from an environmental isolate of Sphingobium sp. containing all of the distinct amino acid motifs of class A β-lactamases. It shares 77 to 80% amino acid sequence identity with putative β-lactamases that are present on the chromosome of all Sphingobium species whose genomes were sequenced and annotated. Thus, SGM-type β-lactamases are native to this genus. Antibiotic susceptibility testing classifies SGM-1 as an extended-spectrum β-lactamase, conferring the highest level of resistance to penicillins. Although SGM-1 contains the conserved cysteine residues characteristic of class A carbapenemases, it does not confer resistance to the carbapenem antibiotics imipenem, meropenem, or doripenem but does increase the MIC of ertapenem 8-fold. SGM-1 hydrolyzes penicillins and the monobactam aztreonam with similar catalytic efficiencies, ranging from 10 5 to 10 6 M −1 s −1 . The catalytic efficiencies of SGM-1 for cefoxitin and ceftazidime were the lowest (10 2 to 10 3 M −1 s −1 ) among the cephalosporins tested, while the catalytic efficiencies against all other cephalosporins varied from about 10 5 to 10 6 M −1 s −1 . SGM-1 exhibited measurable but not significant activity toward the carbapenems tested. SGM-1 also showed high affinity for clavulanic acid, tazobactam, and sulbactam ( K i < 1 μM); however, only clavulanic acid significantly reduced the MICs of β-lactams. 

INTRODUCTION 

β-Lactamases are the major mechanism of resistance to β-lactams in Gram-negative bacteria, and more than a thousand individual enzymes ( 1 ), belonging to four major molecular classes—A, B, C, and D—are currently known. Class A β-lactamases are represented by several hundreds of individual enzymes isolated from both clinical pathogens and environmental bacterial species. The first well-characterized class A β-lactamases, such as TEM-1 and SHV-1, were narrow-spectrum enzymes that conferred resistance to penicillins and early narrow-spectrum cephalosporins ( 1 ). To circumvent β-lactamase-mediated resistance by these enzymes, expanded-spectrum cephalosporins and β-lactamase inhibitors have been introduced into the clinic. This subsequently triggered the rapid evolution of TEM- and SHV-type β-lactamases toward broadening their substrate profiles to include these novel compounds ( 2 ). Despite their remarkable evolutionary plasticity, TEM- and SHV-type enzymes have failed to evolve clinical resistance to another group of β-lactam antibiotics introduced into the clinic, the carbapenems ( 3 ). Instead, novel types of class A enzymes, capable of hydrolyzing carbapenem antibiotics, were selected from yet unidentified bacterial sources. They include SFC-, NMC-A/IMI-, FPH-, BIC-, KPC-, GES-, and SME-type enzymes, with KPC- and some GES-type enzymes being the most clinically important ( 4 – 6 ). Class A carbapenemases share relatively low amino acid sequence identity with “classical” TEM-, SHV-, or CTX-type β-lactamases, and all harbor a characteristic interdomain disulfide bridge formed by two conserved cysteine residues. Although the importance of this bridge for the carbapenemase activity of the enzymes has yet to be established, it is evident that its presence does not necessarily result in such activity, as FTU-1, BEL-type, and the majority of GES-type β-lactamases do not confer resistance to carbapenem antibiotics, despite the presence of the conserved cysteine residues ( 7 – 10 ). Here, we report a novel extended-spectrum class A β-lactamase, SGM-1, that is native within the genus Sphingobium . 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Cloning of the gene for SGM-1. 

The gene for the SGM-1 β-lactamase from Sphingomonas sp. strain SKA58 (GenBank accession number ZP_01304116 ) was optimized for expression in Escherichia coli and custom synthesized (GenScript) with unique NdeI and HindIII restriction sites flanking the gene at the 5′ and 3′ termini, respectively. In addition, the predicted leader sequence for SGM-1 was substituted with that of the TEM-1 β-lactamase to facilitate transport of the enzyme to the periplasm. The gene was then cloned between the NdeI and HindIII sites of the vectors pHF016 (for susceptibility testing) and pET24a(+) (Novagen, for periplasmic enzyme expression) to give the plasmids pHF016:SGM-1 and pET24a(+):SGM-1P, respectively ( 10 ). For protein expression and the subsequent isolation of mature SGM-1 from the bacterial cytoplasm, the leader sequence was removed by PCR using the primers SGM1P1 (5′-ATAATCATATGGCTGATCGCCAAAAAACACCG-3′) and oHF103 (5′-AGTGCGGCCGCAAGCTTA-3′). The amplified gene was then cloned between the NdeI and HindIII sites of the vector pET24a(+) to give the plasmid pET24a(+):SGM-1. 

Antibiotic susceptibility testing. 

The susceptibility profile of E. coli JM83 harboring SGM-1 for β-lactam antibiotics alone or in the presence of β-lactamase inhibitors was determined using the broth microdilution method as defined by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute ( 11 ). E. coli JM83 transformed with either pHF016:SGM-1 or pHF016, as a control, was used at a final bacterial inoculum of 5 × 10 5 CFU/ml in Mueller-Hinton II broth (Difco). The experiment was performed in triplicate, and the results were interpreted after incubation at 37°C for 16 to 20 h. 

Protein expression and purification. 

E. coli BL21(DE3) harboring pET24a(+):SGM-1P were grown at 37°C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 60 μg of kanamycin/ml. The cells were grown to an optical density of 0.6 at 600 nm, and protein expression was induced using 0.4 mM IPTG (isopropyl-β- d -thiogalactopyranoside). The culture was further incubated at 22°C for an additional 18 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g and 4°C for 15 min, and the periplasmic fraction was released as previously described ( 12 ). The periplasmic fraction was dialyzed against 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0) at 4°C and loaded onto a DEAE anion-exchange column equilibrated in 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0). The column was washed with 2.5 column volumes of 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0), followed by elution of SGM-1 from the column with 50 mM NaCl in 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0). Fractions containing SGM-1 were pooled and were ≥95% pure as judged by SDS-PAGE. The enzyme was stored at 4°C in 25 mM Tris (pH 8.0) at a concentration of 105 μM. The enzyme concentration was determined using the predicted extinction coefficient (Δε 280 = 17,780 M −1 cm −1 [ http://www.justbio.com/index.php?page=protcalc ]). Isolation of SGM-1 from the cytoplasm was performed according to the same procedure, except the vector pET24a(+):SGM-1 was used for induction, and the cells we lysed by sonication prior to enzyme purification. 

Enzyme kinetics. 

The hydrolysis of β-lactam substrates were evaluated spectrophotometrically using a Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Varian). The activity of the SGM-1 β-lactamase was measured at room temperature, and the final reaction mixture contained 50 mM NaP i (pH 7.0) and 0.2 mg of bovine serum albumin (BSA)/ml. The following extinction coefficients and wavelengths were used: ampicillin (Δε 235 = −670 M −1 cm −1 ), oxacillin (Δε 260 = +440 M −1 cm −1 ), ticarcillin (Δε 235 = −660 M −1 cm −1 ), piperacillin (Δε 235 = −793 M −1 cm −1 ), cephalothin (Δε 262 = −8,610 M −1 cm −1 ), cefuroxime (Δε 262 = −7,800 M −1 cm −1 ), ceftazidime (Δε 260 = −10,500 M −1 cm −1 ), cefotaxime (Δε 265 = −6,643 M −1 cm −1 ), ceftriaxone (Δε 265 = −6,668 M −1 cm −1 ), cefepime (Δε 264 = −10,050 M −1 cm −1 ), cefoxitin (Δε 265 = −7,507 M −1 cm −1 ), aztreonam (Δε 318 = −623 M −1 cm −1 ), imipenem (Δε 297 = −10,930 M −1 cm −1 ), meropenem (Δε 298 = −7,200 M −1 cm −1 ), ertapenem (Δε 295 = −10,940 M −1 cm −1 ), doripenem (Δε 299 = −11,540 M −1 cm −1 ), nitrocefin (Δε 500 = +15,900 M −1 cm −1 ), and sulbactam (Δε 236 = +1,784 M −1 cm −1 ). The steady-state velocities were determined at various concentrations of substrate using the linear phase of the reaction time course with the exception of the carbapenems meropenem, ertapenem, and doripenem, which displayed biphasic time courses. For the latter three antibiotics, the time courses were fit nonlinearly with equation 1 ( 13 ) using Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, Inc.): 
A t = A 0 + v s s t + v i − v s s k obs ( 1 − e − k obs t ) (1) 
where the initial absorbance is denoted by A 0 , A t denotes the absorbance at time t , the initial and steady-state velocities are denoted by v i and v ss , respectively, and k obs denotes the observed rate constant for interconversion between the two phases. To determine the steady-state kinetic parameters k cat and K m , the data were fit nonlinearly with the Michaelis-Menten equation ( equation 2 ) using Prism 5: 
v = V max S K m + S (2) 
where the velocity is denoted by v , S denotes the substrate concentration, and V max = k cat E , where E is the enzyme concentration. Under circumstances in which saturation could not be reached, the reaction time course for complete hydrolysis of three concentrations of substrate (where S ≪ K m ) were fit with equation 3 to determine the ratio k cat / K m : 
A t = A ∞ + ( A 0 − A ∞ ) e − k t (3) 
where A t and A 0 are as defined above, the final absorbance is denoted by A ∞ , and k = ( k cat / K m ) E , where E is the enzyme concentration. 

The inhibitor dissociation constants ( K i ) for the β-lactamase inhibitors clavulanic acid, sulbactam, and tazobactam were determined with nitrocefin as a reporter substrate using the Dixon method ( 14 ). The reactions were performed at room temperature, the final reaction mixture contained 50 mM NaP i (pH 7.0), 0.2 mg of BSA/ml, fixed concentrations of nitrocefin (50 and 75 μM with clavulanic acid, 50 and 100 μM with sulbactam, and 30 and 50 μM with tazobactam), and various concentrations of inhibitor, and the reactions were initiated by the addition of SGM-1 (0.1 to 0.3 nM, final). 

The partition ratio was ascertained for SGM-1 with three β-lactamase inhibitors using the titration method ( 15 ). SGM-1 (100 or 500 nM) in 50 mM NaP i (pH 7.0) and 0.2 mg of BSA/ml was incubated overnight at 4°C with the β-lactamase inhibitor at various molar ratios (clavulanic acid up to 200:1, sulbactam up to 20,000:1, and tazobactam up to 5,700:1). The residual activity of SGM-1 was determined at room temperature after at least a 100-fold dilution of the overnight reaction into the following mixture: 50 mM NaP i (pH 7.0), 0.2 mg of BSA/ml, and 500 μM nitrocefin (as a reporter substrate). The partition ratio, as previously defined ( 16 , 17 ), was evaluated as the ratio of inhibitor to enzyme that resulted in ≥90% inactivation of SGM-1. 

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Amino acid sequence analysis of SGM-1. 

In our search for novel class A carbapenemases, we analyzed the amino acid sequence of a putative chromosomal β-lactamase from Sphingomonas sp. strain SKA58 (GenBank accession number ZP_01304116 ), isolated from seawater in the north Atlantic Ocean and whose genome had been sequenced. This strain was recently reclassified as belonging to the genus Sphingobium ( 18 ). We refer to this putative enzyme as SGM-1 ( S phin G obiu M ). The genus Sphingobium belongs to the Sphingomonadaceae family, bacteria that are found widely distributed in nature, especially in aquatic environments. 

SGM-1 contains all of the conserved amino acids and amino acid motifs characteristic of class A β-lactamases, including S 70 XXK 73 (containing the catalytic serine), S 130 DN, K 234 TG, E 166 (a residue critical for deacylation), and R 220 (a residue that anchors the C3 or C4 carboxylate of β-lactam antibiotics) ( Fig. 1 ). SGM-1 shares 24 to 41% amino acid sequence identity ( Table 1 ) with other characterized class A β-lactamases that also harbor the conserved cysteine residues (underlined in Fig. 1 ) characteristic of class A carbapenemases. We further analyzed whether SGM-like enzymes could be found in other genera of the family Sphingomonadaceae whose genomes had been sequenced. Using the amino acid sequence of SGM-1 as a template, we conducted a search for related enzymes in the database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information using their basic local alignment search tool (BLAST). Very closely related putative β-lactamases (77 to 80% amino acid sequence identity) were identified only within the chromosomes of all microorganisms belonging to the genus Sphingobium (we refer to these enzymes as SGM-2 to SGM-7). This result indicates that SGM-type β-lactamases are native in Sphingobium . Analysis of the genetic environment for the SGM-type enzymes revealed no known insertion sequences or transposons, with the exception of SGM-1, which has two putative transposases annotated downstream of the gene. Since it is not yet established whether these hypothetical proteins are in fact active transposases, we cannot speculate on their possible role in the dissemination of the gene for SGM-1.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing. 

To further characterize SGM-1, we determined the MICs of various β-lactam antibiotics against E. coli JM83 expressing the enzyme. SGM-1 conferred high levels of resistance to all penicillins tested ( Table 2 ). In the presence of 2 μg of clavulanic acid/ml, the MIC of ampicillin against E. coli JM83 producing SGM-1 decreased 64-fold and the MIC of amoxicillin dropped from >2,048 to 16 μg/ml when the ratio of amoxicillin and clavulanic acid was fixed at 2:1 ( Table 2 ). At the same time, E. coli JM83 producing SGM-1 remained resistant to penicillins in the presence of tazobactam and, even more so, in the presence of sulbactam; the MICs of ampicillin in the presence of 4 μg of tazobactam/ml and sulbactam were reduced merely 8- and 2-fold, respectively ( Table 2 ). SGM-1 significantly elevated the MICs of all cephalosporins tested, with exception of the 7-α-methoxy-substituted cephalosporins, cefoxitin and cefmetazole, and the 7-α-methoxy-substituted oxacephem, moxalactam. The enzyme also increases the MIC of the monobactam aztreonam 512-fold. Of the four carbapenem antibiotics tested, production of SGM-1 resulted in an 8-fold increase in the MIC of ertapenem, while the MICs of imipenem, meropenem, and doripenem were within 2-fold of the level produced by a strain lacking the β-lactamase gene. Cumulatively, the result of antibiotic susceptibility testing indicates that SGM-1 is an extended-spectrum β-lactamase.

We compared the antibiotic susceptibility profile of SGM-1 to both KPC-type, possessing carbapenemase activity, and BEL-type enzymes, which are not considered carbapenemases, as they share the closest amino acid sequence identity with SGM-1 and contain the conserved cysteine residues characteristic of class A carbapenemases ( Table 1 ) ( 19 – 22 ). The high-level resistance to penicillins observed in the presence of SGM-1 was also seen in the presence of both BEL- and KPC-type β-lactamases. The MICs for cephalosporins were similar for strains harboring SGM-1 or BEL-type enzymes, whereas the MICs for this class of antibiotics were 8- to 64-fold higher with strains producing KPC-type carbapenemases. The exceptions were the 7-α-methoxy-substituted cephalosporins (cefoxitin and cefmetazole), for which all three types of β-lactamases conferred lower MICs. High-level resistance to aztreonam was observed in the presence of SGM-1, BEL-, and KPC-type enzymes. Among the three clinically used β-lactamase inhibitors, clavulanic acid was the most potent for all three enzymes, as judged by MIC testing. Overall, SGM-1 has an antibiotic susceptibility profile more closely matching BEL-type β-lactamases, enzymes possessing no carbapenemase activity. 

Kinetic studies of SGM-1. 

β-Lactamases are periplasmic enzymes, thus we attempted to purify SGM-1 from the periplasm of E. coli . This protocol did not yield significant quantities of purified enzyme, so we isolated the mature protein from the cytoplasm in much higher yield. We then compared the catalytic activity of both enzymes against ampicillin and did not observe any differences (data not shown). We conducted steady-state kinetic studies of SGM-1 isolated from the cytoplasm with various β-lactam substrates ( Table 3 ). Despite low relative affinity ( K m = 43 to 330 μM), the enzyme exhibited high catalytic efficiency against all four penicillin antibiotics tested (the k cat / K m values for ampicillin, oxacillin, ticarcillin, and piperacillin were 1.6 × 10 6 , 8 × 10 5 , 5.2 × 10 5 , and 2.6 × 10 6 M −1 s −1 , respectively) due to high turnover ( k cat = 33 to 350 s −1 ). SGM-1 turned over the extended-spectrum cephalosporins cefuroxime, cefotaxime, and ceftriaxone with catalytic efficiencies of 1.2 × 10 5 to 5.3 × 10 5 M −1 s −1 and cefepime with a catalytic efficiency of 7.6 × 10 4 M −1 s −1 . The catalytic efficiency of the enzyme against ceftazidime was low ( k cat / K m = 10 3 M −1 s −1 ) despite the ability of SGM-1 to confer a 256-fold increase in the MIC for this antibiotic to 64 μg/ml ( Table 2 ). This is likely the result of the poor penetration rate of ceftazidime, similar to what was seen with the FPH-1 β-lactamase ( 5 , 23 ). In agreement with the MIC value, the catalytic efficiency of SGM-1 against aztreonam was similar to that of other good substrates, such as penicillins.

We compared the kinetic parameters for hydrolysis of β-lactams by SGM-1 to both KPC- and BEL-type class A β-lactamases, as we did for the antibiotic susceptibility profile (see above). High catalytic efficiency is observed for SGM-1 and BEL-type enzymes toward penicillin antibiotics ( k cat / K m = 10 5 to 10 6 M −1 s −1 ). Although turnover is higher for SGM-1 ( k cat = 33 to 350 s −1 ) than BEL-type enzymes ( k cat = 1 to 10 s −1 ), the latter compensate for low turnover by a much higher relative affinity for penicillins ( K m = 0.3 to 20 μM) compared to SGM-1 ( K m = 43 to 330 μM) ( 7 , 20 ). The kinetic parameters for the hydrolysis of cephem antibiotics and the monobactam aztreonam are similar for both SGM-1 and BEL-1, but BEL-2 has a much higher affinity for extended-spectrum cephalosporins and aztreonam due to a L162F mutation (compared to BEL-1) within the Ω-loop ( 20 ). It is interesting that SGM-1 also contains a leucine at position 162, as in BEL-1 ( Fig. 1 ). KPC-type carbapenemases also have high catalytic efficiency toward penicillins and, like BEL-type enzymes, have relatively lower rates of hydrolysis ( k cat = 13 to 280 s −1 ) and lower values for K m (18 to 130 μM) ( 19 , 22 , 24 – 26 ) compared to SGM-1. The kinetic parameters for hydrolysis of cephalosporins and aztreonam are similar between SGM-1 and KPC-type enzymes. Taken together, this indicates that the hydrolytic efficiency of SGM-1 and BEL-/KPC-type β-lactamases for penicillins, cephalosporins, and the monobactam aztreonam are not significantly different. 

Of the four carbapenems evaluated, imipenem was the only one that did not exhibit biphasic turnover kinetics with SGM-1. Despite the low turnover of imipenem ( k cat = 0.028 s −1 ), the low K m (1.2 μM) contributed to a moderate catalytic efficiency of the enzyme for this antibiotic (2 × 10 4 M −1 s −1 ). The steady-state kinetic parameters of SGM-1 for imipenem are comparable to those for GES-2 ( k cat = 0.012 s −1 and K m = 0.7 μM), with both enzymes conferring only a 2-fold increase in the MIC of the antibiotic above background level ( 10 ). Hydrolysis of three other carbapenems (meropenem, ertapenem, and doripenem) by SGM-1 displayed biphasic turnover kinetics. This behavior is not unique to SGM-1, as biphasic kinetics have been observed during turnover of carbapenems by other class A β-lactamases, such as TEM-1, and are likely due to conformational changes within the active site of the enzyme that lead to slower steady-state deacylation rates ( 27 , 28 ). The k cat values for the initial phase of hydrolysis of meropenem, ertapenem, and doripenem (∼0.01 to 0.02 s −1 ) were over an order of magnitude faster than for the steady-state phase ( k cat ≈0.00040 s −1 ). The half-life for the initial phase of hydrolysis for each antibiotic was 2 to 3 min. Since the bacterial doubling time is about 20 min, and the initial phase of hydrolysis of the antibiotics is significantly faster than in the steady state, both phases would contribute to hydrolysis in vivo . SGM-1 was able to hydrolyze two to three molecules of these antibiotics prior to the transition to steady-state turnover, similar to what was observed for TEM-1 ( 29 ). The relative affinity of SGM-1 for meropenem, ertapenem, and doripenem ( K m = 5 to 20 μM) is comparable to what is observed with other KPC-type carbapenemases ( K m = 3 to 10 μM) ( 19 , 22 , 26 ). Thus, poor turnover of these carbapenems by SGM-1 accounts for the lower catalytic efficiency of this enzyme compared to the KPC-type β-lactamases ( k cat / K m = 1.3 × 10 5 to 1.4 × 10 6 M −1 s −1 ) ( 19 , 22 , 26 , 30 ) and, consequently, the inability of SGM-1 to confer resistance to carbapenem antibiotics. 

The dissociation constants for the β-lactamase inhibitors clavulanic acid, sulbactam, and tazobactam from SGM-1 were 0.36, 0.66, and 0.16 μM, respectively ( Table 4 ). Despite the high affinity of SGM-1 toward all inhibitors, only clavulanic acid substantially decreased the MIC of penicillins ( Table 2 ). This indicates that other factors, such as hydrolysis, contribute to the inability of tazobactam and sulbactam to efficiently inhibit the enzyme. To evaluate whether β-lactam inhibitors are substrates for SGM-1, we determined their MICs ( Table 2 ). The MICs of clavulanic acid and tazobactam did not change in the presence of SGM-1; however, the MIC of sulbactam increased 4-fold, indicating that sulbactam is a substrate for SGM-1. As such, we evaluated the steady-state kinetic parameters for hydrolysis of sulbactam ( Table 3 ) and observed its moderate turnover ( k cat = 0.3 s −1 ).

To gain further insight into the inhibition process, we determined the partition ratio for clavulanic acid, tazobactam, and sulbactam with SGM-1. The partition ratio is a measure of the number molecules of inhibitor the β-lactamase turns over prior to irreversible inactivation of the enzyme ( 15 ). SGM-1 was able to turn over only ∼100 molecules of clavulanic acid before becoming irreversibly inactivated, whereas tazobactam and sulbactam were more capable of avoiding irreversible inhibition, with partition ratios of 3,900 and 13,000, respectively ( Table 4 ). The low partition ratio for clavulanic acid explains why it is a more potent inhibitor than tazobactam or sulbactam. The partition ratios and inhibitor dissociation constants for SGM-1 with clavulanic acid and sulbactam were similar to those reported for the TEM-1 β-lactamase ( r = 125; K i = 0.4 μM and r = 10,000; K i = 1.6 μM, respectively) ( 31 ), whereas these parameters for tazobactam were more comparable to GES-2 ( r = 7500, K i = 0.7 μM) ( 32 ). 

Conclusion. 

SGM-1 is a novel class A β-lactamase that contains two cysteine residues conserved in all currently known class A carbapenemases and shares <42% amino acid sequence identity with these enzymes. The antibiotic susceptibility profile and our steady-state kinetic analysis show that SGM-1 is an extended-spectrum β-lactamase, susceptible to inhibition by clavulanic acid but not sulbactam or tazobactam, but is not a carbapenemase. High amino acid sequence identity with putative class A β-lactamases conserved within the chromosome of all species of the genus Sphingobium strongly indicates that SGM-1 is native in these microorganisms. Although SGM-1 has not yet been detected in a clinical setting, the presence of the SGM-type enzymes in all Sphingobium species, widely distributed in the environment, constitutes a potential threat for the dissemination of these novel extended-spectrum enzymes into clinically relevant pathogens.
