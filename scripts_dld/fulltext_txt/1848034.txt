                  Changes in cytoplasmic levels of free-ionized calcium regulate the contraction and relaxation of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle. Aside from changing the intrinsic rate of energy use within the cell, an intervention that alters the strength of contraction of cardiac or vascular smooth muscle must in general alter either the intracellular calcium level, change the calcium requirements of the contractile apparatus, or exert both effects. Intracellular calcium handling is modulated by at least three other important second messengers: cyclic adenosine monophosphate, cyclic guanosine monophosphate, and inositol 1,4,5 triphosphate. These substances modulate intracellular calcium handling at multiple steps in the excitation-contraction coupling scheme. In vascular smooth muscle, diacylglycerol appears to play an important role in the regulation of myofilament calcium requirements. Intracellular calcium homeostasis is maintained through the action of sarcolemmal mechanisms that extrude calcium into the extracellular space; these mechanisms include a sodium-calcium exchange mechanism and an energy-dependent calcium pump. Abnormalities in electrophysiologic properties, sarcoplasmic reticulum function, energy use and supply, and contractile element interaction have been identified in experimental studies of animals and patients with various cardiovascular diseases. These abnormalities may be differentially affected by therapeutic agents that act on the heart or vasculature; therefore it is important to understand the underlying cellular abnormalities and subcellular actions of inotropic, vasoconstrictor, and vasodilatory drugs to apply rational therapeutics in the clinical setting.

   </xocs:meta><xocs:rawtext xmlns:xoe="http://www.elsevier.com/xml/xoe/dtd">The cellular basis of contraction and relaxation in cardiac and vascular smooth muscle  Changes in cytoplasmic levels of free-ionized calcium regulate the contraction and relaxation of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle. Aside from changing the intrinsic rate of energy use within the cell, an intervention that alters the strength of contraction of cardiac or vascular smooth muscle must in general alter either the intracellular calcium level, change the calcium requirements of the contractile apparatus, or exert both effects. Intracellular calcium handling is modulated by at least three other important second messengers: cyclic adenosine monophosphate, cyclic guanosine monophosphate, and inositol 1,4,5 triphosphate. These substances modulate intracellular calcium handling at multiple steps in the excitation-contraction coupling scheme. In vascular smooth muscle, diacylglycerol appears to play an important role in the regulation of myofilament calcium requirements. Intracellular calcium homeostasis is maintained through the action of sarcolemmal mechanisms that extrude calcium into the extracellular space; these mechanisms include a sodium-calcium exchange mechanism and an energy-dependent calcium pump. Abnormalities in electrophysiologic properties, sarcoplasmic reticulum function, energy use and supply, and contractile element interaction have been identified in experimental studies of animals and patients with various cardiovascular diseases. These abnormalities may be differentially affected by therapeutic agents that act on the heart or vasculature; therefore it is important to understand the underlying cellular abnormalities and subcellular actions of inotropic, vasoconstrictor, and vasodilatory drugs to apply rational therapeutics in the clinical setting. (AM HEART J 1991;121:961.)  James P. Morgan, MD, PhD, Cynthia L. Perreault, BS, Kathleen G. Morgan, PhD.  Boston, Mass.  The intracellular concentration of free, ionized calcium ions ([Ca 2+ji) is a primary determinant of contractile activity in skeletal, cardiac, and vascular smooth muscle.1 Most interventions that affect the strength of contraction of muscle must in general alter either the intracellular Ca2+ level itself, change the Ca2+ requirements of the contractile apparatus, or exert both effects. The most direct way to determine which of these actions an intervention might have is to record [Ca2+ji in active muscle and correlate the results with mechanical activity. Several suitable intracellular calcium indicators are currently available for experimental application, including bioluminescent and fluorescent compounds. Aequorin is a bioluminescent calcium indicator that emits light when it combines with calcium. In our laboratories, aequorin is the Ca2+ indicator of choice for multicellular studies, because (1) it is possible to  From the Charles A. METHODS  Aequorin has been used to study excitation-contraction coupling processes in a wide variety of skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle preparations. The advantages and disadvantages of aequorin relative to other calcium indicators have been the subject of comprehensive reviews.5 The most common method for loading aequorin into the cytoplasm of cells is by pressure injection through fine-tipped glass micropipettes.s This method works well for relatively large cells, such as skeletal muscle fibers, in which injection during a single pipette penetration can produce bright aequorin signals. By way of contrast, in cardiac muscle cells, microinjection is a long and tedious process, because usually 40 to 100 superficial cells must be penetrated and loaded to obtain a detectable light response. Microinjection  961  962 Morgan et al.  March 1991  American Heart Journal  of smooth muscle cells, which are smaller than cardiac myocytes, is an even more difficult and time-consuming technical accomplishment.'  Because of the technical difficulty of microinjection of photoproteins, alternative approaches to loading have been explored in several laboratories. The attempted approaches include liposome fusion, temporary disruption of cell membranes by short high-voltage pulses or ultrasonography, exposure to hypoosmolar or "hyperpermeabilizing" solutions, centrifugation of cell suspensions, "scrape loading" of cell cultures, and fusion of cells with photoprotein-loaded ghost cells (for a review see Blinks5). Most of these techniques have not been applied successfully to cardiac or vascular smooth muscle, although hypoosmolar solutions have been used to load aequorin into dispersed rat ventricular cells.8 With regard to intact multicellular preparations of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle, the use of "hyperpermeabilizing" solutions has provided the only alternative to microinjection.9 As originally described by Sutherland et al.,' this method involves sequentially exposing muscles to three different solutions that are designed to make the cell membranes hyperpermeable (solution 1), load photoprotein into the cells (solution 2), and reverse the hyperpermeable state (solution 3). The rationale for this approach centered on the use of high concentrations of the Cat+-chelator ethyleneglycol-bis(0-aminoethylether)-N,N'-tetraacetic acid (EGTA) in solution 1 to render the sarcolemma permeable to aequorin. In 1982 we modified this technique for use with aequorin in mammalian smooth muscle and amphibian cardiac muscle.10 Incomplete recovery of mammalian working myocardium led to further modifications in 1983 and 1984 and resulted in the development of a useful alternative to microinjection, which, however, still used high concentrations of EGTA." We recently modified this approach so that the concentration of calcium chelator in the loading solutions can be reduced, thereby minimizing the chances of irreversible damage to the cell membranes, and we have described an approach for applying this technique to muscles surrounded by thickened connective tissue sheaths.12 Moreover, we have applied this technique to the isolated perfused whole heart preparation.13 Representative aequorin signals are shown in Figs. 1 to 3.  Interpretation of aequorin signals is complicated by the nonlinearity of the light response, which is directly proportional to the photoprotein concentration, but increases as a power of the free calcium concentration, that is, as [Ca2+]n. Over the range of calcium concentrations relevant to the experiments described in this article, n = 2.5; therefore a 5.7-fold increase in light will be produced by a twofold increase in [Ca2+]i. An important consequence of this nonlinear relationship is that if the [Cal+]i is nonuniform in a given cell, the intensity of the light emission will be higher than it would be if the same amount of calcium were distributed uniformly. Therefore the amplitude of the aequorin signal will be dominated by regions of the cell that contain the highest Cat+ concentrations, that is, entry and release sites, and except under conditions in which the intracellular calcium concentration reaches a steady state, does not necessarily reflect the Cat+ concentration imme  diately surrounding the myofilaments. For this reason, aequorin signals recorded in these experiments were not usually translated into quantitative calcium concentrations. Although other methods for recording Ca 2+ are available, including metallochromic and fluorescent indicators and Ca 2+-selective electrodes, bioluminescent indicators such as aequorin have several advantages for use in physiologic systems, as recently reviewed by Blinks.5  EXCITATION-CONTRACTION COUPLING IN CARDIAC MUSCLE  In the mammalian heart, excitation-contraction coupling is initiated when depolarization permits trigger Ca 2+ to enter the myoplasm through calcium channels located in the sarcolemma; this trigger Cat+ releases a much larger quantity of activator Cat+ from intracellular stores contained in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 4).1-4,14 The released Ca 21 interacts with troponin-C of the regulatory complex on the myofilaments to initiate cardiac contraction; relaxation occurs when Ca 2+ dissociates from the contractile apparatus and is resequestered by the energydependent Ca 2+ pump of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (an adenosine triphosphatase [ATPase]). Intracellular Cat+ homeostasis is maintained by sarcolemmal mechanisms that extrude Cat+ into the extracellular space, including a sodium-calcium exchange mechanism and an energy-dependent Ca 2+ pump (not shown in Fig. 4). The initial excitatory event of membrane depolarization is also associated with intracellular entry of Na+, which is ultimately extruded by the energy-dependent Na+/K+ pump (an ATPase) or by the sodium-calcium exchanger, which operates on the basis of concentration gradients and does not appear to require adenosine triphosphate. Each of these steps represents a potential site of action for inotropic drugs. Fig. 4 also shows that Cat+ entry by way of the voltage-dependent Ca 2+ channels and resequestration of Ca 2+ by the sarcoplasmic reticulum are governed by their degree of phosphorylation (indicated by P).  The scheme outlined above focuses on the central role played by the Cat+ ion in the mediation of excitation-contraction coupling. Under normal circumstances, the cardiac myocyte is able to maintain homeostatic [Ca 2+1i against a large transsarcolemmal concentration gradient. Abnormal function at any of the steps outlined in Fig. 4 could result in systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction. Fig. 1 shows the light signals (i.e., intracellular Cat+ transients) and isometric contractions recorded in aequorin-loaded cardiac muscle of a control patient without heart failure, a patient with dilated cardiomyopathy, and a patient with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy." EXCITATION-CONTRACTION COUPLING IN VASCULAR SMOOTH MUSCLE.  Many of the interventions used to treat heart failure, including common inotropic agents, afterload  and preload-reducing agents, and diuretics, have pronounced effects on vascular tone. 26 Changes in [Ca 2+], play a central role in the contractionrelaxation cycles of vascular smooth muscle, although the specific steps appear to be somewhat different. 2-4, 27-3o In smooth muscle, Ca 2' entering the cytoplasm from either the extracellular space or the intracellular stores activates the enzyme myosin light chain kinase (Fig. 6). Myosin light chain kinase mediates the phosphorylation of the 20,000 dalton light chains of myosin, which in the presence of actin will then lead to an increase in myosin-ATPase activity and promote cross-bridge cycling. Before the muscle can relax, the myosin must be dephosphorylated by a phosphatase that does not appear to be Ca t+-dependent. Therefore in contrast to cardiac muscle, Ca 2+ may serve only as a trigger to initiate contraction, since the rate of relaxation appears to be governed by factors other than the [Ca2+]i level.  Once the contraction develops, force maintenance also appears to be largely independent of [Ca''+]i, at least in the presence of certain inotropic drugs.3°, 31 For example, the lower panel of Fig. 2 shows the correlation between [Ca 2+]i and tension responses with potassium depolarization. Note that the [Ca 2+1, profile more or less parallels the force profile in response to potassium depolarization; in contrast, in response to phenylephrine (upper panel of Fig. 2), a transient increase in [Ca2+]i during the development of the tonic contraction is followed by a decline in [Ca 2+], to near basal levels during the period of tone maintenance. Also note that the amount of force maintained per unit of elevation of [Ca 2+], differs in the presence of these two agonists, that is, the steady state force versus calcium ratio is much greater in the presence of phenylephrine than in the presence of potassium  Volume 121 Number 3, Part 1  Contraction/relaxation of cardiouascular muscle 967  depolarization. These types of data suggest that the Ca" responsiveness of the contractile apparatus can be modified in vascular smooth muscle as it can in cardiac muscle." In studies of drugs with vasodilator effects that might be useful in the management of patients with heart failure, we found that agents that act to increase intracellular cyclic AMP concentrations, such as isoproterenol and papaverine, can cause an apparent increase in the [Ca2+]i requirement for contraction; thus smooth muscle relaxation may be observed in the presence of constant or even increasing levels of Ca2+ .31  In addition to the myosin light chain kinase system, there appear to be functionally calcium-independent mechanisms of contraction in vascular smooth muscle that can be activated by agonists such as prostaglandin F2., 32 and phorbol esters. 33 For example, Fig. 3 shows that contraction can occur in the absence of any significant increase in [Ca 2+]i. These types of studies suggest that a "second system," which appears to have a very low Ca2+ requirement, might involve activation of protein kinase C. The mechanism by which this might occur is illustrated in Fig. 7 as originally proposed by Berridge34 and Nishizuka.31 When most agonists bind to receptors in smooth muscle and other tissue types, the binding is transduced into a cellular response by a sequence of events. Mediated through a G protein, phospholipase C is activated and splits phosphatidyl inositol biphosphate in the cell membrane into two products, diacylglycerol and IP3. IP3 has been shown to be capable of releasing [Ca2+]i from the sarcoplasmic reticulum in smooth muscle and probably causes the initial spike-like increase in [Ca2+] with agonists that cause [Ca 2+1, signals such as that shown in the upper panel of Fig. 2. The other compound, diacylglycerol, has been shown by Nishizuka35 to be capable of activating protein kinase C. The activation of protein kinase C is [Ca 2+]i dependent, but Nishizuka has shown that in the presence of an adequate concentration of diacylglycerol, the [Ca 2+1i requirements of protein kinase C can be saturated by resting cellular Ca2+ levels. Therefore it is feasible that protein kinase C can act in a functionally [Ca 2+1 i-independent manner. Protein kinase C probably phosphorylates a presently undefined protein to cause contraction.29  The above sequence of events can explain the [Ca 2+]i-force relationship shown in the lower panel of Fig. 2, but not a third type of pattern in which there is no increase in [Ca 2+1,.29 The left-hand side of Fig. 7 shows a possible explanation for these data. When phospholipase C or D acts on other membrane lipids that do not contain IP3, such as phosphatidyl choline, diacylglycerol can be released in the absence of any increase in IP336, 3' and consequently in the absence of any change in [Ca 2+]i.             <ce:abstract class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para view="all" id="simple-para.0010">Changes in cytoplasmic levels of free-ionized calcium regulate the contraction and relaxation of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle. Aside from changing the intrinsic rate of energy use within the cell, an intervention that alters the strength of contraction of cardiac or vascular smooth muscle must in general alter either the intracellular calcium level, change the calcium requirements of the contractile apparatus, or exert both effects. Intracellular calcium handling is modulated by at least three other important second messengers: cyclic adenosine monophosphate, cyclic guanosine monophosphate, and inositol 1,4,5 triphosphate. These substances modulate intracellular calcium handling at multiple steps in the excitation-contraction coupling scheme. In vascular smooth muscle, diacylglycerol appears to play an important role in the regulation of myofilament calcium requirements. Intracellular calcium homeostasis is maintained through the action of sarcolemmal mechanisms that extrude calcium into the extracellular space; these mechanisms include a sodium-calcium exchange mechanism and an energy-dependent calcium pump. Abnormalities in electrophysiologic properties, sarcoplasmic reticulum function, energy use and supply, and contractile element interaction have been identified in experimental studies of animals and patients with various cardiovascular diseases. These abnormalities may be differentially affected by therapeutic agents that act on the heart or vasculature; therefore it is important to understand the underlying cellular abnormalities and subcellular actions of inotropic, vasoconstrictor, and vasodilatory drugs to apply rational therapeutics in the clinical setting.</ce:simple-para>
