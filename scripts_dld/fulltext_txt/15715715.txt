                  Despite a trend of declining consumption, resistance to co-trimoxazole has increased during a 12-year period in Stockholm. The molecular background to this surprising development was investigated by using PCR to screen for integrons and specific resistance genes, followed by sequence analysis of selected integrons, in 105 clinical urinary isolates of Gram-negative bacteria selected partly for trimethoprim resistance. Sixty-five integrons of class 1 or 2 were detected in a subset of 59 isolates, and of these positive isolates, all but one were resistant to trimethoprim. However, 11 isolates were resistant to trimethoprim, but negative for integrons. Isolates positive for integrons were resistant to an average of 4.2 antibiotics, compared with 1.9 antibiotics for integron-negative isolates. Despite this, the only gene cassettes identified in 19 class 1 integrons analysed were dfr and aadA cassettes. Thus, only resistance to trimethoprim, streptomycin, spectinomycin and sulphonamides could be explained by the presence of integrons in these isolates. A new dfr gene, named dfrA22, was discovered as a single gene cassette in a class 1 integron. In addition, sulphonamide resistance in many isolates was caused by carriage of sul2, which has no known association with integrons. Resistance to co-trimoxazole and many other antibiotics was thus not accounted for fully by the presence of integrons in these isolates.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara60" view="all">Despite a trend of declining consumption, resistance to co-trimoxazole has increased during a 12-year period in Stockholm. The molecular background to this surprising development was investigated by using PCR to screen for integrons and specific resistance genes, followed by sequence analysis of selected integrons, in 105 clinical urinary isolates of Gram-negative bacteria selected partly for trimethoprim resistance. Sixty-five integrons of class 1 or 2 were detected in a subset of 59 isolates, and of these positive isolates, all but one were resistant to trimethoprim. However, 11 isolates were resistant to trimethoprim, but negative for integrons. Isolates positive for integrons were resistant to an average of 4.2 antibiotics, compared with 1.9 antibiotics for integron-negative isolates. Despite this, the only gene cassettes identified in 19 class 1 integrons analysed were <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> cassettes. Thus, only resistance to trimethoprim, streptomycin, spectinomycin and sulphonamides could be explained by the presence of integrons in these isolates. A new <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> gene, named <ce:italic>dfrA22</ce:italic>, was discovered as a single gene cassette in a class 1 integron. In addition, sulphonamide resistance in many isolates was caused by carriage of <ce:italic>sul2</ce:italic>, which has no known association with integrons. Resistance to co-trimoxazole and many other antibiotics was thus not accounted for fully by the presence of integrons in these isolates.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">INTRODUCTION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Overuse and misuse of antimicrobial agents is widespread throughout the world. As a result, resistance rates to available and affordable antimicrobial agents continue to increase in many countries for many pathogenic and commensal bacteria [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib2 bib3 bib4">1–4</ce:cross-refs>]. In Sweden, use of antimicrobial agents is quite restricted, so overuse and misuse is less frequent than in many other parts of the world; however, increasing rates of resistance to many antimicrobial agents have been observed [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>]. In a 12-year investigation at the Karolinska Hospital in Stockholm, a trend towards rapidly declining consumption of trimethoprim-sulphamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) associated with increasing resistance to the same agents was noted [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>]. This development suggests that the mechanism for selection of the corresponding resistance genes may not involve simply direct selection pressure by these drugs. In earlier studies of the relationship between consumption of trimethoprim and resistance to the drug, performed in Finland, no clear correlation could be established [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. A decreased consumption of trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole seemed to have little immediate effect on the overall resistance situation.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Streptomycin provides an obvious example of the slow decline in antimicrobial resistance. This agent has scarcely been used in human therapy during the last 20 years, yet resistance levels to streptomycin remain high in clinical isolates [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>]. Similarly, in spite of a dramatic decrease in sulphonamide consumption in the UK, ascribed to the restricted prescription of the drug since 1995, sulphonamide resistance remains common in clinical isolates of <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>]. It seems probable that co-selection of antibiotic resistance genes occurs when many types of antibiotics are used, but precise knowledge is lacking in most cases. It is also possible that other agents, such as heavy metals and certain types of disinfectants and antiseptics, could contribute to co-selection, resulting in an increase in antimicrobial resistance levels.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Other factors that probably contribute to the spread of antimicrobial resistance are genetic elements called integrons. These elements are capable of insertion, excision and expression of gene cassettes. Integration and excision of gene cassettes occur by site-specific recombination, catalysed by an integrase, <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>, encoded by the 5'-conserved segment of the integron. This conserved segment also contains a recombination site, <ce:italic>attI</ce:italic>, and a promoter, P<ce:inf loc="post">ant</ce:inf>, which is common for the entire variable region containing the gene cassettes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib10 bib11 bib12">10–12</ce:cross-refs>]. Downstream of class 1 integrons, a semiconserved segment containing the <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic> genes, encoding resistance to sulphonamides and quaternary ammonium compounds, respectively, can often be found. Integrons can carry many different types of gene cassettes, and practically all of those so far characterised are antibiotic resistance genes. At the present time, &gt;70 different gene cassettes are known, with up to nine cassettes observed in a single integron [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib13 bib14">13,14</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">A large group of trimethoprim resistance genes, clustering in three distinctive structural families, are found exclusively on cassettes in integrons. A smaller group of five genes, <ce:italic>dfr3, 4, 8, 9</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>10</ce:italic>, has so far not been found on cassettes. No <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> gene has been found to alternate between a cassette and a location elsewhere [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib15">15</ce:cross-ref>]. All <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes encode trimethoprim-resistant dihydrofolate reductases. The unusual dihydrofolate reductase genes <ce:italic>dfr2a, 2b, 2c</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>2d</ce:italic> constitute an homologous group of cassettes which is unrelated to any other <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes and which encodes peptides of 78 amino-acids. These genes are also called <ce:italic>dfrB</ce:italic> genes according to a different nomenclature system [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>]. The remaining group of <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>.20 dihydrofolate reductases (encoded by <ce:italic>dfrA</ce:italic> genes in the alternative nomenclature) are around 160 amino-acids in size and share 20–95% identity [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib13 bib17 bib18">13,17,18</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para50" view="all">There are only three known genes encoding resistance to sulphonamides in Gram-negative bacteria, namely <ce:italic>sul1, sul2</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>sul3.</ce:italic> The first gene, <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>, has been observed only as part of the 3'-conserved sequence in class 1 integrons, but <ce:italic>sul2</ce:italic> has never been found as part of an integron. The <ce:italic>sul3</ce:italic> gene was first reported in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> from pigs in Switzerland in 2003. It has also been described in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> from pigs, cattle and poultry in Germany [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib13 bib19 bib20 bib21 bib22">13,19–22</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para60" view="all">The aim of the present study was to investigate the molecular background of the increasing resistance to trimethoprim and sulphamethoxazole in a collection of clinical urinary isolates of Gram-negative bacteria. Following the observation of a new <ce:italic>dfr2</ce:italic> gene and the <ce:italic>sul3</ce:italic> gene in the same collection [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib23 bib24">23,24</ce:cross-refs>], the decision was taken to investigate the molecular characteristics of these isolates in detail by PCR and sequence analysis.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">MATERIALS AND METHODS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Bacteria</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">The study investigated 105 clinical urinary isolates of Gramnegative bacteria collected during May and June 2001 at the Department of Clinical Microbiology, Karolinska Hospital, Stockholm. Of these, 71 were consecutive trimethoprim-resistant or intermediately-resistant isolates, while the remaining 34 were consecutive trimethoprim-susceptible isolates. The isolates were identified initially by standard biochemical tests and were tested for resistance to trimethoprim by disk diffusion [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>] using Isosensitest agar and antibiotic disks (Oxoid AB, Sollentuna, Sweden). Zone breakpoints used for determining trimethoprim sensitivity and resistance were 17 mm and 13 mm, respectively, as recommended by the Swedish Reference Group for Antibiotics (SRGA) (<ce:inter-ref id="interrefs10a" xlink:href="http://www.srga.org" xlink:type="simple">http://www.srga.org</ce:inter-ref>) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib26 bib27">26,27</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Antibiotic resistance</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">An extended resistance test was performed by disk diffusion with disks containing ampicillin 10 µg, trimethoprim 5 µg, sulphonamides 300 µg (sulphathiazole 111 µg, sulphadiazine 78 µg and sulphamerazine 111 µg), chloramphenicol 30 µg, cefuroxime 30 µg, amoxycillin 20 µg with clavulanic acid 10 µg, gentamicin 30 µg, streptomycin 10 µg, amikacin 30 µg, netilmicin 30 µg, kanamycin 30 µg and spectinomycin 25 µg (Oxoid). Zone breakpoints were those recommended by SRGA, supplemented with breakpoints from NCCLS for amoxycillin-clavulanic acid, kanamycin, streptomycin and sulphonamides [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib26 bib27 bib28">26–28</ce:cross-refs>]. Breakpoints for spectinomycin were established in the laboratory.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">PCR</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">Templates for PCR were made by resuspending a loopful of bacterial growth in 100 µL of sterile filtered water (Sigma-Aldrich, Stockholm, Sweden) and boiling for <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>.2 min, followed by a short centrifugation step and storage at –20°C until use. PCR master mixes were prepared according to a standard protocol (Perkin Elmer/Wallac Sverige AB, Upplands Väsby, Sweden) in which 1.5 µL of template was added to 24 µL of reaction mixture containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> (Applied Biosystems, Stockholm, Sweden), 200 µM dNTPs (Amersham Biosciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden), 0.5 µM each primer, and 0.6 U <ce:italic>Taq</ce:italic>Gold (Applied Biosystems), pH 8.3. Amplifications were performed in a GeneAmp PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems) with one cycle of 95°C for 10 min, 30 cycles of 94°C for 45 s, 64, 51 or 55°C (as appropriate) for 45 s and 72°C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The sequences of the specific PCR primers and the annealing temperatures used for different primer pairs are listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Amplicons were analysed by electrophoresis on agarose (NuSieve 3 : 1; BioWhittaker/In vitro Sweden AB, Stockholm, Sweden) 0.5, 1.0 or 2.0% w/v gels in TAE buffer (4 mM Trisacetate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) at 100–120 V for 30–55 min. Molecular size markers used were numbers III, VI, VII and VIII from Boehringer Mannheim (Scandinavia AB, Bromma, Sweden). Gels were stained with ethidium bromide and photographed under UV light. <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strains carrying plasmids pMS128, R67, R483 or R100.1 (Department of Medical Biochemistry and Microbiology, Uppsala University, Sweden) were used in all PCR runs as positive controls for integrons of class 1 and 2 and for the <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> gene, respectively. A sample with no DNA template was used as a negative control in every batch of PCRs. Positive controls were not available for some of the primer pairs; these PCRs were repeated at several annealing temperatures and primer concentrations to confirm negative results.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">DNA sequence analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">For sequence analysis, 45 µL of PCR products were first purified with the Jet quick spin column technique kit or the Jet quick gel extraction kit (Genomed/Saveen Werner AB, Malmö, Sweden). Primers used for sequence analysis were Igr1-f and -r, Igr1-f2 and -r2 and Sul3-f and -r (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). ABI Prism Big Dye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kits v.2.0 and 1.1 (Applied Biosystems) were used for the sequencing reactions, with 60 s at 96°C, 25 cycles of 96°C for 10 s, 50°C for 5 s and 60°C for 4 min, and a final extension at 60°C for 1 min. The sequencing reaction products were then precipitated according to the ethanol/sodium acetate or the ethanol/EDTA precipitation protocols recommended in the kit, followed by analysis with an ABI Prism 310 Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Selected isolates were first sequenced from Igr1-f2 and Igr1-r2 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). If the sequences obtained were not clearly overlapping, new primers were designed for further sequencing reactions (primer walking). The integrons of a few isolates were also sequenced with primers Igr1-f and Igr1-r (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) toanalyse the non-overlapping sequences at both ends. The raw sequence data were processed with the Sequencher program (Gene Codes Corporation, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Subsequent nucleotide sequence analysis was performed by BLAST analysis.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec80" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">Antibiotic susceptibility</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">
                        <ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/> lists the antibiotic susceptibilities of the 105 isolates included in the study. Since the isolates were selected in part on the basis of trimethoprim resistance, the figures do not represent the typical resistance levels found among urinary isolates in the Karolinska hospital. However, in addition to trimethoprim and sulphonamide resistance, resistance levels to ampicillin, streptomycin, chloramphenicol and spectinomycin were relatively high.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">PCR screening for integrons</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">In total, 65 integrons were detected in 59 isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). Class 1 integrons were more common than class 2 integrons. Of the 59 isolates, 37 carried a single class 1 integron, three carried two class 1 integrons, three carried one class 1 integron and one class 2 integron, and seven carried a single class 2 integron. In addition to these 56 integrons which included a gene-cassette region, nine isolates were positive for the class 1 integrase, and were therefore also considered to be positive for class 1 integrons. No class 3 or class 4 integrons were found.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">The amplification products of class 1 integrons varied in size between 1 and 2 kb, with three clusters of <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>.1.05 kb, 1.3 kb and 1.8 kb, while those of class 2 integrons were all of <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>.2.7 kb (corresponding to the total size of the three gene cassettes known to be most prevalent in conserved class 2 integrons and the associated 5'-and 3' segments amplified by the primers). No conclusions could be drawn regarding the distribution of integrons among different bacterial species since there were relatively few isolates of species other than <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Relationship between integrons and antimicrobial resistance</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para160" view="all">All the isolates positive for integrons, except one, were resistant to trimethoprim, and all isolates carrying a class 1 integron, except two, were also resistant to sulphonamides. No fully susceptible isolate was integron-positive. Of the 105 isolates tested, 53 were resistant to four or more antibiotics, but only nine of these isolates were of a species other than <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>. Of these 53 isolates, 43 were integron-positive; thus ten isolates remained that were resistant to at least four antimicrobials and were integron-negative. Overall, isolates that were integron-negative expressed resistance to an average of 1.9 antimicrobial agents, while integronpositive isolates were resistant to an average of 4.2 of the 12 antimicrobial agents tested.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para170" view="all">Eleven isolates were resistant to trimethoprim and integron-negative, and PCRs for <ce:italic>dfr3, 8, 9</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>10</ce:italic>, i.e., four of the five <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes not known to form part of gene cassettes, were also negative (the sequence of the <ce:italic>dfr4</ce:italic> gene has not yet been published and it was therefore not possible to design a PCR for this gene). Thus, the molecular basis of trimethoprim resistance in these 11 isolates remained unexplained.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Sulphonamide resistance and <ce:italic>sul</ce:italic> genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para180" view="all">Of 66 sulphonamide-resistant isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>), 36 carried a <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> gene, in connection with a class 1 integron, and 45 carried a <ce:italic>sul2</ce:italic> gene, with 25 isolates positive for both <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>sul2.</ce:italic> Four isolates were sulphonamide-resistant and negative for both <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>sul2.</ce:italic> These four isolates were tested by PCR for the <ce:italic>sul3</ce:italic> gene, which was discovered recently in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> isolates from pigs in Switzerland [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>]. Two of these four isolates were positive [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>], but the remaining 64 isolates were negative. Thus two isolates remained that were sulphonamide-resistant and negative for all three known <ce:italic>sul</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para190" view="all">One isolate was positive for <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>, but negative by PCR with three different primer-pairs specific for class 1 integrons. Apart from sulphonamides and trimethoprim, this isolate was also resistant to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, streptomycin and spectinomycin, piperacillin, norfloxacin and ciprofloxacin. PCR with a forward primer specific for <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic> and a reverse primer specific for <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> was positive, and the size of the amplified product corresponded to the expected size of the sequence and that of the positive control isolate with an intact 3' integron sequence.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">Sequence analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para200" view="all">Gene cassettes containing known <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes are in the size range 380–620 bp, while cassettes containing <ce:italic>aad</ce:italic> genes mediating resistance to aminoglycosides have sizes of 590–860 bp [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="bib13">13</ce:cross-ref>]. The integron cassette regions found in the present study had sizes consistent with the resistance patterns conferred upon their hosts, with the exception of 19 integrons in 18 isolates which were selected for sequence analysis. Seven different <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes, three different <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> genes and one <ce:italic>orf</ce:italic> cassette were found, but no other gene cassettes conferring resistance to other antimicrobial agents were identified. The clusters of integron PCR products (1.05 kb, 1.3 kb and 1.8 kb) corresponded to integrons carrying a <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> gene cassette, an <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> gene cassette, and a combined <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> gene cassette, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para210" view="all">Two of the gene cassettes revealed previously unknown sequences. The first was a homologue to the group of <ce:italic>dfr2</ce:italic> gene cassettes. This new gene was designated <ce:italic>dfr2d</ce:italic> (alternatively, <ce:italic>dfrB4</ce:italic>; EMBL accession no. AJ429132) and appeared as a single cassette in the integron [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>]. The second new gene was also detected as a single gene cassette in a class 1 integron and was named <ce:italic>dfr22.</ce:italic> This gene (<ce:italic>dfrA22</ce:italic> according to an alternative nomenclature; EMBL accession no. AJ628423) was <ce:italic>c</ce:italic>.93% identical to <ce:italic>dfr13</ce:italic> and belongs to the small subgroup which previously contained only <ce:italic>dfr12</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>dfr13</ce:italic>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para220" view="all">Antibiotic resistance levels among Gram-negative bacteria in Sweden are fairly low compared with levels in many other parts of the world [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>], possibly indicating that resistance-mediating elements occur less frequently. However, an increase in resistance to co-trimoxazole in <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> has been observed in Sweden, despite a decrease in the use of co-trimoxazole during the same period. The present study therefore investigated 105 clinical urinary isolates of Gram-negative bacteria with respect to their antimicrobial resistance and the molecular basis for this resistance. Trimethoprim and sulphonamide resistance in the isolates could, in many cases, be explained by the presence of integrons carrying <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> gene cassettes and <ce:italic>sul</ce:italic> genes. However, 11 isolates expressed trimethoprim resistance encoded by unidentified genes, and ten of the isolates that were negative for integron carriage expressed resistance to at least four antibiotics. This result was in contrast to the observation that isolates carrying integrons are usually resistant to twice as many antibiotics as isolates not carrying integrons. These phenomena could be explained by a failure of the PCR to recognise integrons containing <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes and other genes present in the isolates, perhaps because of changes in the primer target sites, or it is possible that the trimethoprim resistance was encoded by unknown <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes not carried by integrons.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para230" view="all">Sequence analysis identified different <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> gene cassettes (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). This variation in <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> gene cassettes, and the variation in antibiograms observed (results not shown), confirmed that clonal spread was not causing the observed increase in resistance. Only resistance to trimethoprim, spectinomycin, streptomycin and sulphonamides was shown to be integronmediated. It was surprising that no antibiotic resistance gene cassettes, other than <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic>, were identified in the 19 integrons analysed by sequencing.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para240" view="all">Selective pressure from one antibiotic can simultaneously select for genes mediating resistance to other antibiotics when they are physically linked, e.g., in the form of gene cassettes in integrons. This was thought to be responsible for the increasing resistance to trimethoprim and sulphamethoxazole, since the combination of these two drugs is no longer used to a great extent. The genes conferring the resistance, <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>sul</ce:italic>, are often linked in the widely distributed integrons of class 1. Trimethoprim is still used for the treatment of urinary tract infection, but is often replaced by other drugs, such as nitrofurantoin, fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib29 bib30">29,30</ce:cross-refs>]. According to the model of Austin <ce:italic>et al</ce:italic>. [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>], the current consumption of trimethoprim might be sufficient to maintain levels of trimethoprim and sulphamethoxazole resistance, and possibly cause a further increase in resistance. Continued selective pressure by a constant consumption has been shown to be enough for a sigmoid rise in resistance levels, which then explains a further rise before the resistance stabilises at an equilibrium level. Unfortunately, the delayed resistance response curve, according to this theory, does not suggest that an equally fast reduction in resistance levels will occur following a decline in the overall amount of drug used. Experimental studies in Finland and the UK have yielded the same result [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib7 bib9 bib31">7,9,31</ce:cross-refs>], and it seems that if the ‘fitness cost’ of resistance is sufficiently low, even rather modest consumption of an antibiotic can cause an overall increase in resistance to the drug in society as a whole [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>]. Integrons still carry, and often express, <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> gene cassettes which confer resistance to streptomycin and spectinomycin, antibiotics that are used only rarely in human medicine at present. Integrons have a capability to excise gene cassettes which are not useful for the host bacterial cell, but these cassettes remain stable in many bacterial isolates. This suggests that carriage of integron-borne resistance gene cassettes does not impose a significant burden on the bacteria.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para250" view="all">Despite the above observations, the total sulphonamide consumption in humans in Sweden is probably insufficient to drive an increase in sulphonamide resistance. In this and a previous study [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>], a significant proportion of the sulphonamide resistance observed was conferred by the acquisition of <ce:italic>sul2</ce:italic> genes which have no known connection to integrons and <ce:italic>dfr</ce:italic> genes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>]. Theobserved increase in co-trimoxazole resistance can therefore not be ascribed solely to trimethoprim consumption. Veterinary use of these drugs and the appearance of a third <ce:italic>sul</ce:italic> gene are possible clues to the complex mechanisms behind the continued increase in sulphonamide resistance. However, integrons are also linked to other resistance elements. Remarkably, properties that are not encoded by integron-borne gene cassettes also seem to be more prevalent among integroncarrying isolates as compared with those devoid of integrons. One such example is quinolone resistance, which is known to be caused mainly by chromosomal mutations, and yet still seems to be more common in isolates that also carry an integron [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs110" refid="bib33 bib34">33,34</ce:cross-refs>]. Additionally, there seems to be a correlation between the presence of integrons and resistance to a greater number of antibiotics [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs120" refid="bib14 bib34 bib35">14,34,35</ce:cross-refs>]. Since the integrons analysed in the present study did not contain gene cassettes conferring resistance to all the other different types of antibiotics to which the isolates were resistant, such resistance genes might be coselected with the integrons in another way. The 3' end of some class 1 integrons contains a region where genes which do not constitute gene cassettes have been acquired by an unknown mechanism. For example, the <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> gene seems to be inserted stably in the 3'-end of integrons without any trace of a cassette structure in its genetic surroundings.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para260" view="all">Overall, it seems that the continued rise in trimethoprim and sulphonamide resistance might be ascribed partly to a correlated spread of resistance genes caused by the selective pressure of antibiotics and the genetic linkage of integrons and the resistance genes they contain to other resistance-mediating elements. However, integrons are not always situated on plasmids, and plasmids are not always transferred easily between bacterial strains; indeed, very little is known about the spread of resistance elements <ce:italic>in vivo</ce:italic>. Thus, contrary to expectations and despite the identification of many integrons in the resistant isolates studied, no linkages of resistance genes were detected in integrons that could account for co-selection of co-trimoxazole resistance following the use of other antibacterial agents.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
