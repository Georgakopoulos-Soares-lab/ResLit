ABSTRACT 

A colistin-resistant Escherichia coli isolate from a commercial poultry farm in China carried two colistin resistance genes, mcr-1 and variant of mcr-3 , in an IncP plasmid. The variant of the mcr-3 gene, named mcr-3.11 , encoded two amino acid substitutions compared with the mcr-3 gene. A novel genetic structure, IS Kpn40-mcr-3-dgkA -IS Kpn40 , might be the key element mediating the translocation of mcr-3 through the formation of a circular form. The mcr-1 and mcr-3 genes, which are colocated on a plasmid, might pose a huge threat to public health. 

KEYWORDS: colistin, mcr - 3 , mcr - 1 , Escherichia coli , circular form 

TEXT 

The emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR) in Gram-negative pathogens has become a challenging threat to public health in recent years. Colistin is considered one of the last lines of defense against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. 

The emergence of the plasmid-mediated mobile colistin resistance gene mcr-1 was first identified on an IncI2 plasmid from Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae in 2016 ( 1 ). Until now, five kinds of mobile colistin resistance genes, mcr-1 to mcr-5 , have been reported ( 1 – 5 ). The gene mcr-3 was first characterized in the IncHI2-type plasmid from porcine Escherichia coli , followed by the characterization of 10 other mcr-3 variants. Recently, the mcr-3 gene has been proven to disseminate among Enterobacteriaceae ( 3 , 6 – 8 ) and aeromonads ( 9 , 10 ); aeromonads may act as a potential reservoir for mcr-3 . Compared with MCR-1, the MCR-3 protein has only 32.5% amino acid identity, and the mcr-3 gene in Enterobacteriaceae might have originated from Aeromonas species ( 3 ). In this study, we report the colocation of mcr-1 and a variant of mcr-3 on an IncP plasmid from an E. coli strain. 

In the current study, E. coli strain ECSC102 was isolated from a chicken cloacal swab in Sichuan Province, China, in 2015. Species identification was performed using a BD Phoenix-100 system (Becton Dickinson, USA) and confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The MICs of various antimicrobials were determined using the broth dilution method from the CLSI guidelines ( 11 ). Strain E. coli ECSC102 was resistant to ampicillin (MIC, >256 μg/ml), colistin (MIC, 8 μg/ml), cefotaxime (MIC, 16 μg/ml), ciprofloxacin (MIC, 32 μg/ml), cefoxitin (MIC, 64 μg/ml), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (MIC, 64/1,216 μg/ml), streptomycin (MIC, 256 μg/ml), and tetracycline (MIC, 256 μg/ml), and it was susceptible to imipenem (MIC, 0.125 μg/ml), tigecycline (MIC, <0.125 μg/ml), aztreonam (MIC, 0.25 μg/ml), and amikacin (MIC, 0.5 μg/ml). PCR amplification (see Table S1 in the supplemental material) and Sanger sequencing indicated that this isolate cocarried the mcr-1 and mcr-3 genes. 

Conjugation experiments were performed with E. coli ECSC102 as the donor and E. coli J53 (azide resistant [Azi r ]) as the recipient. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates (Oxoid) which contained 200 μg/ml sodium azide with 2 μg/ml colistin. Positive transconjugants were identified by the detection of colistin resistance phenotypes and PCR screening for the presence of mcr-1 and mcr-3 (Table S1). The results showed that the transconjugants carried genes mcr-1 and mcr-3 , exhibiting an MIC of 8 μg/ml for colistin, which was a 32-fold increase compared with that for the recipient E. coli J53 (Azi r ). The MIC of colistin for strain WCHECLL123 containing both mcr-3.5 and mcr-1 was 8 μg/ml, that for its transconjugant containing mcr-3.5 was 4 μg/ml, and that for its transconjugant containing mcr-1 was 4 μg/ml ( 7 ). When mcr-1 and mcr-3 genes were simultaneously present in the E. coli strain, there was a higher colistin MIC value than for mcr-1 or mcr-3 alone in the strain. 

The mcr-3 gene in this strain is a novel variant (designated mcr-3.11 ) encoding MCR-3 proteins with two amino acid substitutions (G373V and T468Q) compared with the original mcr-3 on plasmid pWJ1 (GenBank accession no. KY924928 ). Conserved domains of MCR-3.11 were predicted using CD-Search ( 12 ) in NCBI against the Pfam database. MCR-3.11 can be divided into two domains, a domain assigned with phosphoethanolamine transferase (sulfatase) (amino acids [aa] 233 to 518, pfam00884), and a domain predicted to be a transmembrane module (aa 56 to 206, pfam08019), which is the same as MCR-3 (Fig. S3). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that MCR-3.11 has close genetic distance from MCR-3 and MCR-3-like sequences (Fig. S3). Notably, MCR-3.11 originated from Aeromonas species. To assess the colistin resistance phenotype conferred by the mcr-3.11 gene, the corresponding 2,219-bp region (Table S1) derived from the plasmid DNA of the E. coli ECSC102 strain was cloned into a pUC57 vector and transformed into the colistin-susceptible strain E. coli DH5α (TaKaRa). The MIC of colistin for E. coli transformants containing mcr-3.11 was 4 μg/ml, which was 16-fold higher than that of E. coli DH5α alone (0.25 μg/ml). The MIC against E. coli W3110 carrying a cloned vector, pUC19, of the original mcr-3 gene was 4 μg/ml ( 3 ). These results revealed that the amino acid substitutions of MCR-3.11 have not altered its activity against colistin compared with the origin MCR-3. 

The total genomic DNA from the transconjugant that carried genes mcr-1 and mcr-3 was extracted and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China) using a 400-bp paired-end TruSeq library with a 2 × 300 run. The paired-end reads were assembled with SOAP denovo version 2.04 and GapCloser version 1.12. The gaps between different contigs were closed by PCR and Sanger sequencing; the primers used to close the gaps are listed in Table S1. Gene prediction and annotation were performed using the Rapid Annotations using Subsystems Technology (RAST) server ( http://rast.nmpdr.org/ ) and the BLAST program ( http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ). Identification of antibiotic resistance genes was performed using ResFinder 2.1 ( https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/ ). Plasmid replicon types were determined by the PlasmidFinder tool ( http://genomicepidemiology.org/ ). Sequence comparative analysis and generation of plasmids were performed with Easyfig ( 13 ) and BRIG ( 14 ). 

The variant of mcr-3 and the mcr-1 gene were colocated in a conjugative 60.7-kb IncP plasmid, designated pECSC102. It had a length of 60,744 bp, with 47.2% G+C content (Fig. S2). Plasmid pECSC102 aligned very well to two mcr -carrying IncP plasmids, pMCR3_WCHEC-LL123 (GenBank accession no. MF489760 ), found in an E. coli isolate, and pMCR_1511 (GenBank accession no. KX377410 ), found in a K. pneumoniae isolate ( Fig. 1 ). All three of these strains were isolated in Sichuan Province in Southwest China; however, pMCR3_WCHEC-LL12 was recovered from an abdominal abscess of a hospital patient ( 7 ), pMCR_1511 was recovered from the sewage of the hospital ( 15 ), and pECSC102 in this study was isolated from the cloacal swab from a chicken. The apparent spread of the same IncP plasmid with one or two mcr genes between different species and a patient, the hospital environment, and animal production is worrying. Sequence typing for strain ECSC102, performed as previously described ( http://mlst.warwick.ac.uk/mlst/dbs/Ecoli ), revealed that it belonged to sequence type 167 (ST167).

Complete sequence analysis of plasmid pECSC102 revealed that mcr-1 was located on a composite transposon, Tn 6330 ( 16 ), with direct repeats of IS Apl1 flanking mcr-1 . Gene mcr-3 was located on an IS Kpn40-mcr-3-dgkA -IS Kpn40 fragment, with direct repeats of IS Kpn40 flanking mcr-3 . A truncated form of mcr-3 was located on pMCR_1511, and the mechanism for the truncation of mcr-3 was unknown. The genetic configuration comparison between pECSC102 and pMCR3_LL123 showed that the mcr-1 gene was absent from pMCR3_LL123, and the genetic contexts of mcr-3 are not that similar in these two plasmids, as pMCR3_LL123 lacks IS Kpn40 ( Fig. 1 ). The identical backbone suggested that pECSC102, pMCR3_LL123, and pMCR_1511 were derived from a common ancestor. 

IS Kpn40 has a size of 1,213 bp and is flanked by 12-bp inverted repeats (TGTAATGACCCA), which belong to the IS 3 family; IS Kpn40 was first found in an Escherichia coli strain isolated from a swine feedlot ( 3 ). IS 911 and IS 150 are members of the large IS 3 family and proved to be able to form a circular intermediate ( 17 , 18 ). Since direct repeats of insertion sequences (IS) may mediate the dissemination of genes via the formation of a circular form ( 16 , 19 ), inverse PCR assays (Table S1) were performed to amplify the putative circular form that harbored the mcr-3 gene. One circular form of 3,535 bp, mcr-3-dgkA -IS Kpn40 , was confirmed by an inverse PCR assay. After aligning with the pECSC102 sequence, we found that this circular form was derived from the IS Kpn40-mcr-3-dgkA -IS Kpn40 element, which was 100% homologous to this region. BLASTN ( http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ) analysis showed that the 3,535-bp circular form exhibited 99% identity to the corresponding region of pWJ1 (GenBank accession no. KY924928.1 ). A truncated (41-bp remnant) IS Kpn40 was found upstream of mcr-3 in pWJ1, which presumably suggests directly repeated upstream and downstream IS Kpn40 copies at first and subsequently loss of the upstream IS copy during the mobilization of mcr-3 into the pWJ1. Compared with the mcr-3.11 -containing segment, a 12-bp AT-rich (ATTTTTTACAAA) deletion was found upstream of mcr-3 in pWJ1. The 12-bp nucleotides exist in pMCR3_WCHEC-LL123, which has IS 26 downstream of mcr-3.5 instead of IS Kpn40 ( Fig. 2 ). These findings suggest that the association of the resistance gene mcr-3 with IS Kpn40 might have facilitated its translocation.

Here, we report for the first time the cooccurrence of mcr-1 and mcr-3.11 on a single plasmid in an E. coli strain from a commercial poultry farm in China. The mcr-3 circular intermediate originating from IS Kpn40-mcr-3-dgkA -IS Kpn40 may be the key element responsible for mediating the translocation of the mcr-3 gene into various plasmids. The coexistence of the mcr-1 and mcr-3 genes in E. coli isolates may pose a huge threat to public health and warrant further investigation.
