                  Objectives
                  We characterized the first ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing-Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolate detected in Greece, before the introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam in clinical practice.
               
                  Methods
                  
                     K. pneumoniae KP-90 was isolated from a hospitalized patient in Thessaloniki during a nationwide surveillance study conducted between 2014 and 2016. Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested against a panel of agents. Whole-genome sequencing (Ion Torrent TM platform) of the isolate was carried out to identify the acquired resistance genes and mutations that were associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance.
               
                  Results
                  The K. pneumoniae isolate belonged to multilocus sequence type ST258 and harboured bla
                     KPC-23 as the only carbapenemase gene. The isolate had a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 16 mg/L to ceftazidime-avibactam and was highly resistant to imipenem, meropenem (MICs, 512 mg/L) and ceftazidime (MIC, &gt;1024 mg/L). bla
                     KPC-23 was detected on a Tn4401a transposon, located on a pKPQIL-type plasmid. A non-functional outer membrane protein OmpK35 and an OmpK36 variant that had been previously associated with K. pneumoniae isolates of ST258 were detected. Transformation studies with Escherichia coli TOP10 showed that KPC-23 offered similar carbapenem MICs as KPC-2 and KPC-3. However, KPC-23 conferred a four-fold higher ceftazidime MIC (&gt;1024 mg/L), which in the presence of avibactam was reduced (&gt;7-fold) to 8 mg/L, which is just within the limit of the susceptibility breakpoint.
               
                  Conclusions
                  Ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in a KPC-23- producing K. pneumoniae clinical isolate was due to increased ceftazidime hydrolysis and was likely enhanced by OmpK35 porin deficiency.

                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0015">Objectives</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">We characterized the first ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant KPC-producing-<ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> clinical isolate detected in Greece, before the introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam in clinical practice.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0015" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0020">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0015" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-90 was isolated from a hospitalized patient in Thessaloniki during a nationwide surveillance study conducted between 2014 and 2016. Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested against a panel of agents. Whole-genome sequencing (Ion Torrent TM platform) of the isolate was carried out to identify the acquired resistance genes and mutations that were associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec role="results" id="abssec0020" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0025">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0020" view="all">The <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate belonged to multilocus sequence type ST258 and harboured <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23</ce:inf> as the only carbapenemase gene. The isolate had a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 16 mg/L to ceftazidime-avibactam and was highly resistant to imipenem, meropenem (MICs, 512 mg/L) and ceftazidime (MIC, &gt;1024 mg/L). <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23</ce:inf> was detected on a Tn<ce:italic>4401a</ce:italic> transposon, located on a pKPQIL-type plasmid. A non-functional outer membrane protein OmpK35 and an OmpK36 variant that had been previously associated with <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolates of ST258 were detected. Transformation studies with <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> TOP10 showed that KPC-23 offered similar carbapenem MICs as KPC-2 and KPC-3. However, KPC-23 conferred a four-fold higher ceftazidime MIC (&gt;1024 mg/L), which in the presence of avibactam was reduced (&gt;7-fold) to 8 mg/L, which is just within the limit of the susceptibility breakpoint.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec role="conclusion" id="abssec0025" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0030">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0025" view="all">Ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in a KPC-23- producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> clinical isolate was due to increased ceftazidime hydrolysis and was likely enhanced by OmpK35 porin deficiency.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:section id="sec1" role="introduction" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0045">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">During a multicentre nationwide surveillance study that was conducted in Greek hospitals during 2014–2016 <ce:cross-ref refid="bib1" id="crosref0040">[1]</ce:cross-ref>, we found an extensively drug-resistant (XDR) KPC-producing <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolate that was resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam (minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), 16 mg/L). In this study, we performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to elucidate the resistance mechanisms, as it was the first ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant strain isolated in Greece, long before the introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam in clinical practice in this country (December 2017).</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0050">Material and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">Three clinical isolates were used in this study. <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-90 and KP-128, were isolated from urine specimens of hospitalized patients in Thessaloniki (North Greece, 2014) and Athens (Central Greece, 2015), respectively <ce:cross-ref refid="bib2" id="crosref0045">[2]</ce:cross-ref>. <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-95 was isolated from bronchial secretions of a hospitalized patient in the province of Megaris, West Attica (Central Greece, 2016) and sent to our laboratory for molecular testing due to its XDR phenotype. KP-95 and KP-128 were used in this study as reference KPC-producing ST258 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> strains susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam. Experimental methods are described in Supplementary File S1.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec3" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0055">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-90 was resistant to all antimicrobial agents tested except gentamicin (MIC, 1 mg/L), colistin (MIC, 1 mg/L) and meropenem-vaborbactam (MIC, 4 mg/L) (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1" id="crosref0050">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). The isolate was resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam (MIC, 16 mg/L).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">The reference clinical isolates KP-95 and KP-128 were susceptible to ceftazidime-avibactam (MIC, 2 mg/L), while KP-128 was resistant to meropenem-vaborbactam (MIC, 16 mg/L) (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1" id="crosref0055">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">Based on the results of WGS analysis, <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-90 belonged to clonal lineage ST258 and harboured <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23</ce:inf> as the only carbapenemase gene. KP-128 and KP-95 belonged to ST258 and harboured <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf>, respectively. Other acquired resistance genes justifying the resistance phenotype of the three isolates are depicted in <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1" id="crosref0060">Table 1</ce:cross-ref> and in Supplementary File S2.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">The <ce:italic>in silico</ce:italic> derived capsular type of KP-90 (KPC-23) and KP-128 (KPC-2) was KL106 (wzc: 921, wzi: 29), equivalent to capsule cluster type <ce:italic>cps1.</ce:italic> Capsular type of the KPC-3 producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> KP-95 was KL107 (wzc: 916, wzi: 154), associated with the <ce:italic>cps2</ce:italic> cluster.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">All three isolates harboured a non-functional porin OmpK35. This is inferred by a cytidine insertion at nt124, which results in a translation frameshift that truncates the protein at codon 89. The resulting outer membrane porin loss has been reported to decrease susceptibility to β-lactams <ce:cross-ref refid="bib2" id="crosref0065">[2]</ce:cross-ref> and in combination with a β-lactamase, to confer high-level resistance to β-lactams <ce:cross-ref refid="bib3" id="crosref0070">[3]</ce:cross-ref>. We speculate that the truncated OmpK35 contributed to the higher level of β-lactam resistance in KP-90. OmpK36 proteins of the three isolates matched to the NCBI reference sequence WP_002913005.1), with KP-128, to harbour additionally a duplication of amino acids G134–D135, located at the conserved loop L3 of OmpK36. This insertion contributes to high-level resistance to carbapenems <ce:cross-ref refid="bib4" id="crosref0075">[4]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">In all three isolates, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23,</ce:inf> 
                     <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> were detected on identical Tn<ce:italic>4401a</ce:italic> transposons, located between IS<ce:italic>Kpn7</ce:italic> and IS<ce:italic>Kpn6.</ce:italic> In KP-90 and KP-128, Tn<ce:italic>4401a</ce:italic> was located on a pKPQIL-type plasmid (multi-replicon IncFIIk-FIB plasmid). In KP-95, Tn<ce:italic>4401a</ce:italic> was located on an IncX3 plasmid. No mutations known to interfere with expression of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> were evident within promoter sequences P1 and P2 in any of the three isolates.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">Susceptibility testing of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> TOP10 transformants harbouring <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23</ce:inf> (TOP10/pl90), <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> (TOP10/pl128), and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf> (TOP10/pl95)-carrying plasmids is shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1" id="crosref0080">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>. Ceftazidime-avibactam MICs differed between the three transformants depending on the KPC-variant they produced. TOP10/pl90 harbouring <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-23</ce:inf> exhibited high ceftazidime MIC &gt;1024 mg/L, which was reduced to 8 mg/L (&gt;7-fold reduction) in the presence of avibactam. Transformants TOP10/pl128 and TOP10/pl95 carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-3</ce:inf>, respectively, exhibited lower MICs to ceftazidime (128 and 64 mg/L, respectively), which were reduced to 1 mg/L by avibactam (seven- and six-fold reduction, respectively). On the contrary, in all three transformants, meropenem-vaborbactam MICs were identical (0.06 mg/L), suggesting that resistance of KP-128 was non-transferable and probably the result of porin deficiency as shown in Supplementary File S2.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec4" role="discussion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0060">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">In large surveillance studies, ceftazidime-avibactam consistently demonstrates high <ce:italic>in vitro</ce:italic> activity against non-metallo-β-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (&gt;99% susceptibility) <ce:cross-ref refid="bib5" id="crosref0085">[5]</ce:cross-ref>. Resistance due to KPC mutations has been detected in both spontaneous mutants of KPC-3 and those engineered by site-directed mutagenesis in KPC-2 <ce:cross-ref refid="bib5" id="crosref0090">[5]</ce:cross-ref>. Resistance has also been reported to be driven by porin mutations in combination with enhanced expression of KPC in clinical isolates from patients unexposed to ceftazidime-avibactam <ce:cross-ref refid="bib6" id="crosref0095">[6]</ce:cross-ref>. More importantly, resistant mutants have been described in patients under treatment with ceftazidime-avibactam. Resistance has developed in KPC-3 or KPC-2-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> isolates within 10–19 days of therapy and was associated with mutations in the omega loop of the KPC enzyme (R164 to D179). These mutations led to enhanced ceftazidime hydrolysis that could not be completely inhibited by avibactam <ce:cross-refs refid="bib7 bib8 bib9" id="crosrefs0010">[7–9]</ce:cross-refs>. The most common mutation was a tyrosine for aspartic acid substitution at amino acid position 179 (D179Y) <ce:cross-refs refid="bib9 bib10" id="crosrefs0015">[9,10]</ce:cross-refs>. Substitutions T243M and V240G, outside the omega loop of KPC-3, have been also reported to increase ceftazidime-avibactam MIC compared to wild-type isolates <ce:cross-refs refid="bib7 bib11" id="crosrefs0020">[7,11]</ce:cross-refs>. In rank order, the impact of KPC-3 variants on ceftazidime-avibactam MICs was reported to be as follows: D179Y/T243M &gt; D179 &gt; V240G. Variants D179Y/T243M, and D179Y were reported to cause comparable reduction in meropenem MIC, which was greater than that of variant V240G <ce:cross-ref refid="bib7" id="crosref0100">[7]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">KPC-23 (GenBank accession number: AWU66461.1) variant differs from KPC-3 by one (V240A) and from KPC-2 by two amino acid substitutions (V240A and H274Y) (per Ambler numbering scheme). Mehta et al. have shown that the combination of V240A and H274Y substitutions in natural variants of KPC-2 leads to increased catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime hydrolysis and a 10-fold increase in ceftazidime MIC <ce:cross-ref refid="bib12" id="crosref0105">[12]</ce:cross-ref>. However, the efficiency of avibactam to restore the activity of ceftazidime was not evaluated. In another KPC variant, KPC-8, similar substitutions at the same positions (V240G and H274Y) conferred an 80-fold increase in ceftazidime MIC and resulted in ceftazidime-avibactam MIC of 32 mg/L <ce:cross-ref refid="bib7" id="crosref0110">[7]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">KPC-23 appears to be the complete version of KPC-9 (GenBank accession number: ACM91559.1) (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="https://www.lahey.org/Studies/" id="intref0010" xlink:type="simple">https://www.lahey.org/Studies/</ce:inter-ref>), which was first described in a single case report on a patient in Israel <ce:cross-ref refid="bib13" id="crosref0115">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. Recently, an outbreak of ST258 KPC-9-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> was described in a hospital in Ioannina (Northwestern Greece) <ce:cross-ref refid="bib14" id="crosref0120">[14]</ce:cross-ref>. Presently, we do not have data on the extent of dissemination of KPC-23 variant in Greece as sequencing of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> is rarely carried out in clinical microbiology laboratories other than for research purposes. However, ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in the nationwide surveillance study published by our group <ce:cross-ref refid="bib1" id="crosref0125">[1]</ce:cross-ref> remained very rare (0.4%) until 2016. At the time of writing, there are only two case reports of ceftazidime-avibactam resistant carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) reported from two different countries in Europe, Italy and Germany <ce:cross-refs refid="bib9 bib15" id="crosrefs0025">[9,15]</ce:cross-refs>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">KP-90 was detected at least 2 years before the introduction of ceftazidime-avibactam in Greece. Surveillance data showing the extent of dissemination of KPC-23-producing <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> in Greek hospitals do not currently exist, making difficult to assess the magnitude of the problem. Even if KPC-23 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> was rare at present, there is a risk that increasing use of the drug might exert selective pressure in favor of this and other KPC variant(s) that are resistant to the hydrolytic activity of the combination.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">In conclusion, we characterized a clinical isolate of <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> producing KPC-23 that was resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam. Further biochemical and molecular studies are needed to reveal the basis of ceftazidime-avibactam resistance phenotype, conferred by KPC-23, but as resistant strains appear to pre-exist, routine testing for ceftazidime-avibactam susceptibility emerges as an essential step in making treatment decisions. Clinicians should be alerted to the inevitable dispersion of ceftazidime-avibactam resistant isolates.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
