Abstract 

The search for a specific rRNA methylase motif led to the identification of the new macrolide, lincosamide, and streptogramin B resistance gene erm (43) in Staphylococcus lentus . An inducible resistance phenotype was demonstrated by cloning and expressing erm (43) and its regulatory region in Staphylococcus aureus . The erm (43) gene was detected in two different DNA fragments, of 6,230 bp and 1,559 bp, that were each integrated at the same location in the chromosome in several S. lentus isolates of human, dog, and chicken origin. 

INTRODUCTION 

Staphylococcus lentus is a commensal bacterium colonizing the skin of several animal species. It has commonly been isolated from food-producing animals, including poultry and dairy animals ( 10 , 39 ), and from their food products ( 18 , 23 ). People working with animals have also been reported to be carriers of S. lentus ( 6 , 10 ). In dairy sheep and goats, S. lentus has been associated with subclinical mastitis ( 5 , 16 ), and in rare cases, S. lentus has caused infections in humans ( 11 , 13 , 33 ). 

Like other staphylococci, S. lentus has the ability to acquire antibiotic resistance genes ( 6 , 10 , 12 , 31 , 35 , 39 ), including erythromycin ribosome methylase ( erm ) genes, which confer resistance to macrolide, lincosamide, and streptogramin B (MLS B ) antibiotics ( 9 , 17 , 37 ). Erm methylases add one or two methyl groups to adenine A2058 of the 23S rRNA, hindering the ability of MLS B antibiotics to bind to the rRNA target ( 26 ). Erm methylases are either expressed constitutively or inducible. In cases where the methylase is inducible, the erm transcript contains an intact leader region that is involved in translational attenuation ( 7 ). In the absence of inducer molecules, such as 14- or 15-membered ring macrolides, bacteria remain susceptible to noninducing lincosamides and streptogramin B antibiotics in vitro . 

To date, 34 Erm methylase classes have been described for bacterial species ( 35 ) ( Fig. 1 ). While Erm(A), Erm(B), Erm(C), Erm(F), Erm(G), Erm(Q), Erm(T), Erm(Y), and Erm(33) have been reported for several staphylococci ( 35 ), thus far, S. lentus has been found to contain only the erm (A), erm (B), erm (C), and erm (F) genes ( 2 , 9 , 17 , 21 , 37 ).

In March 2007, one S. lentus strain that displayed an inducible MLS B phenotype but carried none of the described erm genes was isolated from the ear of a healthy 5-year-old dog (Bruno Jura hound). The aim of this study was to identify the nature of this MLS B resistance mechanism and to determine whether it is also present in S. lentus strains of different origins. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Bacterial strains, identification, and growth conditions. 

The origin and characteristics of the Staphylococcus strains used in this study are listed in Table 1 . The strains were identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (microflex LT; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Strains SD952 and ASC0 were additionally identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing as described previously ( 15 ). The Staphylococcus aureus strains RN4220 ( 14 ) and 80CR5 ( 8 ) were used in transformation and conjugation experiments, respectively. Escherichia coli DH5α was used for cloning and plasmid propagation. S. lentus strains were cultivated at 37°C on Trypticase soy agar plates containing 5% sheep blood (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ). S. aureus RN4220 and E. coli DH5α transformants were grown on Lennox broth (LB) agar plates (Becton, Dickinson and Company) containing 10 μg/ml tetracycline for selection of pBUS1 constructs and 20 μg/ml chloramphenicol and 70 μg/ml ampicillin for selection of pRB474 constructs, respectively. These concentrations were also used to maintain plasmids in the cells.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing. 

MICs of erythromycin, clindamycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and pristinamycin IA (Molcan Corporation, Richmond Hill, Ontario, Canada) were determined by broth microdilution ( 3 ). Inducible resistance to clindamycin and pristinamycin IA was measured in the presence of 4 μg/ml erythromycin ( 4 ). 

The presence of antibiotic resistance genes was determined using a custom-made microarray (AMR+ve-2; Alere GmbH, Cologne, Germany) ( 25 ). 

DNA extraction. 

Genomic DNA was isolated using a peqGOLD bacterial DNA kit (Peqlab Biotechnologie GmbH, Erlangen, Germany). Plasmid DNA was extracted using a NucleoBond PC20 plasmid purification kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co., Düren, Germany). The kit lysis buffers were supplemented with 50 μg/ml lysostaphin (Sigma-Aldrich), and the cells were lysed for 20 min at 37°C. 

PCR and sequence analysis. 

Standard PCR was performed using Taq DNA polymerase (Solis BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia). PCR for larger amplicons (>1.5 kb) was performed using the Roche Expand Long Template PCR system (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). DNA amplification was performed for 35 cycles, using the primers and conditions indicated in the text. The erm (43) gene was detected by PCR amplification of a 609-bp fragment by use of primers erm(43)-F (5′-TACAGCAGATGATAACATTG) and erm(43)-R (5′-TGTTGTTTCGATATTTTATTTAAG), an annealing temperature of 50°C, and an extension time of 40 s. To test for erm (43)-containing circular molecules, PCR was performed using primers directed outwards from the erm (43)-containing fragment in SD952, namely, primer erm(43)-R and the SD952 orf-7 -specific primer contig50-F5 (5′-TGATTACTGGAGCCGCTG) (expected product size, 1.8 kb; annealing temperature, 50°C; and extension time, 2 min). 

Whole-genome sequencing of S. lentus SD952 was performed using Roche 454 GS Titanium chemistry and protocols (GS Junior system; Roche). In total, 1 μg of genomic DNA was used for rapid library preparation. The sequencing reads were assembled de novo using newbler 2.6 at the Vital-IT Center for High-Performance Computing at the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics ( http://www.vital-it.ch ). The translated contigs were analyzed for the presence of the rRNA adenine dimethylase signature (PROSITE pattern PS01131 [ http://prosite.expasy.org/ ]) by use of the GenomeNet bioinformatics tool MOTIF ( http://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/ ). 

Based on the obtained 454 sequence data, the region containing the integrated erm (43) fragment in SD952 was also sequenced for the S. lentus strains M155 and ASC0. Sequences were obtained from long-range PCR products by use of an ABI Prism 3100 genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Forster City, CA). Primers contig50-F4 (5′-TGACTAGCCACATCTCCG) and contig50-R6 (5′-ATCCGTTAATTTACCGAGC) were used to amplify a DNA fragment containing erm (43) from strain M155 (2.3 kb) or a DNA fragment containing the integration region from strain ASC0 (0.8 kb) (annealing temperature, 52°C; extension time, 7 min) ( Fig. 2 ). Primers contig50-F13 (5′-GTTTGTTAAAATACTTTATCACC) and contig50-R9 (5′-CTCTGCTAAATGATGTTCTG) were used to amplify flanking DNA of the integration region (3.2 kb), which contains the mfs and sugar transporter genes, from ASC0 (annealing temperature, 51°C; extension time, 3.5 min) ( Fig. 2 ). Primers IS431-R (5′-AATGTATGTCCCTCTGCATC) and erm(43)-R (amplifying 1.9 kb) (annealing temperature, 50°C; extension time, 2 min) and primers contig50-F4 and contig50-R9 (amplifying 3.5 kb) (annealing temperature, 51°C; extension time, 3.5 min) were used to amplify both the 5′ and 3′ regions of the integration site in M155 ( Fig. 2 ). In M155, the IS 431 sequence was first detected by sequencing a 0.7-kb PCR product that was obtained with primers specific to DNA upstream of the integration region in SD952 (contig50-F12 [5′-TCACCTTTCTGCATCAAC] and contig50-R8 [5′-CAAATTATGGATACATCTCAG]) at a low annealing temperature (45°C).

Cloning. 

The erm (43) gene was cloned into the shuttle vectors pBUS1 ( 28 ) and pRB474 ( 1 ) as follows. One fragment containing the erm (43) gene and a 294-bp upstream region was amplified by PCR from S. lentus SD952 DNA by use of the forward primer erm(43)-SalI-F1 (5′-cat gtcgac TAAATGAGGGTTAATATCCAG) and the reverse primer erm(43)-XbaI-R (5′-cat tctagA TGAATTTAACGAAGGTGATTC) and then cloned into pBUS1, generating the plasmid pBSSC1. A second fragment, containing the erm (43) gene and a 63-bp upstream region, was cloned into both the pBUS1 and pRB474 vectors after PCR amplification using the forward primer erm(43)-SalI-F2 (5′-cat gtcgac TAAATTACTGGTCATGATGAAC) and the reverse primers erm(43)-XbaI-R and erm(43)-EcoRI-R (5′-cat gaattc ATGAATTTAACGAAGGTGATTC), generating the plasmids pBSSC2 and pRSSC2, respectively. The primers were supplemented with linkers (lowercase letters in sequences) containing a restriction site (underlined) to facilitate cloning into the vectors. PCR for cloning was performed with Pfu DNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI) at an annealing temperature of 51°C and with an extension time of 2 min 15 s. 

DNA-DNA hybridization. 

Southern blot analysis of genomic and plasmid DNAs was performed using a standard protocol for alkaline transfer ( 29 ). An erm (43)-specific probe was synthesized by incorporation of digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled dUTP (Roche) into PCR products by use of primers erm(43)-F and erm(43)-R, with SD952 DNA as the template. Hybridized probes were detected on the membrane by use of anti-DIG-AP Fab fragments and the CDP-Star chemiluminescence substrate (Roche). 

Conjugation and electrotransformation. 

As described previously, conjugation was performed by filter mating, using S. lentus SD952 as the donor strain and S. aureus 80CR5 (Fus r Rif r ) as the recipient strain ( 24 ). Selection occurred on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar containing 100 μg/ml rifampin, 25 μg/ml fusidic acid, and 20 μg/ml erythromycin. In addition, transferability of erm (43) was tested by electroporation of SD952 genomic DNA into RN4220 and selection on NYE agar (1% casein hydrolysate, 0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% sodium chloride) containing 20 μg/ml erythromycin. Electrotransformation of S. aureus RN4220 was performed as previously described ( 30 ). The same protocol was used to transform RN4220 cells with pBUS1 and pRB474 constructs, with the exception that NYE agar containing 10 μg/ml tetracycline and 20 μg/ml chloramphenicol, respectively, was used for selection. 

Nucleotide sequence accession numbers. 

The nucleotide sequences of erm (43) and/or its flanking regions have been deposited in the EMBL database under the following accession numbers: HE650138 for S. lentus SD952, HE775264 for S. lentus M155, and HE775265 for S. lentus ASC0. 

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Identification of erm (43). 

The S. lentus strain SD952, which displayed constitutive resistance to erythromycin and inducible resistance to clindamycin, did not contain any of the erm genes previously described for staphylococci, as determined by microarray analyses ( Table 1 ). No transfer of macrolide resistance occurred either by conjugation to S. aureus 80CR5 or by electroporation of genomic DNA into S. aureus RN4220. To identify the new mechanism of resistance, the entire genome of S. lentus SD952 was sequenced using 454 sequencing. The obtained reads (total number of reads, 129,297; average length, 430 bp) were assembled de novo into 85 contigs, with an N 50 (length-weighted median) of 90,586 bp, a mean contig size of 33,165 bp, a maximum contig length of 165,612 bp, and a contig sum of 2,819,025 bp. The translated contigs were analyzed for the presence of the rRNA adenine dimethylase signature PROSITE pattern PS01131. This motif is present in all described MLS B methylases except for Erm(I), Erm(N), Erm(Z), Erm(32), Erm(37), and Erm(41) ( Fig. 3 ). Two PS01131 amino acid (aa) motifs were detected in the translated contig sequences of SD952, with one associated with a bacterial 16S rRNA dimethylase KsgA-like protein ( 22 ) and the other associated with a 23S rRNA methylase. This 23S rRNA methylase shared <61% aa identity and <72% DNA identity with other acquired Erm proteins ( Fig. 1 ). The gene encoding this new methylase was named erm (43) according to the nomenclature for MLS B genes ( http://faculty.washington.edu/marilynr/ ) ( 27 ).

The pattern is defined as follows: [LIVMAC]-[LIVMFYWT]-[DE]-X-G-[STAPVLCG]-G-X-[GAS]-X-[LIVMF]-[ST]-X(2,3)-[LIVMA]-X(5,8)-[LIVMYF]-X-[STAGVLC]-[LIVMFYHCS]-E-X-D, with aa acceptable for one given position listed in square brackets and “X” indicating any aa with the possible repetition range given in parentheses. In the figure, invariant aa are shaded black, highly conserved aa are shown in dark gray, and less-conserved positions are shown in light gray. Threonine (T) at the first position and histidine (H) at the last position could be included in the PS01131 signature to recognize most Erm methylases. For Erm(41), the sequence published by Nash et al. ( 20 ) was included. Erm(32) is listed even though it does not contain a complete PS01131 signature. 

Characterization and expression of erm (43). 

The erm (43) gene encodes a 243-aa protein and is preceded by sequences encoding two leader peptides, of 17 aa (lp1) and 22 aa (lp2) ( Fig. 2 ), similar to the regulatory region of erm (A) in Tn 554 ( 19 ). The lp2 peptide contains the aa motif (FS)IFVI, which has a key role in the induction of erm (C) ( 36 ). In addition, the regulatory sequence of erm (43) contains two pairs of inverted repeats (IR) (IR1 [5′-AAAGTTCATTAT] plus IR2 [5′-ATGATGAACTTT] and IR3 [5′-TTCATTATGATTC] plus IR4 [5′-GAATATAATGAA]) that are capable of forming stem-loop structures similar to those described for the translational attenuator sequences upstream of inducible erm (C) genes ( 38 ). The putative translational attenuator sequence upstream of erm (43) shows 80% identity in a 204-bp overlap (204 nt/207 nt) to erm (A) in Tn 554 (GenBank accession no. X03216 ) and 73% identity in a 133-bp overlap (204 nt/133 nt) to erm (C) in pT48 (GenBank accession no. NC_001395 ). Putative −10 and −35 promoter sequences exist 223 bp and 245 bp, respectively, upstream of the erm (43) start codon. 

To study its expression, we cloned the erm (43) gene with and without its putative promoter sequences into the shuttle vector pBUS1, which lacks an expression promoter, or pRB474, which allows expression of the cloned genes under the control of the veg II promoter. Plasmid pBSSC1 contained the erm (43) gene and a 294-bp upstream region including the putative promoter sequences. Plasmids pBSSC2 and pRSSC2 contained the erm (43) gene and a 63-bp upstream region lacking promoter sequences, leader peptides, and IR1. The plasmids were electroporated into S. aureus RN4220, and transformants were tested for erythromycin, clindamycin, and pristinamycin IA resistance as well as for inducible resistance to clindamycin and pristinamycin IA. In S. aureus RN4220, pBSSC1 conferred a >9-fold increase in resistance to erythromycin; resistance to clindamycin (>9-fold) and pristinamycin IA (2-fold) also increased after induction with erythromycin ( Table 1 ). No phenotypic expression was observed with pBSSC2, which contained erm (43) lacking the promoter region ( Table 1 ). However, when the same erm (43)-containing fragment was placed downstream of the veg II promoter in plasmid pRSSC2, constitutive resistance to erythromycin (>9-fold increase in resistance), clindamycin (>10-fold increase), and pristinamycin IA (>6-fold increase) was observed ( Table 1 ). Untransformed RN4220 cells and RN4220 cells harboring empty vectors remained susceptible to these antibiotics ( Table 1 ). 

Distribution of erm (43) in S. lentus strains. 

The erm (43) gene was detected in six additional MLS B -resistant S. lentus strains, originating from chickens ( S. lentus strains G38, G45, G56, and G85) and humans (strains M155 and M163), by use of PCR with the primers erm(43)-F and erm(43)-R, and was absent in MLS B -susceptible strains (ASC0, G64, and M157) ( Table 1 ). The erm (43)-containing isolates displayed resistance to erythromycin as well as inducible resistance to clindamycin and did not contain any other erm genes described so far for staphylococci, as determined by microarray analyses ( Table 1 ). PCR analysis using the primers contig50-F4 and contig50-R6, which annealed to DNA regions situated directly upstream and downstream of the putative erm (43)-containing fragment in SD952, revealed the presence of a 2.3-kb fragment for erm (43)-positive strains from chickens and humans and a 7-kb amplicon for the SD952 strain from a dog ( Fig. 2 ). These results indicated that erm (43) was present on two different DNA fragments that were integrated into the same genomic region. In contrast, a 0.8-kb fragment was amplified from MLS B -susceptible strains ASC0, G64, and M157 by use of the same primers. The erm (43) gene was also detected in the S. lentus collection strains CCM2598, CCM2599, and CCUG15599. 

Localization of erm (43) on the S. lentus chromosome and characterization of the flanking region. 

The erm (43)-containing fragment and its flanking regions in SD952 were compared to those of the S. lentus strain M155, containing erm (43), and the MLS B -susceptible strain ASC0, lacking erm (43) ( Fig. 2 ). Sequence analysis showed that the erm (43)-containing fragments of SD952 and M155 integrated into a unique site between a putative major facilitator superfamily gene ( mfs ) and a putative sugar transporter gene in the genome of S. lentus ( Fig. 2 ). In SD952, the erm (43) gene was located on a 6,230-bp fragment that was flanked by 135- or 134-bp imperfect direct repeat (DR) sequences that differed from each other by seven mismatches. The 5′ DR was part of the chromosome, as it was also present in M155 and ASC0, and the 3′ DR appeared to belong to the erm (43)-containing integrated sequence ( Fig. 2 ). This sequence duplication, though found only in SD952, emphasizes that the chromosomal DR sequence may play a role as an attachment site. In addition to erm (43), the integrated fragment in SD952 contained four other open reading frames ( orf-4 to orf-7 ), which coded for a putative dihydrodipicolinate synthase ( orf-4 ), a putative IclR family transcriptional regulator ( orf-5 ), a putative mandelate racemase/muconate lactonizing protein ( orf-6 ), and a putative MFS transporter ( orf-7 ). In M155, the integrated fragment had a length of 1,559 bp and contained the erm (43) gene followed by a 480-bp noncoding region absent in the SD952 sequence ( Fig. 2 ). The sequence of the same region in ASC0 contained only the potential attachment sequence (DR) between the putative mfs and sugar transporter genes ( Fig. 2 ). In strain M155, the putative mfs gene was found to be truncated by the insertion element IS 431 ( Fig. 2 ). The location of erm (43) on the chromosome of S. lentus was demonstrated by DNA hybridization using probes for erm (43) (data not shown). There was no evidence that the erm (43)-containing fragment in SD952 was capable of autonomous translocation. Transfer of macrolide resistance was not observed by DNA transformation or by conjugation using SD952 cells as the donor. Furthermore, no circular forms of erm (43)-containing fragments were detected by PCR. 

Although the mode of acquisition of erm (43) remains unknown, the identification of erm (43) in S. lentus strains from different sources, including methicillin-resistant isolates from chickens and humans ( Table 1 ), indicates that erm (43) is widely distributed in different environments. Erythromycin resistance has frequently been observed among bacteria from the S. sciuri group ( S. sciuri , S. lentus , and S. vitulinus ), but the mechanism of resistance frequently remains unknown ( 32 , 34 ), suggesting that erm (43) may also be widespread in these bacteria. 

Searching for aa motifs involved in enzymatic functions is one approach to detect related proteins with low overall sequence identity. The new Erm(43) protein was identified by such a bioinformatics search for a specific methylase motif encoded in the translated SD952 draft genome. The erm (43) gene is another example that illustrates the presence of novel antibiotic resistance determinants in Staphylococcus species with zoonotic potential.
