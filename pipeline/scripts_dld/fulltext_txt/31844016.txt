KEYWORDS: Cutibacterium acnes , plasmid, multidrug resistance, erm (50), tet (W) 

ABSTRACT 

Antimicrobial-resistant Cutibacterium acnes strains have emerged and disseminated throughout the world. The 23S rRNA mutation and erm (X) gene are known as the major resistance determinants of macrolides and clindamycin in C. acnes . We isolated eight high-level macrolide-clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains with no known resistance determinants, such as 23S rRNA mutation and erm (X), from different acne patients in 2008 between 2013 and 2015. The aim of this study was to identify the novel mechanisms of resistance in C. acnes . Whole-genome sequencing revealed the existence of a plasmid DNA, denoted pTZC1 (length, 31,440 bp), carrying the novel macrolide-clindamycin resistance gene erm (50) and tetracycline resistance gene tet (W). pTZC1 was detected in all C. acnes isolates (eight strains) exhibiting high-level macrolide-clindamycin resistance, with no known resistance determinants (MIC of clarithromycin, ≥256 μg/ml; clindamycin, ≥256 μg/ml). Transconjugation experiments demonstrated that the pTZC1 was horizontally transferred among C. acnes strains and conferred resistance to macrolides, clindamycin, and tetracyclines. Our data showed, for the first time, the existence of a transferable multidrug-resistant plasmid in C. acnes . Increased prevalence of this plasmid will be a great threat to antimicrobial therapy for acne vulgaris. 

INTRODUCTION 

Cutibacterium acnes , formerly Propionibacterium acnes , is one of the inhabitants of human skin and is also known as an exacerbating factor of acne vulgaris. Additionally, C. acnes is involved in the development of surgical site infections in ophthalmology, orthopedics, plastic surgery, prostate cancer, and sarcoidosis ( 1 – 5 ). Antimicrobial agents against C. acnes , including topical clindamycin, oral macrolides, and tetracyclines ( 6 ), have been widely used for the treatment of acne vulgaris. Recently, macrolide-clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains have significantly increased in Japan from 20.3% in 2009 to 2010 to 44.1% in 2016 to 2017 ( 7 ). The mechanisms of resistance to macrolide-clindamycin in C. acnes are through 23S rRNA mutations and methylation of 23S rRNA by a ribosomal methylase gene erm (X) ( 8 , 9 ). The macrolide-clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains with 23S rRNA mutations, the most predominant group of the resistant strains, emerge by antimicrobial exposure ( 10 ). The resistant strains with a 23S rRNA mutation occur individually, typically in acne patients who have a history of antimicrobial use ( 11 , 12 ). In contrast, erm (X) is located on the transposon Tn 5432 and can be horizontally transferred among C. acnes strains ( 7 ). 

We isolated eight high-level macrolide-clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains, having no known resistance determinants, from different acne patients in 2008 and between 2013 and 2015 (MIC of clarithromycin, ≥256 μg/ml; clindamycin, ≥256 μg/ml) ( 12 , 13 ). Here, we aimed to identify the unknown mechanisms of resistance in C. acnes for these eight strains obtained in 2008 and 2013 to 2015. 

RESULTS 

Whole-genome sequencing of high-level macrolide-clindamycin-resistant strain. 

To identify the unknown mechanisms of resistance in C. acnes , we carried out whole-genome sequencing of C. acnes TP-CU389. Whole-genome data revealed that the length of chromosomal DNA was 2,494,387 bp, and the G+C content was 60.0% (GenBank accession no. AP019664 ). Genetic inversions and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were detected in the chromosomal sequence of TP-CU389 compared with that of isolate P.acn33 (GenBank accession no. CP003195 ), which is an identical single-locus sequence typing (SLST) (F1) as the TP-CU389 strain. SNPs were found with 857 bases in the untranslated region but not in open reading frames (ORFs), such as rRNA methylase and 23S rRNA. A novel circular plasmid, named pTZC1, was identified, and its length and G+C content were 31,440 bp and 64.9%, respectively (GenBank accession no. LC473083 ). The plasmid had probable type IV secretion system proteins, namely, VirB4 (TraE), VirB3 (PrgI), VirB6, ViD4 (TraG), and VirD2 (TraI), which are associated with conjugative transfer ( Table 1 ). The data suggest that pTZC1 is potentially a transferable plasmid. Additionally, BLAST analysis showed two antimicrobial resistance genes on pTZC1. One was a tetracycline resistance gene, namely, tet (W), which had an identical gene sequence to tet (W) of Bifidobacterium longum (GenBank accession no. EU434756 ) and Streptococcus suis (GenBank accession no. FN396364 ), with 100% (1,920/1,920 bp) and 98.6% (1,900/1,920 bp) sequence identity, respectively. Tet(W) confers tetracycline resistance by ribosomal protection, similar to Tet(M) and Tet(O) ( 14 ). The other was assumed to be an erm family gene, showing 92.3% (731/792) and 98.9% (260/263) nucleotide and amino acid identities, respectively, to the 23S rRNA adenine N-6-methyltransferase on the chromosome of Corynebacterium glutamicum CS176 (GenBank accession no. BAYH01000055 ). Comparative analysis of the amino acid sequence encoded by the erm gene family showed that it had 85.4% similarity and 55.7% identity of that encoded by erm (39) ( Fig. 1 ). The novel erm family gene member was denoted erm (50).

Molecular epidemiological analysis of C. acnes strains carrying pTZC1. 

Eight C. acnes isolates having pTZC1 exhibited diverse MICs of tetracyclines (MICs of tetracycline, 1 to 32 μg/ml), although they exhibited high-level macrolide and clindamycin resistance (MICs of clarithromycin, ≥256 μg/ml; clindamycin, ≥256 μg/ml) ( Fig. 2 ). The base sequences of erm (50) and tet (W) were identical in all strains. To confirm the identification of the strains carrying pTZC1, an analysis of pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), SLST, and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) were performed. Seven strains carrying pTZC1, which were isolated between 2013 and 2015, exhibited a high similarity of >80% ( Fig. 2 ). Furthermore, all of them belonged to clade F, according to SLST analysis. MLST analysis showed that the clade F1 strains were classified into sequence type 28 (ST28). The clade F4 strains were classified into ST67 or ST70, which are single- and double-locus variants of ST28, respectively. In contrast, molecular epidemiological features of TP-CU73 isolated in 2008 differed from the other strains.

Transconjugation of pTZC1 among C. acnes strains. 

The sequence data of pTZC1 strongly suggested that the plasmid has the potential to be horizontally transferred due to the existence of the type IV secretion system proteins, which are associated with conjugative transfer. When the transconjugation was conducted using the eight C. acnes strains having pTZC1 as a donor, transconjugants were obtained under all donors used ( Table 2 ). pTZC1 was transferred horizontally from eight C. acnes donors to ATCC 11828, with frequencies between 5.3 × 10 −6 to 3.0 × 10 −5 . All transconjugants exhibited high-level resistance to macrolide-clindamycin (≥256 μg/ml). In contrast, their tetracycline MICs differed according to the donor except for the strains having other resistance determinants, such as TP-CU73 having 16S rRNA and ribosomal S10 protein mutation and TP-CU426 and TP-CU437 having ribosomal S10 protein mutation.

DISCUSSION 

The erm (X) gene, which is known as an exogenous resistance determinant in C. acnes , is located on the transposon Tn 5432 ( 8 ). We previously demonstrated that Tn 5432 carrying erm (X) transfers among C. acnes strains ( 7 ). In this study, we discovered a novel erm gene, erm (50), in C. acnes strains exhibiting high-level macrolide-clindamycin resistance that had unknown resistance determinants. Additionally, we identified the probable vir operon gene cluster encoding the type IV secretion system on pTZC1. The vir operon associated with the conjugative transfer of pTZC1 was consistent with a small number of genes similar to the type IV secretion system gene cluster of Streptococcus suis ( 15 ). Although we confirmed the conjugative transfer of pTZC1, the functions of each vir gene remain unclear. Further studies are necessary to fully understand the mechanisms of transfer of pTZC1 in C. acnes . 

All strains carrying pTZC1 had a different susceptibility to tetracycline (MIC, 1 to 32 μg/ml), although they exhibited high-level resistance to macrolides and clindamycin (≥256 μg/ml). The resistance level of tetracyclines in Streptococcus pneumoniae strains carrying tet (M), which confers resistance to tetracyclines by ribosomal protection, as well as tet (W), differed by mutations in the promoter sites ( 16 ). However, in this study, no mutations were found in the 500 bp upstream of tet (W) (data not shown). Therefore, we need to analyze the complete sequence of pTZC1 in the other C. acnes isolates because pTZC1 has a possibility to differ in C. acnes strains. Until now, the decreased susceptibility of C. acnes to tetracyclines was caused by the acquisition of mutations in 16S rRNA and the S10 ribosomal protein, whereas an extrachromosomal resistance determinant has not been reported in C. acnes ( 17 , 18 ). Our findings strongly suggest that dissemination of the strains carrying pTZC1 will cause increased prevalence of C. acnes strains with decreased susceptibility to tetracyclines in the future. 

Clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains were detected with 18.6% prevalence at a clinic in 2008 ( 13 ). C. acnes carrying erm (X) or erm (50) were detected only once each (2.3%). In hospital outpatients, clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains were detected with 18.8% and 39.1% prevalence in 2010 and between 2013 and 2015, respectively ( 11 , 12 ). No difference was found in the prevalence of the macrolide-clindamycin-resistant strains with 23S rRNA mutations between 2010 and 2013–2015, while the strains carrying erm (X) increased 6-fold from 2010 to 2013–2015. Furthermore, the strains carrying erm (50), which were not detected in 2010, emerged between 2013 and 2015 and accounted for 10.1%. These data indicate that an increased prevalence of clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains was due to the acquisition of exogenous resistance determinants. These data indicate that an increased prevalence of clindamycin-resistant C. acnes strains is owing to the acquisition of exogenous resistance determinants. Additionally, our data suggest that a C. acnes strain carrying pTZC1 had already emerged in 2008 and increased in 2013. The phylogenetic analysis showed that an increased prevalence of the strains belonging to clade F in acne patients might be strongly related to the incidence of the strains carrying pTZC1. On the other hand, pTZC1 was also found in the strain with another genotype (clade C1). The data indicate that the plasmid pTZC1 might be able to transfer between various C. acnes strains. It is considered that Tn 5432 containing erm (X) in C. acnes originated from Corynebacterium striatum ( 8 ). To prevent the emergence of multidrug-resistant C. acnes strains, we should monitor the existence of the strains carrying pTZC1 not only in Japan but also in other countries. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Bacterial strains and plasmids. 

C. acnes strains isolated from patients with acne vulgaris in 2008 (one strain) and between 2013 and 2015 (seven strains) were used ( 12 , 13 ). These strains exhibited high-level macrolide-clindamycin resistance and had no known resistance determinants, such as 23S rRNA mutation and erm (X). Antimicrobial susceptible C. acnes ATCC 11828 (MIC of clarithromycin, ≤0.06 μg/ml; clindamycin, ≤0.06 μg/ml), which has a known whole-genome sequence and no macrolide-clindamycin resistance determinants, was used as the recipient strain in the transconjugation experiment. It was conferred rifampin resistance via chromosomal mutation for the selection of transconjugants. The C. acnes strain was spread onto modified Gifu anaerobic medium (modified GAM) agar (Nissui Pharmaceutical Co., Tokyo, Japan) plate containing 5 μg/ml rifampin (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan), after growth in modified GAM broth (Nissui Pharmaceutical Co.) under anaerobic conditions. After incubation for 5 days, the colonies were selected as rifampin-resistant mutants. These mutants displayed resistance to 50 μg/ml of rifampin ( 19 , 20 ). C. acnes isolates having pTZC1 without 23S rRNA mutation or erm (X) were used as donors. For antimicrobial susceptibility testing, C. acnes ATCC 11828, an antimicrobial susceptible strain, and ATCC 6919, a type strain, were used as quality controls ( 12 , 21 , 22 ). 

Genome sequence. 

Whole-genome sequencing of the C. acnes TP-CU389 (T-20b) was performed using the PacBio RS II platform (Pacific Biosciences Inc., California, US). Genomic DNA was extracted according to the following method. The C. acnes strain was grown for 48 h in modified GAM broth (Nissui Pharmaceutical Co., Tokyo, Japan) under anaerobic conditions before being centrifuged to collect the bacterial cells. The bacterial cells were suspended in Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer (pH 7.0), containing 10 mg/ml lysozyme (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) and 5 mg/ml achromopeptidase (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical), and were incubated for 60 min at 37°C. The reaction solution was treated with phenol-chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) to obtain purified genomic DNA. The sequences derived were annotated using a BASys program ( https://www.basys.ca/ ). Amino acid and nucleotide sequences were compared with those in the sequence databases using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), which is hosted by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ). 

Detection of pTZC1. 

The erm (50), tet (W), and traE were amplified using the GoTaq green master mix (Promega Co., Wisconsin, USA). In addition to the detection of erm (50) and tet (W), to have an overview of pTZC1, traE was selected on the opposite side of erm (50) and tet (W) in sequence with pTZC1. The primers are erm (50)-F (5′-TCAATGAGGCGACGATCAGAC-3′) and erm (50)-R (5′-GGTGAACACGTCATGGACGA-3′), tet (W)-F (5′-CTGCCCGTTGGTGTAAAAGG-3′) and tet (W)-R (5′-ATGCCCTGCGGAAAATGAACA-3′), and traE -F (5′-CCATTTTCCCGAACACGCTC-3′) and traE -R (5′-GCGGAGCTCGAGATGCAGA-3′). For each gene, the PCR conditions were as follows: erm (50), 94°C for 30 s, 59°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s (30 cycles); tet (W), 94°C for 30 s, 57°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 40 s (30 cycles); and traE , 94°C for 30 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 40 s (30 cycles). The PCR products were verified using DNA sequence analysis ( 12 ). 

Molecular epidemiological analysis. 

The phylogenetic typing of C. acnes was carried out using pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), single-locus sequence typing (SLST), and multilocus sequence typing (MLST). PFGE analysis was performed using the restriction enzyme SpeI, according to the method of Oprica et al. ( 11 , 23 ). The SLST and MLST were determined using the database of SLST for Propionibacterium acnes ( http://medbac.dk/slst/pacnes ) ( 24 ) and MLST ( http://pacnes.mlst.net/ ) ( 25 ), respectively. 

Transconjugation experiment. 

Plasmid transconjugation between C. acnes strains was performed using the filter mating method ( 7 ). In brief, C. acnes strains were grown for 3 days in modified GAM broth at 35°C under anaerobic conditions before the culture was incubated until an optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) of 0.2. Each bacterial suspension of donor and recipient strains was mixed in a 4:1 bacterial population ratio, and the culture was filtered through nitrocellulose membranes (diameter, 13 mm; pore size, 0.45 μm; Advantec, Tokyo, Japan). The membrane was applied to modified GAM agar (Nissui Pharmaceutical) and incubated for 5 days. Bacteria on the membrane were suspended in broth and spread onto agar plates containing 5 μg/ml of clarithromycin (Tokyo Chemical Industries, Tokyo, Japan) and 50 μg/ml of rifampin. After incubation for 5 days, the growing colonies were selected as transconjugants. Plasmid acquisition in the transconjugants was confirmed by the determination of SLST and detection of the erm (50), tet (W), and traE genes. 

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing. 

Antimicrobial susceptibilities were evaluated by determining the MICs. The MICs were determined by the agar doubling-dilution method, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines and our previous studies ( 12 , 21 , 22 ). The following antimicrobial agents were used: clarithromycin, clindamycin hydrochloride, minocycline hydrochloride (Tokyo Chemical Industries), roxithromycin, tetracycline (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical), and doxycycline hyclate (Sigma-Aldrich Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The following breakpoints of antimicrobials were defined by our laboratory ( 12 ): 2 μg/ml, erythromycin; 2 μg/ml, clarithromycin; 2 μg/ml, roxithromycin; 8 μg/ml, clindamycin; 16 μg/ml, doxycycline; and 16 μg/ml, minocycline. 

Data availability. 

The nucleotide sequences of C. acnes TP-CU389 chromosome and plasmid pTZC1 are available under the GenBank accession numbers AP019664 and LC473083 , respectively.
