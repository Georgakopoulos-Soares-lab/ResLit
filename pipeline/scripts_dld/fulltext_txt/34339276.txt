LETTER 

The emergence and transmission of metallo-β-lactamases (MBL) are significant public health concerns that threaten the utility of antimicrobial therapy ( 1 , 2 ). Seoul imipenemase (SIM)-type MBL genes, bla SIM-1 and bla SIM-2 , have been identified in Acinetobacter spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae , and Pseudomonas aeruginosa from South Korea, China, and Egypt ( 3 – 11 ) but not from the United States. Here, we describe the identification of a bla SIM-1 -carrying P. aeruginosa , isolated from a patient in Rhode Island (RI) in 2020 with a complicated medical history (see the supplemental material), including chronic lower extremity ulcers and wounds and a left trans-metatarsal amputation, but no recent travel outside the United States. The detection of this rare MBL gene was possible through activities of the Antibiotic Resistance Laboratory Network (AR Lab Network) established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) ( 12 ). 

An isolate of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa was provided to the RI State Health Laboratory (RISHL) by a hospital laboratory for confirmation and characterization (see the supplemental material) per RI regulations for carbapenem-resistant bacteria (216-RICR-30-05-1). The isolate was subsequently forwarded to the Wadsworth Center (WC) for further characterization, as described previously ( 13 ). The isolate was tested for carbapenemase production using the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) ( 14 ) and MBL production using metallo-beta-lactamase Neo-Sensitabs (Rosco Diagnostics, Taastrup, Denmark), both of which were positive. Multiplex real-time PCR assays for bla KPC , bla NDM , bla VIM , bla IMP , and bla OXA-48-like genes (see the supplemental material) were negative. 

Reference antibiotic susceptibility testing was completed at the CDC using in-house-developed frozen broth microdilution panels according to CLSI guidance ( 14 ) (see the supplemental material). The isolate was resistant to imipenem and meropenem and remarkably not susceptible to multiple other antibiotics across all classes tested ( Table 1 ).

For whole-genome sequencing (WGS), genomic DNA was extracted on a QIAcube-HT (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq. An in-house-developed bioinformatics pipeline was used to assign multilocus sequence type (MLST) and identify antibiotic resistance (AR) genes, as described previously ( 13 ). The isolate belonged to sequence type (ST) 167, and the bla SIM-1 gene was detected at 100% coverage and 99.87% identity, differing from GenBank AY887066.1 by a synonymous A-to-G mutation at nucleotide position 687. As the protein sequence was identical, this allele is still characterized as bla SIM-1 ( 15 ). 

Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT, Oxford, UK) MinION sequencing was completed to characterize the genomic context of bla SIM-1 . Genomic DNA was extracted using the Nanobind CBB Big DNA kit (Circulomics, Baltimore, MD, USA) and sheared in Covaris G tubes, followed by multiplexing (1D Native barcoding kit EXP-NBD104, EXP-NBD114) and ligation and sequencing (kit SQK-LSK109) on a SpotON flow cell R9.4.1 FLO-MIN106. Basecalling was done using guppy v4.2.3 in fast mode on a MinIT (ONT), and demultiplexing was done using qcat v1.0.1. 

Full genome assembly methods are described in the supplemental material. The final assembly was completed using Flye v2.8.2 ( 16 ), followed by polishing with medaka v1.0.3 (long reads) ( 17 ) and Pilon v1.23 (short reads) ( 18 ). The assembly was annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 ( 19 ), AMRFinderPlus v3.9.3 ( 15 ), and IntegronFinder v2.0rc6 ( 20 ). Plasmids were characterized using MOB-typer v3.0.0 ( 21 ) and by querying against the PLSDB v2020_11_19 ( 22 ) using mash dist v2.2 ( 23 ). 

The assembly consisted of a circular chromosome and two circular plasmids, pPae-WC20-001-01 (159,998 bp) and pPae-WC20-001-02 (153,270 bp), which share a 115-kb region but are distinct in the first 45 kb of pPae-WC20-001-01 and 38 kb of pPae-WC20-001-02, which encode mostly hypothetical proteins ( Fig. 1 ). Both plasmids carry a complete class 1 integron with four attC sites and four AR gene cassettes containing (i) bla SIM-1 , (ii) aac(6’)-Ib 4 , (iii) a fused gene cassette containing bla OXA-10 and aadA1 , and (iv) dfrA1 , in addition to sul1 in the 5′ conserved segment. The integron is distinct from previously described bla SIM -carrying integrons (see Fig. S4 in the supplemental material). Four additional AR genes were carried on both plasmids, and eight were carried on the chromosome ( Table 2 ).

All genes were identified at 100% coverage and >99% identity. All chromosomal AR genes, including the OXA-50 family gene bla OXA-494 and the AmpC variant bla PDC-3 , are known to be intrinsic to P. aeruginosa . 

Plasmid replicon genes were not identified in either plasmid. pPae-WC20-001-01 was assigned to relaxase type MOB H and was classified as mobilizable, while pPae-WC20-001-02 was not assigned to a relaxase type and was classified as nonmobilizable. Neither plasmid shared significant identity with other available sequenced bla SIM -carrying plasmids, but both shared regions carrying plasmid replication, integration, transposition, and metal resistance genes with previously described Pseudomonas plasmids ( Fig. 1 ). 

The presence of two bla SIM-1 -carrying plasmids in the isolate was supported by both Flye and inspection of long-read mapping to the assembly (see the supplemental material). Whether both plasmids were in the same bacterial cell is unclear, but their presence in a single P. aeruginosa isolate exemplifies the dynamic nature of plasmid populations ( 24 , 25 ) and the need for careful assessment of long-read genome assemblies. 

This report represents the first identification and genomic characterization of bla SIM in the United States and the first complete sequences of bla SIM-1 -carrying plasmids from P. aeruginosa . As the patient in this investigation did not travel, it is possible that bla SIM genes are more widely distributed in the United States than is currently understood. This is not surprising, as available molecular test methods do not generally include this target. 

WGS is an essential method for characterizing AR bacteria that are carbapenemase producing but PCR negative for targeted AR genes and identifying uncommon or emerging AR genes that can be targeted by future assays ( 12 , 26 – 28 ). Continued surveillance through the AR Lab Network and similar efforts worldwide is needed to prevent further dissemination of these public health threats. 

Data availability. 

Illumina and MinION sequences are available in the NCBI sequence read archive (SRA), and the final genome assembly is available on GenBank (BioSample SAMN16982406 ).
