                  Bacteria able to grow in aqueous:organic two-phase systems have evolved resistance mechanisms to the toxic effects of solvents. One such mechanism is the active efflux of solvents from the cell, preserving the integrity of the cell interior.Pseudomonas putida S12 is resistant to a wide variety of normally detrimental solvents due to the action of such an efflux pump. The genes for this solvent efflux pump were cloned from P. putida S12 and their nucleotide sequence determined. The deduced amino acid sequences encoded by the three genes involved show a striking resemblance to proteins known to be involved in proton-dependent multidrug efflux systems. Transfer of the genes for the solvent efflux pump to solvent-sensitive P. putida strains results in the acquisition of solvent resistance. This opens up the possibilities of using the solvent efflux system to construct bacterial strains capable of performing biocatalytic transformations of insoluble substrates in two-phase aqueous:organic medium.

                     <ce:bold>Schematic representation of essential features of solvent-resistant bacteria.</ce:bold> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>, solvents diffuse to and preferentially partition to the cytoplasmic membrane where they cause disruptions in membrane functions by increasing membrane fluidity and affecting bilayer stability (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref10" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref20" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>B,</ce:italic> to compensate for these effects, solvent-resistant bacteria modify the composition of the membrane either by isomerizing <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>- into<ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-unsaturated fatty acids from the membrane lipids (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref30" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="cecref40" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>) or by changing the headgroup composition (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref50" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref60" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>). This compensation will only be partial and thus in a dynamic process, solvents have to be removed continuously from the membrane. <ce:italic>C,</ce:italic> removal to a certain extent may be by degradation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref70" refid="bib32">32</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>D</ce:italic>, very recently, on the basis of whole cell experiments, evidence was obtained that an unprecedented energy-dependent export system for hydrophobic solvents is in operation (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref80" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>E</ce:italic>, in combination with a retarded influx of solvents due to modifications at the outer membrane (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref90" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref100" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>), the new efflux pump functions as the key factor in solvent tolerance.</ce:simple-para>

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara100" view="all">Bacteria able to grow in aqueous:organic two-phase systems have evolved resistance mechanisms to the toxic effects of solvents. One such mechanism is the active efflux of solvents from the cell, preserving the integrity of the cell interior.<ce:italic>Pseudomonas putida</ce:italic> S12 is resistant to a wide variety of normally detrimental solvents due to the action of such an efflux pump. The genes for this solvent efflux pump were cloned from <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 and their nucleotide sequence determined. The deduced amino acid sequences encoded by the three genes involved show a striking resemblance to proteins known to be involved in proton-dependent multidrug efflux systems. Transfer of the genes for the solvent efflux pump to solvent-sensitive <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> strains results in the acquisition of solvent resistance. This opens up the possibilities of using the solvent efflux system to construct bacterial strains capable of performing biocatalytic transformations of insoluble substrates in two-phase aqueous:organic medium.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>
         </head>
         <body view="all">
            <ce:sections>
               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">The microbial transformation of hydrocarbons is important not only in environmental applications such as soil remediation and waste stream purification, but also in biocatalytic applications for the production of specialty chemicals. The metabolic pathways by which many of these compounds are degraded in various bacteria have been elucidated and in many cases the genes coding for the enzymes involved have been cloned and sequenced (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref180" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>). A major problem in applying hydrocarbon degrading bacteria to industrial processes is their susceptibility to the toxic effects of the very substrate that the organism is utilizing as a carbon source. This is often due to accumulation of the hydrophobic compound in bacterial membranes which can cause devastating effects on membrane structure (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref190" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref200" refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>). A second problem in the application of catabolic pathways in the synthesis of fine chemicals is that many of the desired substrates of enzymatic reactions are sparingly soluble in water and thus may not be fully bioavailable to microorganisms. The use of solvent tolerant bacteria allows the introduction of a nonpolar phase to the medium, dissolving the desired substrate, and increasing the exposure of the cell to the substrate.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Many different mechanisms have been described that contribute to solvent resistance (Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="fig1">1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>) but despite these efforts no comprehensive overview is available to explain the physiological response of microorganisms to toxic organic solvents (for a recent review, see Weber and de Bont (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>)). Our laboratories have been investigating the ability of <ce:italic>Pseudomonas putida</ce:italic> S12 to withstand toxic concentrations of toluene and other organic solvents (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>). This organism has evolved at least two mechanisms to combat the accumulation of hydrophobic solvents in the membrane or the interior of the cell. One key observation was the detection of <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>- rather than <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>-unsaturated fatty acids in the membrane of the solvent-tolerant bacterium upon exposure to solvents (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>). The conversion of <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>- to <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>-unsaturated fatty acids by a direct isomerization alters the packing of the phospholipids in the bacterial membrane. This results in a change in membrane fluidity, making the membrane less likely to allow solvents to partition into it, decreasing the detrimental effects on the membrane due to solvent partitioning, and thus increasing the solvent resistance of the cell (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>). Recently it has been shown that a second mechanism of solvent resistance is possessed by <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12. This is an energy-dependent active efflux system for solvents such as toluene (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>) that may function in a fashion similar to that for multidrug efflux pumps found in many antibiotic-resistant microorganisms. Thus, <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 employs at least two mechanisms for active defense against the detrimental effects of solvents: one functioning to keep solvents out of the interior of the cell and a second functioning to prevent solvents from partitioning into the cell membrane.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">In the present report we describe the construction of transposon mutants of <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 that have lost the solvent tolerant phenotype. This allowed the cloning of the genes responsible for a solvent efflux pump that is involved in the ability of <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 to withstand toxic concentrations of organic solvents. The nucleotide sequence of the genes involved was determined and their relationship to other bacterial efflux systems is discussed.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle10">EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec20" view="all">
                     <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                        <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle20">Bacterial Strains, Plasmids, Media, and Growth of Strains</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para40" view="all">
                              <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib10">10</ce:cross-ref>) is the wild-type strain capable of growth at supersaturated solvent concentrations (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>) and is the object of the present investigation. <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> JK1 is a solvent-sensitive mutant of <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 derived in the present work by transposon mutagenesis with Tn<ce:italic>Mod</ce:italic>-KmO. P. putida JJD1, also derived in the present work, contains a kanamycin gene/ColE1 origin cassette insertion in the genome of<ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12.                   <ce:section id="cesec80" view="all">
                     <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                        <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Isolation of Solvent-sensitive Mutants</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para90" view="all">
                              <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic>S12 was chosen for a molecular study of the basis of solvent resistance due to the extensive physiological studies that have been performed on the strain (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib4 bib5 bib6">4-6</ce:cross-refs>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib24 bib25 bib26">24-26</ce:cross-refs>). The organism can grow in the presence of a wide variety of normally toxic solvents with log P<ce:inf loc="post">OW</ce:inf>values ranging from 2.3 to 3.5 (Table <ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="tblI">I</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tblI"/>). Initially, several solvent-sensitive transposon mutants were constructed using Tn<ce:italic>Mod</ce:italic>-KmO. P. putida S12 mutants which are no longer resistant to solvents were detected by the inability of kanamycin-resistant exconjugants to grow on L medium in the presence of supersaturated vapor concentrations of toluene as described under “Experimental Procedures.” Several toluene-sensitive mutants were obtained and one of these, designated strain JK1, was chosen for further analysis. Besides toluene, JK1 is sensitive to a number of other solvents with log P<ce:inf loc="post">OW</ce:inf> values less than or equal to 3.5 (Table <ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="tblI">I</ce:cross-ref>), indicating that a single genetic trait is responsible for resistance to all of the solvents tested.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Cloning and Analysis of the Genes for Solvent Resistance</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para100" view="all">To characterize the genes for solvent resistance in <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic>S12 in more detail, the region of the genome containing the transposon insertion in mutant JK1 was cloned. This was aided by the fact that the Tn<ce:italic>Mod</ce:italic>-KmO transposable element utilized in the construction of the mutants contains an origin of replication derived from plasmid ColE1. Total genomic DNA from JK1 was cleaved with<ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI, ligated to form circular molecules, and electroporated into <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> JM109 with selection for kanamycin resistance. Since <ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI does not cleave the transposon, the resulting clone, pJD101 (Fig.<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>), must contain DNA from both sides of the transposon insertion. Approximately 11 kilobases of genomic DNA was cloned along with the transposon which contains the origin of replication and the kanamycin resistance gene (2 kb). The point at which the transposon is inserted is only 1 kb away from one end of the<ce:italic>Bam</ce:italic>HI fragment cloned from strain JK1. This being the case, a second, overlapping 4-kb <ce:italic>Pst</ce:italic>I fragment was cloned from strain JK1 (designated pJD102, Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                           <ce:para id="para110" view="all">The cloned genomic DNA was initially analyzed by determining the nucleotide sequence to either side of the transposon insertion point using primers specific for either end of the transposon. Screening for similar nucleotide sequences in the GenBank data base revealed a significant match with genes coding for multidrug resistance export pumps, consistent with the hypothesis that a solvent efflux pump is a key solvent resistance mechanism of <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12. The complete nucleotide sequence of an operon into which the transposon had inserted was determined using a series of subclones and internal oligonucleotide primers. Both strands of a 6.5-kb <ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I fragment were sequenced in their entirety. To accurately determine the nucleotide sequence at the transposon insertion point, a 0.5-kb DNA fragment was amplified by PCR from the genome of the wild-type strain<ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 using oligonucleotide primers flanking the transposon insertion point in strain JK1. A cartoon of the nucleotide sequence obtained is shown in Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref> to show the relationship of the open reading frames with the two clones pJD101 and pJD102 and the complete nucleotide sequence is shown in Fig.<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="fig3">3</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>. The three open reading frames show significant homology to the three proteins that assemble to form proton-dependent multidrug resistance efflux pumps (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>) and thus the genes were labeled <ce:italic>srp</ce:italic> for <ce:underline>s</ce:underline>olvent<ce:underline>r</ce:underline>esistance <ce:underline>p</ce:underline>ump.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec120" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Complementation of P. putida JK1</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para120" view="all">To prove that the three open reading frames detected in the cloned fragment at the point of insertion of the Tn<ce:italic>Mod</ce:italic>-KmO transposon actually are responsible for solvent resistance, complementation experiments were performed. This required reconstruction of the operon since the clones obtained (pJD101 and pJD102) contain the transposon mutagenized DNA. A<ce:italic>Bgl</ce:italic>II-<ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I kanamycin resistance cassette also containing the ColE1 origin of replication from Tn<ce:italic>Mod</ce:italic>-KmO was inserted at the <ce:italic>Bgl</ce:italic>II and <ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I sites of a 6.7-kb <ce:italic>Cla</ce:italic>I fragment derived from pJD101. The resulting plasmid (pJD103, Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>) was electroporated into<ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 to construct a new mutation by site-specific reciprocal recombination. The resulting strain, JJD1, contains a kanamycin resistance gene adjacent to the <ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes in the genome. A 12-kb <ce:italic>Eco</ce:italic>RI genomic fragment containing the kanamycin/ColE1 cassette was cloned from JJD1. This plasmid, designated pJD104 (Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>), contains the intact <ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes. A 6.5-kb<ce:italic>Sst</ce:italic>I fragment was cloned from pJD104 into the vector pUCP22 in both orientations with respect to the <ce:italic>lac</ce:italic> promoter. JK1 containing either of these two plasmids, designated pJD105 and pJD106 (Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="fig2">2</ce:cross-ref>), regained solvent resistance. JK1(pJD105), containing the<ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes in the same orientation as the <ce:italic>lac</ce:italic>promoter, regained resistance to all of the solvents that the original strain, <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12, was resistant to (Table <ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="tblI">I</ce:cross-ref>). However, JK1(pJD106), containing the <ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes in the opposite orientation to the <ce:italic>lac</ce:italic> promoter, regained resistance to only two solvents, hexane and cyclohexane, with log P<ce:inf loc="post">OW</ce:inf> values near the border of the resistance phenotype. These results are consistent with the solvent resistance phenotype being dependent on the level of expression of the <ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes.</ce:para>
                        </ce:section>
                        <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                           <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">Transfer of the Solvent Resistance Phenotype</ce:section-title>
                           <ce:para id="para130" view="all">
                              <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 displays multiple physiological responses to organic solvents (see Introduction). Intuitively, a solvent efflux pump would be the most important mechanism of solvent resistance since it would be involved in actively removing solvents from the cell. Experiments were therefore performed to determine whether the solvent efflux pump by itself is capable of imparting the solvent resistance phenotype on other <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> strains. The two plasmids, pJD105 and pJD106, were electroporated into the normally solvent sensitive<ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> PPO200. The resulting recombinant strains are able to grow on rich medium in a toluene-saturated atmosphere, whereas the parent strain PPO200 with the vector pUCP22 could not (Fig.<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="fig4">4</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). PPO200(pJD106) grew slightly slower than PPO200(pJD105), probably due to the fact that the<ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes are expressed from the <ce:italic>lac</ce:italic> promoter in pJD105. In liquid culture, PPO200(pUCP22) could withstand concentrations of toluene up to 2.8 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> while both PPO200(pJD105) and PPO200(pJD106) showed resistance to the toxic effects of toluene up to a concentration of 4.9 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps> (Table<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="tblII">II</ce:cross-ref>
                              <ce:float-anchor refid="tblII"/>). Neither of the recombinant strains were resistant to a second phase of toluene (5.6 m<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>). These experiments indicate that the solvent resistance phenotype can be transferred to other bacterial strains and that the resistance can be enhanced by higher levels of gene expression.</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para140" view="all">In the past several years solvent-resistant microorganisms have been isolated directly from the environment (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib5">5</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib29 bib30 bib31 bib32 bib33">29-33</ce:cross-refs>) or through the process of mutation of a solvent-sensitive strain (<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib34 bib35 bib36">34-36</ce:cross-refs>). It is evident that these organisms must therefore have specific adaptation mechanisms that impart the solvent resistance. Physiological studies on microorganisms isolated from the environment that are naturally resistant to high levels of organic solvents have revealed that many different factors may play a role (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>). Naturally solvent-resistant bacteria have been shown to alter the composition of the cell membrane by increasing the ratio of <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic>- to<ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>-unsaturated fatty acids (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>) or by changing the headgroup composition (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>). This change in the cell membrane produces a physical barrier, preventing solvents from entering the cell by decreasing membrane fluidity. This would not entirely prevent solvents from entering the cell, only slow down their diffusion into the cell and increase the time needed to reach equilibrium with the external environment. An intuitively better method of solvent resistance would be to physically remove the solvent from the cell. One way of doing this would be to degrade the solvent but this would only be effective against low concentrations of solvents. Evidence was recently obtained that an energy-dependent export system for hydrophobic solvents functions to remove solvents from the interior of whole cells (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>). This solvent efflux pump should be a key element in solvent resistance by naturally solvent-resistant bacteria. The genes for such a solvent efflux pump in <ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> S12 were identified via transposon mutagenesis to construct a solvent-sensitive strain. The genes were cloned and sequenced and their role in solvent resistance verified through complementation of the transposon mutation. The three genes involved were labeled <ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> for <ce:underline>s</ce:underline>olvent<ce:underline>r</ce:underline>esistance <ce:underline>p</ce:underline>ump.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para150" view="all">The deduced amino acid sequences of the proteins encoded by the<ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic> genes have extensive homology with those for proton-dependent multidrug efflux systems of the resistance/nodulation/cell division family (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>). This “RND” family of efflux pumps is composed of three protein components that together span the inner and outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria: an inner membrane transporter (SrpB analogues), an outer membrane channel (SrpC analogues), and a periplasmic linker protein (SrpA analogues). Members of this family have been shown to be involved in export of antibiotics, metals, and oligosaccharides involved in nodulation signaling. Based on the work presented here, this family can be broadened to include a new class of efflux pump, involved in export of solvents. Dendrograms showing the phylogenetic relationship of SrpA, SrpB, and SrpC to other proteins involved in multidrug resistance are shown in Fig. <ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="fig5">5</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig5"/>. The<ce:italic>srpABC</ce:italic>-encoded proteins show the most homology with those for the <ce:italic>mexAB</ce:italic>/<ce:italic>oprM</ce:italic>-encoded multidrug resistance pump found in <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>). SrpA, SrpB, and SrpC are 57.8, 64.4, and 58.5% identical to MexA, MexB, and OprM, respectively. The three dendrograms show that the Srp and the Mex proteins fall into a distinct class, separate from but still closely related to the other members of the RND family of efflux pumps. The evolutionary relationship of the solvent resistance pump to multidrug resistance pumps is not surprising since they both function to export hydrophobic molecules from the cell. It is logical that a solvent efflux pump would have evolved since it would enable microorganisms to survive in close proximity to oil or coal deposits (a rich source of carbon and energy) in the environment.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para160" view="all">There have been several attempts to clone genes that are involved in solvent resistance leading in many cases to the identification of a protein that is somehow involved in making the cell resistant to a single solvent or to a group of related solvents. Most of these studies took place using <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> that was forced under selective pressure to become solvent resistant. Mutations in a number of different genes can result in an increased tolerance to a particular chosen solvent, any one of which can allow the cell to grow in the presence of the solvent. Genes implicated in increased organic solvent tolerance of <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> include the uncharacterized<ce:italic>ostA</ce:italic> (for <ce:underline>o</ce:underline>rganic <ce:underline>s</ce:underline>olvent<ce:underline>t</ce:underline>olerance) (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>), <ce:italic>ahpC</ce:italic> encoding alkylhydroperoxide reductase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>), <ce:italic>robA</ce:italic> encoding a global regulatory protein (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref750" refid="bib41">41</ce:cross-ref>), and <ce:italic>soxS</ce:italic> encoding regulatory proteins controlling the superoxide response regulon (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref760" refid="bib42">42</ce:cross-ref>). These genes enhance the survivability of the organism in the presence of a particular solvent but are not responsible for solvent resistance<ce:italic>per se</ce:italic> since they do not aid in understanding the true mechanism(s) of solvent resistance found naturally in environmental isolates. This article, however, represents the first example of cloning and characterization of genes for a major solvent resistance mechanism: a proton-dependent solvent efflux pump.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para170" view="all">We have shown that the cloned genes can be transferred to another<ce:italic>P. putida</ce:italic> strain with the concomitant gain of solvent resistance by that organism. This has far reaching implications for industrial applications in the fine chemistry area. Existing and potential biocatalytic processes for compounds such as catechols, phenols, medium chain alcohols, and enantiopure epoxides are suboptimal because the products formed are very toxic to normal microorganisms. Product accumulation to a concentration which allows economic downstream processing is inherently prevented by the physical characteristics of these compounds. The ability, as demonstrated here, to take a normally solvent-sensitive strain and make it solvent resistant will greatly enhance the ability of a given strain to perform a desired biocatalytic reaction resulting in otherwise toxic products.</ce:para>
