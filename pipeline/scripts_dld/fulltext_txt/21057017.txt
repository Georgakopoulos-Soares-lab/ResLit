Abstract 

Knowledge of the nature of resistance determinants in natural habitats is fundamental to increasing our understanding of the development of antibiotic resistance in clinical settings. Here we provide the first report of a tetracycline resistance-encoding plasmid, pBHS24, from a marine sponge-associated bacterium, Bacillus sp. strain #24, isolated from Haliclona simulans . 

The increased selection pressure created by the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in clinical and agricultural settings has undoubtedly driven the evolution and spread of resistance determinants ( 8 , 11 ). While many resistance genes are believed to have their origin in natural ecosystems, their abundance, nature, and ecological role in such settings remain relatively obscure ( 2 ). It is nevertheless accepted that this knowledge is fundamental to tackle the ingenious ways that bacteria curtail the effectiveness of antimicrobial agents. 

Research on marine sponge-associated bacteria has increased exponentially in recent years, but this has focused predominantly on phylogeny and on the search for novel bioactive compounds ( 13 , 22 ). While much data are available on the microbial ecology of marine sponges ( 12 , 22 ), comparatively little is known about the incidence and diversity of antibiotic resistance determinants and the often associated mobile elements among these bacteria. 

We have recently characterized the spore-forming population of the marine sponge Haliclona simulans , collected from Gurraig Sound, Kilkieran Bay, Galway, Ireland. One of the isolates was identified by partial 16S rRNA gene sequencing as a Bacillus sp., with 96% sequence identity to its closest relative, Bacillus lehensis . Bacillus sp. strain #24 was unable to grow in SYP ( 12 ) and LB media unless supplemented with either 50% artificial seawater ( 12 ) or 2% NaCl and was resistant to tetracycline and erythromycin (MICs of 75 μg ml −1 and 3 mg ml −1 , respectively. MIC values reported in this study are defined as the minimal concentration of antibiotic able to inhibit the growth of the strains when spotted on Muller-Hinton medium plates containing increasing concentrations of the antibiotic). Screening of isolate #24 for plasmid DNA ( 6 ) was positive, with multiple bands visible on agarose gels. In order to ascertain its role in antibiotic resistance, total plasmid DNA purified from isolate #24 (QIAprep Spin miniprep kit optimized for Bacillus ; Qiagen GmbH) was used to transform Bacillus subtilis 168 competent cells. This strain is sensitive to erythromycin and displays only a very low level of resistance to tetracycline (2 μg ml −1 ) associated with the presence of a chromosomally carried tet (L) gene ( 9 , 20 ). B. subtilis 168 is easily differentiated from isolate #24 by colony morphology (Fig. 1 ). A large number of transformants were obtained on LB medium-tetracycline plates, while no colonies grew on those containing erythromycin (both at 5 μg ml −1 ). This implied that tetracycline resistance was associated with plasmid DNA, while erythromycin resistance was probably chromosomally encoded. As expected, no growth was observed on antibiotic control plates when plasmid DNA was not added to the cells. A single plasmid, named pBHS24, of identical size to that of one of the two present in the natural host Bacillus sp. strain #24, was purified from B. subtilis Tet r transformants (Fig. 1 ). The level of resistance conferred by pBHS24 in the B. subtilis background was significantly higher than that in Bacillus sp. strain #24 (MICs > 100 μg ml −1 ). This could be due to the presence of the plasmid in a different genetic background or possibly to a synergistic effect between the plasmid-encoded Tet r determinant and the chromosomally carried tet (L) gene in B. subtilis 168. Conversely, we have not screened Bacillus sp. strain #24 for previously described Tet r determinants, nor have we cured it of pBHS24, and therefore, the concomitant existence of Tet r genes in different genomic locations of this strain cannot be excluded.

Double digestion of pBHS24 with EcoRI and HindIII generated three fragments of approximately 3.1, 1, and 0.85 kb, indicating a plasmid of approximately 5 kb in size. Initial sequencing of these fragments upon being cloned into the plasmid vector pUC18 was achieved with the M13 primers (GATC Biotech AG, Germany). The complete sequence of the plasmid was finalized in both directions by primer walking, using these constructs and pBHS24 as templates. 

To our knowledge, pBHS24 is the first plasmid encoding antibiotic resistance to be sequenced from a sponge bacterial isolate. BLAST analysis ( http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ) ( 3 ) revealed the nucleotide sequence of pBHS24 to be almost identical (99%) to those of two recently described tetracycline resistance-encoding plasmids, pMA67 (GenBank accession no. DQ367664 ) and pLS55 (GenBank accession no. EF605268 ). pMA67 was identified in the honey bee pathogen Paenibacillus larvae , a Gram-positive spore former, which causes American foulbrood ( 16 , 17 ). pLS55 was identified in the Gram-positive anaerobe Lactobacillus sakei Rits 9, isolated from an Italian Sola cheese ( 5 ). This lactic acid bacterium is used in fermented meat products and is found naturally on fresh meat and fish ( 10 ). More recently, the sequence of an almost identical plasmid, pSU1, from the Gram-positive spore former Sporosarcina ureae , isolated from the subsurface beneath a broiler chicken farm, was placed in the database (publication date, 30 March 2010; GenBank accession no. GU584223 ). pMA67 has also been transformed into Bacillus megaterium and Escherichia coli , despite its instability in the latter host ( 17 ), and here, we demonstrate that B. subtilis is also a suitable host for pBHS24. Coincidently, three of the four Gram-positive natural hosts identified to date are spore-forming bacteria. We do not know the incidence of this plasmid within the sponge-associated microbiota, the wider marine environment, or indeed, other habitats. We also have no evidence of the direct transfer of pBHS24 between the different identified hosts or of whether the plasmid exchange involved intermediate species. Despite this, the presence of nearly identical plasmids from commensal symbiotic and pathogenic bacterial species from four different genera, isolated from quite distinct ecological habitats, attests not only to the wide genetic transfer of this Tet r element but also to its broad host range. It also supports the idea that environmental bacteria, including those associated with marine sponges, may play a role as reservoirs of resistance genes, which could potentially be transferred into clinically relevant bacteria via their associated mobile elements. 

pMA67, and by inference, the other plasmids, is predicated to be a rolling-circle-replicating, mobilizable plasmid ( 17 ). However, contrary to what has been reported for pMA67 ( 17 ), single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) of pBHS24 appears to be visible on agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide (Fig. 1 ). While the nearest matches to the sequences of the Rep and Cop proteins of pBHS24 and related plasmids are protein sequences from plasmids of different Lactobacillus species (80 to 75% and 74 to 50% identity, respectively), the Mob protein, which is likely to be involved in the transfer of these plasmids, has sequences present in Bacillus plasmids that are nearest matches (≤56%). These differences may indicate separate origins for different sequences within the plasmid. 

The only other functional sequence present on these plasmids besides those potentially involved in replication and mobilization is that of the tetracycline resistance tet (L) gene. Tetracyclines are broad-spectrum antibiotics which have been widely used in clinical, agricultural, and aquacultural settings and have seen their efficacy limited by the spread of many mechanistically different resistance determinants ( 19 ). tet (L) codes for a multifunctional efflux pump ( 14 , 19 ), which is involved in physiological roles such as Na + resistance, K + acquisition, and alkaline pH homeostasis ( 14 , 23 ). Surprisingly, the eight nucleotide differences observed between pLS55 and pBHS24 are located within the tet (L) gene and its regulatory regions (Fig. 2 ). The same applies to the three nucleotide differences between pSU1 and pBHS24, which are also located within tet (L) and common to three of the six nucleotide differences seen with pMA67 (Fig. 2 ). Murray et al. ( 17 ) have shown that the Tet(L) protein of pMA67 differs considerably from all the other previously reported Tet L determinants, with phylogenetic analysis indicating a relatively ancient divergence from other plasmid-carried tet (L) genes. The sequences of plasmid-encoded Tet(L) proteins differ by less than 2%, while they differ from those of the four related plasmids discussed here by approximately 13%, with the Bacillus cereus plasmid pBC16-Tet(L) having the nearest match (87%) ( 5 , 17 ).

Subcloning of pBHS24 in E. coli has shown that transformants containing the recombinant molecule with the 3.1-kb tet (L)-carrying insert were resistant to tetracycline. In contrast, transformants bearing either of the other two fragments were unable to grow in the presence of the antibiotic. This suggested that the tetracycline resistance determinant was expressed and fully functional in the Gram-negative heterologous host. This is in agreement with findings by Murray et al. ( 17 ), who demonstrated experimentally that the tet (L) gene carried by plasmid pMA67 alone was sufficient for conferring tetracycline resistance in E. coli ( 17 ). 

Some of the natural hosts for these plasmids have been isolated from environments exposed to antibiotics, thereby justifying the acquisition of a resistance gene by the microflora of the exposed habitat. For instance, oxytetracycline has been used in the management of American foulbrood, and resistance in P. larvae appears to correlate with the presence of pMA67 ( 16 ). Antibiotics used in therapy and agriculture accumulate in the environment and contaminate aquatic habitats where they can exert their selective pressure on the native flora ( 2 , 4 , 7 ). Importantly, oxytetracyclines are frequently used in aquaculture ( 21 ), and tetracycline resistance genes, including tet (L), have been described in many aquatic environments ( 1 , 15 , 18 ). 

Alternatively, the multifunctional Tet(L) efflux pump present in pBHS24 could be providing Bacillus sp. strain #24 with some functional advantages through, for example, increased adaptation to the marine-sponge environment. These could hypothetically involve Na + resistance, tolerance to toxic agents, such as heavy metals, or perhaps transport of signaling or other bioactive molecules, which might be present in this environment and might be the natural substrates for these pumps ( 2 ). Curiously, the closest known relatives of isolate #24 are alkaliphilic bacterial strains, and Tet(L) has a well-established role in alkaline resistance ( 14 ).

The nucleotide sequence of plasmid pBHS24 from Bacillus sp. strain #24 has been deposited in GenBank under accession number HM235948 .
