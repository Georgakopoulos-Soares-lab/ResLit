                  Objectives
                  Multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (MDR-Ab), particularly strains producing oxacillinase (OXA)-type carbapenemases, have rapidly emerged in health care settings as a frequent cause of serious infections with limited treatment options. This study evaluated the in vitro activity of sulbactam (SUL) combined with durlobactam (DUR) against a collection of carbapenemase-producing A. baumannii, and investigated the mechanisms of resistance.
               
                  Methods
                  Susceptibility testing was performed on 100 isolates by either broth microdilution or by the Epsilometer test. Isolates were screened for the insertion sequence ISAba1 upstream of the intrinsic chromosomal blaADC by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Whole genome sequencing was performed on 25 SUL-DUR resistant isolates, and analyses were performed using the Center for Genomic Epidemiology platform. Target gene sequences were compared to A. baumannii American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 17978.
               
                  Results
                  SUL-DUR exhibited excellent activity against A. baumannii isolates with susceptibility levels as follows: amikacin, 18%; colistin, 91%; cefepime, 5%; imipenem, 0%; minocycline, 46%; SUL, 3%; sulbactam-cefoperazone, 8%; SUL-DUR, 71% (based on a breakpoint at 4 mg/L). Twenty-five non-New Delhi metallo-ß–lactamase (NDM)-producing isolates had SUL-DUR MIC values &gt;4 mg/L, amongst which 14 isolates showed substitutions in penicillin-binding protein (PBP)3, previously shown to be associated with SUL-DUR resistance. Substitutions that have not previously been described were detected in SUL-DUR targets, namely PBP1a, PBP1b, PBP2, and PBP3. By contrast, there was no evidence of the involvement of permeability or efflux.
               
                  Conclusions
                  SUL-DUR exhibited excellent in vitro antibacterial activity against carbapenemase-producing A. baumannii isolates. Amongst the 25 resistant isolates, we identified a number of mechanisms which may be contributing factors, in particular PBP substitutions and the production of specific beta-lactamases.

            <ce:abstract id="abs0002" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0003" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0003">Objectives</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara008" view="all">Multidrug-resistant <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> (MDR-Ab), particularly strains producing oxacillinase (OXA)-type carbapenemases, have rapidly emerged in health care settings as a frequent cause of serious infections with limited treatment options. This study evaluated the in vitro activity of sulbactam (SUL) combined with durlobactam (DUR) against a collection of carbapenemase-producing <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, and investigated the mechanisms of resistance.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0004" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0004">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara009" view="all">Susceptibility testing was performed on 100 isolates by either broth microdilution or by the Epsilometer test. Isolates were screened for the insertion sequence IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> upstream of the intrinsic chromosomal <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Whole genome sequencing was performed on 25 SUL-DUR resistant isolates, and analyses were performed using the Center for Genomic Epidemiology platform. Target gene sequences were compared to <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 17978.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0005" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0005">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara010" view="all">SUL-DUR exhibited excellent activity against <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> isolates with susceptibility levels as follows: amikacin, 18%; colistin, 91%; cefepime, 5%; imipenem, 0%; minocycline, 46%; SUL, 3%; sulbactam-cefoperazone, 8%; SUL-DUR, 71% (based on a breakpoint at 4 mg/L). Twenty-five non-New Delhi metallo-ß–lactamase (NDM)-producing isolates had SUL-DUR MIC values &gt;4 mg/L, amongst which 14 isolates showed substitutions in penicillin-binding protein (PBP)3, previously shown to be associated with SUL-DUR resistance. Substitutions that have not previously been described were detected in SUL-DUR targets, namely PBP1a, PBP1b, PBP2, and PBP3. By contrast, there was no evidence of the involvement of permeability or efflux.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abss0006" role="conclusion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0006">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara011" view="all">SUL-DUR exhibited excellent in vitro antibacterial activity against carbapenemase-producing <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> isolates. Amongst the 25 resistant isolates, we identified a number of mechanisms which may be contributing factors, in particular PBP substitutions and the production of specific beta-lactamases.</ce:simple-para>

                  <ce:label>1</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0008">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para0008" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> is one of the so-called ‘ESKAPE’ pathogens. The ESKAPE pathogens are a group of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria comprised of <ce:italic>Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>Enterobacter</ce:italic> spp. Carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> is labelled as “Priority 1; Critical” on the WHO's Global Priority List of Antibiotic-Resistant Bacteria to Guide Research, Discovery, and Development of New Antibiotics <ce:cross-ref id="crf0012" refid="bib0001">[1]</ce:cross-ref>. Multidrug-resistant (resistance to three or more classes of antimicrobials) <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> (MDR-Ab) have rapidly emerged in health care settings as a frequent cause of serious infections including pneumonia, bacteremia, and wound infections <ce:cross-ref id="crf0013" refid="bib0002">[2]</ce:cross-ref>. The incidence of infections and outbreaks involving bacterial isolates that produce oxacillinase (OXA)-type carbapenemases has increased significantly over the past two decades. As a result, most therapeutic options, including last-resort drugs (e.g. carbapenems), have been rendered ineffective [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0014" refid="bib0002">2</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0015" refid="bib0003">3</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0009" view="all">The so-called first-generation ß-lactamase inhibitor sulbactam (SUL) has been in clinical use since its approval in the 1980s. It is usually used in combination with ampicillin or cefoperazone for the treatment of infections caused by broad-spectrum ß-lactamase-producing <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. because of its unique antibacterial activity against this organism <ce:cross-ref id="crf0016" refid="bib0004">[4]</ce:cross-ref>. Hence, SUL possesses dual ß-lactam and ß-lactamase inhibitory activity, with the ability to bind to and subsequently inhibit serine ß-lactamases, but also to bind to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), including PBP1a, PBP1b, and PBP3 in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. <ce:cross-ref id="crf0017" refid="bib0004">[4]</ce:cross-ref>. However, the degradation of SUL by some ß-lactamases, including TEM-1 <ce:cross-ref id="crf0018" refid="bib0005">[5]</ce:cross-ref>, and its poor inhibitory activity against some class D enzymes, has resulted in the poor activity of SUL combinations currently available (e.g. ampicillin-sulbactam) <ce:cross-ref id="crf0019" refid="bib0006">[6]</ce:cross-ref>. Durlobactam (DUR) is a novel non-ß-lactam diazabicyclooctane (DBO) ß-lactamase inhibitor that exhibits broad-spectrum activity against class A, C, and D ß-lactamases <ce:cross-ref id="crf0020" refid="bib0007">[7]</ce:cross-ref>. Compared to avibactam (AVI), another DBO inhibitor that is currently licensed for clinical use, DUR exhibits greater activity against class D ß-lactamases, including those with carbapenemase activity <ce:cross-refs id="crf0021" refid="bib0007 bib0008 bib0009">[7–9]</ce:cross-refs>. Studies have found that the mechanism of action of DUR is similar to that of AVI; the inhibitor forms a covalent bond with the active site serine, resulting in the carbamylation and inactivation of the ß-lactamase before dissociation of the intact inhibitor [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0023" refid="bib0007">7</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0024" refid="bib0008">8</ce:cross-ref>]. Like SUL, DUR can be considered to have dual action in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, as it has been shown to exhibit binding and inhibition features with respect to PBP2 (and to a lesser extent, PBP1a), although this does not lead to sufficient antibacterial activity on its own against this organism <ce:cross-ref id="crf0025" refid="bib0007">[7]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para0010" view="all">Recently, SUL and DUR have been combined to target MDR-Ab, particularly those producing OXA-type carbapenemases, <ce:cross-refs id="crf0026" refid="bib0007 bib0008 bib0009">[7–9]</ce:cross-refs> and several recent studies have reported the excellent activity of SUL-DUR against MDR <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. This in vitro study aimed to both evaluate the SUL-DUR combination against a predefined collection of MDR-Ab, and to explore the possible mechanisms for corresponding resistance.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec0002" role="materials-methods" view="all">
                  <ce:label>2</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0009">Materials and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec0003" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0010">Isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0011" view="all">One hundred nonduplicate clinical <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> isolates with previously characterized resistance mechanisms were used in this study. They were selected to be representative of the MDR patterns commonly observed in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> among human strains of worldwide origin that contribute to infection (e.g. septicemia; pulmonary and urinary infection; and catheter infections). This collection comprised producers of OXA-23 (n = 73), OXA-72 (n = 10), OXA-40 (n = 6), OXA-58 (n = 5), OXA-24 (n = 1), and New Delhi metallo-ß–lactamase (NDM) (n = 5, NDM-1 [n = 4], and NDM-5 [n = 1]) enzymes. Within these, a subset of 42 isolates were particularly resistant because they also produced 16S rRNA methylases (which are pandrug-resistant to aminoglycosides), and 9 were colistin (COL)-resistant.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0004" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0011">Susceptibility testing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0012" view="all">The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for each strain and drug combination were determined following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI M100-S30, 2020) <ce:cross-ref id="crf0028" refid="bib0010">[10]</ce:cross-ref>. Materials included premanufactured frozen 96-well broth microdilution panels with Mueller Hinton Broth (MHBII) growth medium and antibiotics supplied by Entasis Therapeutics (Waltham, MA): amikacin (AMK), COL, cefepime (FEP), minocycline (MIN), SUL, sulbactam-cefoperazone (SUL-CEF), and SUL-DUR. SUL-DUR was tested as a dilution of SUL in the presence of DUR at a fixed concentration of 4 mg/L. Imipenem (IMI) MICs were determined by the Epsilometer test (Biomerieux, La Balme Les Grottes, France). Breakpoints of R&gt;4 mg/L, based upon the CLSI ampicillin-sulbactam breakpoint, were used for SUL, SUL-CEF, and SUL-DUR (for which no breakpoints have yet been established against <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0005" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0012">Whole genome sequencing and analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0013" view="all">Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on all nonNDM isolates with SUL-DUR MIC values of ≥ 8 mg/L on a MiSeq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using the Nextera sample preparation method (2 × 300 bp paired-end reads and a coverage of ≥50X). Reads were assembled into contigs using the Shovill pipeline (<ce:inter-ref id="interref0001" xlink:href="https://github.com/tseemann/shovill" xlink:type="simple">https://github.com/tseemann/shovill</ce:inter-ref>) and contigs were annotated using Prokka software <ce:cross-ref id="crf0029" refid="bib0011">[11]</ce:cross-ref>. Sequence types (STs), the presence of resistance genes, and the confirmation of speciation were determined using MLST (Multilocus sequence typing) 2.0, ResFinder 4.1 <ce:cross-ref id="crf0030" refid="bib0012">[12]</ce:cross-ref>, and KmerFinder 3.2 <ce:cross-ref id="crf0031" refid="bib0013">[13]</ce:cross-ref> software available on the Center for Genomic Epidemiology platform (<ce:inter-ref id="interref0002" xlink:href="https://www.cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services" xlink:type="simple">https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services/</ce:inter-ref>). Mutations and substitutions in PBPs were identified by sequence extraction and comparison with the <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> ATCC 17978 genome (GenBank Accession No. CP018664). NCBI BLAST (National Center for Biotechnology Information Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) was used to investigate the prevalence of PBP protein sequences, and variants producing &lt;10 hits were considered ‘uncommon’.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para0014" view="all">Novel <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> alleles, <ce:italic>blaADC-259</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>blaADC-260</ce:italic>, were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers OK340849 and OK396701, respectively.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0006" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0013">Screening of ISAba1</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0015" view="all">Isolates were screened by PCR for the presence of the insertion sequence <ce:italic>ISAba1</ce:italic> upstream of <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> as previously described <ce:cross-ref id="crf0032" refid="bib0014">[14]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec0007" role="results" view="all">
                  <ce:label>3</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0014">Results and discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec0008" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0015">Susceptibility testing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0016" view="all">Susceptibility testing (<ce:cross-ref id="crf0033" refid="tbl0001">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl0001"/>) showed the susceptibility levels to the tested antibiotics as follows: AMK, 18%; COL, 91%; FEP, 5%; IMI, 0%; MIN, 46%; SUL, 3%; SUL-CEF, 8%; and SUL-DUR, 71%. SUL-DUR exhibited greater levels of susceptibility, with the exception of COL, to all tested antibiotics, most notably when compared with imipenem. It is important to note that, largely due to colistin-heteroresistance, COL treatment of MDR-Ab infections has been associated with the emergence of resistance during therapy and unfavourable outcomes <ce:cross-ref id="crf0034" refid="bib0015">[15]</ce:cross-ref>. Low levels of susceptibility to the aminoglycoside AMK could be attributed to the production of the 16S rRNA aminoglycoside resistance methylase (ArmA) for 42 isolates in this study. Overall, a comparison of SUL, SUL-CEF, and SUL-DUR showed a significant ß-lactamase antibacterial effect of DUR against most of the strains, including those producing class D carbapenemases (OXA-23, OXA-24, OXA-40, OXA-58, and OXA-72), and one out of five NDM-producing isolates. This result is consistent with the known inability of DUR, as with all clinically available inhibitors, to inhibit the activity of class B metallo-ß–lactamases <ce:cross-ref id="crf0035" refid="bib0007">[7]</ce:cross-ref>. Twenty-five isolates that did not produce NDM-type enzymes had SUL-DUR MIC values above the preliminary breakpoint of 4 mg/L [<ce:cross-ref id="crf0036" refid="bib0016">16</ce:cross-ref>,<ce:cross-ref id="crf0037" refid="bib0017">17</ce:cross-ref>]. Nine strains were resistant to COL, and in four out of these nine strains, SUL-DUR may offer a possibility of treatment (MIC &lt;8 mg/L). Approximately 46% of the isolates were susceptible to MIN, which resembles previously reported results for tetracycline derivatives versus <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. <ce:cross-ref id="crf0038" refid="bib0018">[18]</ce:cross-ref>. The MIC values of FEP were very high (95% ≥16 mg/L), indicating that this antibiotic cannot be considered as a possible alternative for treating infections caused by MDR-Ab isolates. As expected, the MIC values of SUL-CEF were also much higher than those of SUL-DUR. Overall these results are similar to those found in previous studies assessing the in vitro activity of SUL-DUR <ce:cross-refs id="crf0039" refid="bib0019 bib0020 bib0021">[19–21]</ce:cross-refs>
                     </ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec0009" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0016">Analysis and characteristics of SUL-DUR-resistant isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para0017" view="all">Twenty-five isolates with SUL-DUR MIC values of ≥ 8 mg/L that did not harbour any <ce:italic>blaNDM</ce:italic> genes were subject to WGS in order to define the molecular mechanism of resistance. The characteristics of these strains are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="crf0041" refid="tbl0002">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl0002"/>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:section id="sec0010" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.1</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0017">Beta-lactamases</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0018" view="all">The acquired OXA-type carbapenemase encoding genes that were identified among all SUL-DUR resistant isolates were as follows: <ce:italic>blaOXA-23</ce:italic> (n = 19), <ce:italic>blaOXA-72</ce:italic> (n = 5), and <ce:italic>blaOXA-24</ce:italic> (n = 1). Amongst the intrinsic <ce:italic>blaOXA-51</ce:italic>-like genes, most isolates had <ce:italic>blaOXA-66</ce:italic> (n = 18), followed by <ce:italic>blaOXA-68</ce:italic> (n = 2) and <ce:italic>blaOXA-90</ce:italic> (n = 2), and single isolates each had either <ce:italic>blaOXA-64, blaOXA-69</ce:italic>, or <ce:italic>blaOXA-94</ce:italic>. Three isolates harboured extended spectrum ß-lactamase (ESBLs) genes, namely <ce:italic>blaGES-11</ce:italic> (n =1) or <ce:italic>blaCTX-M-115</ce:italic> (n = 2); one isolate also harboured <ce:italic>blaCARB-16</ce:italic>. Seven isolates harboured <ce:italic>blaTEM-1</ce:italic>, which is known to confer resistance to SUL <ce:cross-ref id="crf0042" refid="bib0005">[5]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="sec0011" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.2</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0018">Sequence types</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0019" view="all">Seven and 17 different STs were identified according to the Pasteur (Pa) <ce:cross-ref id="crf0043" refid="bib0022">[22]</ce:cross-ref> and Oxford (Ox) <ce:cross-ref id="crf0044" refid="bib0023">[23]</ce:cross-ref> MLST schemes, respectively. The most represented STs were ST2Pa (n = 19) and ST1816Ox (n = 5). ST2Pa belongs to global clone 2 (GC2) and is the most dominant ST worldwide <ce:cross-ref id="crf0045" refid="bib0003">[3]</ce:cross-ref>. ST2Pa is commonly associated with <ce:italic>blaOXA</ce:italic> carbapenemase gene carriage and has been frequently reported as the cause of most nosocomial outbreaks <ce:cross-ref id="crf0046" refid="bib0003">[3]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="sec0012" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.3</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0019">ADC variants and ISAba1</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0020" view="all">The presence or absence of insertion sequence IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> upstream of the intrinsic cephalosporinase, <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic>, was investigated to identify any correlation with SUL or SUL-DUR resistance. Indeed, it was previously shown that the presence of this insertion sequence upstream of <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> results in overexpression of this Ambler class C ß-lactamase gene, and therefore leads to increased MICs of penicillins and cephalosporins <ce:cross-ref id="crf0047" refid="bib0014">[14]</ce:cross-ref>. Polymerase chain reaction screening identified the presence of IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> upstream of <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> in 78 of 100 isolates, therefore indicating that the <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> gene was likely to be overexpressed in those strains, but with no obvious correlation with MICs of SUL. Most (24 of 25) of the SUL-DUR resistant isolates harboured the IS<ce:italic>Aba1</ce:italic> element upstream of the <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> gene. Ten different <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> variants were identified, two of which correspond to novel alleles. In a previous study <ce:cross-ref id="crf0048" refid="bib0024">[24]</ce:cross-ref>, carriage of an overexpressed <ce:italic>blaADC-30</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>blaADC-73</ce:italic> gene was suggested to contribute to SUL-resistance; this was observed in 2 and 8 isolates in this study, respectively.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="sec0013" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.4</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0020">Penicillin binding proteins</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0021" view="all">Within the 25 SUL-DUR-resistant isolates, 17 (68%) were found to encode PBP3 and exhibit a series of substitutions comparable to those of the wildtype sequences; PBP3 was the primary target of SUL in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> 
                           <ce:cross-ref id="crf0049" refid="bib0025">[25]</ce:cross-ref>. Five different PBP3 substitutions, relative to <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> ATCC 17978, were identified, two of which were identified in 2 (T526S) and 12 (A515V) isolates, respectively. The latter substitutions have been previously shown to be associated with resistance to SUL-DUR [<ce:cross-refs id="crf0050" refid="bib0019 bib0020 bib0021">19–21</ce:cross-refs>, <ce:cross-ref id="crf0052" refid="bib0026">26</ce:cross-ref>]. The remaining three isolates harbored N392T, I517N, and V565L substitutions in their PBP3 sequences which, to our knowledge, have not been previously described as a cause or contributing factor to antibiotic resistance.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para0022" view="all">Because no substitutions could be identified within the PBP3 sequence compared with the reference sequence in eight isolates, the sequences of other PBPs were analyzed accordingly, and substitutions were investigated both by comparison with the reference genome ATCC 17978 and by BLAST analyses. Six strains had substitutions in PBP2, a target for DUR <ce:cross-ref id="crf0053" refid="bib0007">[7]</ce:cross-ref>, including Q106L (n = 2), P665A (n = 3), and P662T (n = 1). However, BLAST analyses revealed that only P662T could be considered an ‘uncommon’ substitution if we consider &lt;10 BLAST hits to be ‘uncommon’. Within PBP1a, another SUL target, one isolate harbored a G181S substitution and two isolates had T38A, A244T, Q644K, and T776A changes, but only the G181S and Q644K were found to be ‘uncommon’ when compared with sequences in GenBank. Nineteen isolates carried substitutions within PBP1b: 15 with P112S; 2 with M726V; 1 with N513H; and one isolate showing both P112S and P545L substitutions. However, only the P545L substitution could be considered potentially significant. Apart from the known PBP targets (PBP1a, 1b, 2, and 3) for SUL and DUR, one isolate (N933) harbored a transposon insertion after T302 in PBP6b.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para0023" view="all">The role of these substitutions, excepting the previously reported PBP3 mutations (T526S and A515V), have yet to be confirmed biochemically with respect to resistance to SUL-DUR. However, if we consider only ‘uncommon’ or known substitutions within the known SUL (PBPs 1a, 1b and 3) or DUR (PBP2) targets, then only two isolates, N612 and R3396, did not show significant PBP substitutions relative to the reference genome <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> ATCC 17978.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="sec0014" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.5</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0021">Outer membrane proteins</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0024" view="all">No significant mutations or disruptions in major porin encoding genes (<ce:italic>ompA, carO, ompW</ce:italic>, and <ce:italic>oprD)</ce:italic> were detected by analysis of sequences obtained by WGS (data not shown). Of particular note was the high level of sequence conservation between SUL-DUR-resistant and SUL-DUR-susceptible strains for <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic>, despite this porin being reported to be involved in DUR permeation into <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> cells <ce:cross-ref id="crf0054" refid="bib0027">[27]</ce:cross-ref>. This suggests that SUL-DUR resistance in the isolates from this study is not related to <ce:italic>ompA</ce:italic>-mediated uptake.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="sec0015" view="all">
                        <ce:label>3.2.6</ce:label>
                        <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0022">Efflux</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para0025" view="all">The sequences of efflux genes, belonging to the Resistance-Nondulation–Division (RND) family of efflux pumps, were investigated for any common genotype amongst the SUL-DUR resistant strains. Notably, four and one isolates were missing the <ce:italic>adeC</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>adeH</ce:italic> genes, respectively, which both encode the outer membrane components of the AdeABC and AdeFGH efflux pumps. However, the absence of the <ce:italic>adeC</ce:italic> gene has been reported to be relatively common amongst <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> isolates <ce:cross-ref id="crf0055" refid="bib0028">[28]</ce:cross-ref>. In three isolates, the two-component system <ce:italic>adeRS</ce:italic>, which regulates the expression of <ce:italic>adeAB</ce:italic>, was absent and the <ce:italic>adeS</ce:italic> gene was truncated in another isolate. The <ce:italic>adeA</ce:italic> gene was also truncated in four isolates. The mutations or sequence variations observed in the efflux components in these isolates are unlikely to play a role in SUL-DUR resistance because such mutations or variants are usually associated with increased susceptibility to antimicrobials <ce:cross-ref id="crf0056" refid="bib0029">[29]</ce:cross-ref>. While this work was in progress, a recent study reported that efflux systems likely play no role in SUL-DUR resistance <ce:cross-ref id="crf0057" refid="bib0030">[30]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec0016" role="conclusion" view="all">
                  <ce:label>4</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="cesectitle0023">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para0026" view="all">SUL-DUR was shown in this study to have notable in vitro antibacterial activity against a representative set of MDR-Ab isolates. It was found to be superior to all comparator agents tested with the exception of colistin. Twenty-five isolates were resistant to SUL-DUR in this study, mechanisms of which remain to be fully elucidated. However, we did identify mechanisms which may be contributing factors, such as PBP substitutions and the production of specific ß-lactamases, namely <ce:italic>blaTEM-1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>blaADC</ce:italic> variants. These results suggest that SUL-DUR could be an effective treatment option for infections caused by MDR-Ab, although the mechanisms of SUL-DUR resistance that were observed in 25 isolates remain to be fully elucidated. Therefore, further study of resistance mechanisms to SUL-DUR is imperative.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
