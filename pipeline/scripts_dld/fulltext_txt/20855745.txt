Abstract 

A multidrug efflux pump designated LmrS (lincomycin resistance protein of Staphylococcus aureus ), belonging to the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) of transporters, was cloned, and the role of LmrS in antimicrobial efflux was evaluated. The highest relative increase in MIC, 16-fold, was observed for linezolid and tetraphenylphosphonium chloride (TPCL), followed by an 8-fold increase for sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), trimethoprim, and chloramphenicol. LmrS has 14 predicted membrane-spanning domains and is homologous to putative lincomycin resistance proteins of Bacillus spp., Lactobacillus spp., and Listeria spp. 

Staphylococcus aureus is an important pathogenic bacterium responsible for an array of infections ranging from superficial skin infections to serious necrotizing pneumonia, toxic shock syndrome, bacteremia, endocarditis, and surgical wound infections ( 14 ). The success of S. aureus as a serious pathogen has also been due to its ability to develop resistance to a wide variety of antimicrobial compounds. S. aureus strains that are resistant to monovalent cationic antimicrobials, bivalent quaternary ammonium compounds, macrolides, quinolones, tetracycline, and β-lactams have widely been described. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is well known as an agent of nosocomial infections, and MRSA that is also resistant to vancomycin (VRSA) has been reported from all over the world (reviewed in references 40 and 47 ). More recently, the emergence of community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) has given a new dimension to the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria as a better-evolved pathogen ( 4 ). The resistance to structurally different antimicrobials involves alteration of the drug target sites, inactivation of the drug, reduction in cellular permeability, and bacterial efflux pumps ( 25 ). Multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux pumps extrude a wide range of structurally dissimilar substrates, while a few are substrate specific and extrude small amounts of selective antimicrobial compounds ( 30 ). The genes encoding multidrug efflux pumps are generally found on the bacterial chromosome, while those encoding selective drug efflux pumps are found on transferable genetic elements or plasmids ( 32 ). Several multidrug efflux genes from the S. aureus chromosome have been identified and characterized, such as the NorA, NorB, and NorC genes, which confer resistance to quinolones; the Tet38 gene, which confers resistance to tetracyclines; and the MsrA gene ( 7 , 10 , 18 , 24 , 42 ). The plasmid-borne qac genes ( qacA , - B , and - C ), often found in clinical isolates, encode efflux proteins responsible for resistance to quaternary ammonium compounds (Qac) and other cationic biocides ( 22 , 26 ). Other plasmid-borne qac genes ( qacG , - H , and - J ) are present predominantly in food-borne and veterinary isolates of S. aureus ( 3 , 13 ). 

The major facilitator superfamily (MFS) is the largest group of solute transporters, comprised of 58 families which function to transport diverse molecules such as sugars, amino acids, vitamins, Krebs cycle intermediates, etc. ( 17 , 32 ). MFS transporters are secondary active transporters with single-polypeptide chains containing 400 to 600 amino acids that transport small solutes across the membrane by using electrochemical gradients. Although the families in the MFS are quite diverse from one another, sequence similarity between members within families is highly significant ( 30 ). 

In the study reported here, we identified a putative gene, lmrS , encoding a membrane protein with 14 predicted transmembrane helices characteristic of the MFS family of proteins. We hypothesized that LmrS is a multidrug efflux pump with drug/H + antiporter activity. The results of this study suggest that LmrS is indeed an efflux protein capable of extruding multiple and structurally unrelated antimicrobial compounds. 

Identification, cloning, and analysis of a putative efflux gene. 

lmrS was identified in the whole genome sequence of Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus COL (GenBank accession no. CP000046 ), corresponding to the coordinates 2236817 to 2235375. The pure genomic DNA was isolated from methicillin-resistant S. aureus OM505 ( 38 ) by using a commercial kit (Epicentre Biotechnologies, Madison, WI). lmrS was amplified using primers 5′-GC AAGCTT ATGGCTAAAGTTGAATTAACAAC-3′ and 5′-GC GGATCC TTAAAATTTCCTTCTATTACTTT-3′, containing, respectively, HindIII and BamHI restriction sites (underlined). The PCR product was digested with BamHI and HindIII, separated on a 0.7% agarose gel, purified from the gel using a commercial gel extraction kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA), and ligated into similarly digested pSP72 (Promega, Madison, WI) by using a quick ligation kit (Fermentas, MD). The ligation mix was electrotransformed into an antibiotic-hypersensitive strain of Escherichia coli KAM32 devoid of major efflux pumps ydhE and hsd ( 29 ), and the transformants were selected on LB agar containing 100 μg/ml ampicillin to obtain KAM32/pSP72 lmrS . The antimicrobial resistance conferred by LmrS in KAM32/pSP72 lmrS was determined by the broth microdilution method of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) ( 5 ). E. coli KAM32 containing the plasmid vector alone ( E. coli KAM32/pSP72) was used as the control. Each broth microdilution experiment was repeated four times. Relative fold increases were calculated by dividing the mean MIC of KAM32/pSP72 lmrS by the mean MIC of KAM32/pSP72. Antimicrobial profiling of KAM32/pSP72 lmrS revealed high antibiotic resistance to lincomycin (MIC of 125 μg/ml), kanamycin (MIC of 125 μg/ml), and fusidic acid (MIC of 250 μg/ml) (Table 1 ). Likewise, KAM32/pSP72 lmrS showed high resistance to other antimicrobials, such as the surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (MIC of 250 μg/ml) and tetraphenylphosphonium chloride (TPCL) (MIC of 156.25 μg/ml). The highest relative increase in MIC, 16-fold, was for TPCL and the antibiotic linezolid (MIC of 31.25 μg/ml). An 8-fold increase was observed for the following antimicrobials: SDS, ethidium bromide, trimethoprim, florfenicol, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, streptomycin, fusidic acid, and kanamycin.

We also tested the effect of subinhibitory concentrations (MIC 50 ) of known efflux pump inhibitors carbonyl cyanide m -chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) (MIC of 8 μg/ml for E. coli KAM32) and reserpine (MIC of 62 μg/ml for E. coli KAM32) on LmrS. In the presence of 4 μg/ml CCCP, the MIC of fusidic acid for KAM32/pSP72 lmrS was lowered from 250 μg/ml to 62.5 μg/ml (factor of 4). However, the MICs of linezolid and kanamycin increased by factors of 2 and 1.5, respectively, while CCCP did not change the MIC of lincomycin for KAM32/pSP72 lmrS . Reserpine at 31.25 μg/ml lowered the MIC of fusidic acid from 250 to 31.25 (factor of 8) and the MIC of kanamycin from 125 to 93.75 (factor of 1.3) but did not have any effect on lincomycin and linezolid efflux by LmrS. A recent study has reported that reserpine can select for multiple-antibiotic-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae strains ( 12 ). Similarly, we speculate that at subinhibitory concentrations, CCCP and reserpine act as efflux substrates for LmrS and thus lead to enhanced efflux when used in combination with antibiotics which are also substrates for the same efflux pump. 

The 1,443-nucleotide-long lmrS encodes a putative protein of 480 amino acids with a calculated molecular mass of 47.69 kDa and an isoelectric point (pI) of 10.4. A BLAST analysis of LmrS revealed homology with putative lincomycin resistance proteins of several Staphylococcus species, such as Staphylococcus epidermidis , Staphylococcus warneri , Staphylococcus capitis , Staphylococcus haemolyticus (76% identity and 89% similarity), and Staphylococcus carnosus (64% identity and 83% similarity). Other Gram-positive bacteria with proteins homologous to LmrS include Enterococcus gallinarum , Lactobacillus spp., and several Bacillus and Listeria spp., such as Listeria monocytogenes , Listeria innocua , Bacillus pumilus , Bacillus coagulans , and Bacillus cereus (44% identity and 65% similarity). 

Alignment of LmrS with putative lincomycin resistance proteins of other Gram-positive bacteria revealed highly conserved motifs characteristic of the MFS family. A phylogenetic tree showing LmrS in relation to other lincomycin-resistant proteins is depicted in Fig. 1 . LmrS of S. aureus clustered closely with a homologous protein from S. warneri . The putative lincomycin resistance proteins of Gram-positive bacteria from other genera formed distinct clusters.

Ethidium bromide accumulation assay. 

An ethidium bromide accumulation assay was performed by growing E. coli KAM32/pSP72 lmrS and E. coli KAM32/pSP72 at 37°C in LB broth containing 20 mM potassium lactate and 100 μg/ml ampicillin until the mid-log phase (optical density at 625 nm [OD 625 ] of 0.5). Cells were washed twice in M9 minimal salts, and the OD was adjusted to 0.2. Culture (1.5 ml) was added to the cuvette before measurement of the baseline cellular fluorescence for 60 s. Following this, ethidium bromide was added to a final concentration of 5 μM, and the fluorescence was measured using an F-2500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) at emission and excitation wavelengths of 580 nm and 500 nm, respectively. Ethidium bromide fluoresces upon binding to the double-stranded DNA. Following this, the protonophore carbonyl cyanide m -chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) was added to a final concentration of 100 μM to disrupt the electrochemical gradient across the membrane. Levels of accumulation of ethidium bromide varied significantly between KAM32/pSP72 and KAM32/pSP72 lmrS , with a 30% decrease in accumulation of ethidium bromide in cells containing LmrS (Fig. 2 ). Upon addition of CCCP at approximately 300 s, the electrochemical gradient across the membrane was destroyed and ethidium bromide accumulation increased to 100%, shown by percent fluorescence. There were no significant changes seen in the KAM32/pSP72 control cells upon the addition of CCCP.

Ethidium bromide efflux assay. 

For the ethidium bromide efflux assay, the cells were grown as described above, washed, and resuspended in M9 minimal salts mixed with 10 μM ethidium bromide and 0.01 mM CCCP. This effectively loaded the cells with ethidium bromide after an incubation time of 6 min at 37°C. After incubation, cells were pelleted at 13,000 rpm for 1 min, resuspended in M9 minimal salts mixed with 10 μM ethidium bromide, and measured immediately using the F-2500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Baseline fluorescence was measured for 100 s. To induce efflux activity, an energy source was given to the cells, whereby cellular metabolism could be initiated, hence creating an electrochemical gradient across the plasma membrane through aerobic metabolism. To do this, potassium lactate (20 mM) was added, and the fluorescence was measured immediately for an additional 240 s. For removal of the proton gradient, CCCP was added to a final concentration of 100 μM, and fluorescence was measured a third time, for 400 s. All fluorescence assays were done in accordance with previously described protocols ( 39 ), with minor modifications. The ethidium bromide efflux assay showed a 40% decrease in fluorescence for E. coli KAM32/pSP72 lmrS compared to that for the E. coli KAM32/pSP72 control cells, suggesting an active extrusion of ethidium bromide from the cells harboring lmrS (Fig. 3 ). Following the addition of the protonophore CCCP, the fluorescence of both the control and the experimental cells increased due to the collapse of the proton gradient across the membrane. This phenomenon provides indirect evidence that suggests H + -dependent activity of LmrS.

RT-PCR. 

We studied the expression of lmrS in 4 clinical strains of Staphylococcus aureus in our collection ( 36 ). Following 12 h of growth in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth, total RNA was extracted using a RiboPure RNA extraction kit (Ambion, TX), and 500 ng of the DNase-treated total RNA was converted to cDNA by using an iScript select cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, CA). Oligonucleotides Lmr-RT1 (TGGTCGTGTAATGGCTGGTTCACT) and Lmr-RT2 (AGCTGCCATAACAAGCACAGCTAC) were used to amplify a 180-bp region of lmrS in a 25-μl reaction volume consisting of 12.5 μl of Brilliant II SYBR green QPCR master mix (Stratagene, CA) and 1 μl of the cDNA in a MyiQ real-time PCR (RT-PCR) detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). cDNA synthesized from E. coli total RNA and templateless SYBR green master mix were used as negative controls. The expression levels were compared using threshold cycle ( C T ) values. The real-time PCR results are suggestive of the constitutive nature of lmrS gene expression. For Staphylococcus aureus OM505, from which the lmrS gene was cloned, the mean threshold cycle value was 18.9. The expression levels of lmrS in three other clinical strains, S. aureus 584, S. aureus 209, and S. aureus 623, were similar, with mean C T values of 17.4, 20.3, and 21.6, respectively. 

In this study, we characterized an efflux pump (LmrS) of the major facilitator superfamily from S. aureus and produced strong evidence that it is a secondary active multidrug efflux pump. The secondary structure of LmrS has 14 predicted transmembrane domains. QacA, found in multidrug-resistant S. aureus , is a well-characterized efflux protein that contains 14 transmembrane segments (TMS) ( 31 , 33 ). A homologous protein, EmrB, in E. coli confers resistance to multiple antibiotics ( 11 , 19 ). VceA and VceB, identified in Vibrio cholerae , are homologous to EmrB, with 14 TMS that confer resistance to a number of antibiotics, including chloramphenicol and nalidixic acid ( 6 ). Comparison of LmrS with the membrane proteins in the Transport Classification Database (TCDB) revealed homologies with the lincomycin resistance protein LmrB of Bacillus subtilis (39% identity and 62% similarity), YhcA of Erwinia chrysanthemi (26% identity and 48% similarity), the efflux pump protein FarB of Neisseria gonorrhoeae and EmrB of E. coli (25% identity and 45% similarity), Vce of V. cholerae (21% identity and 47% similarity), and the tetracycline-resistant protein Tet38 of S. aureus (23% identity and 42% similarity). Whole genome sequencing of numerous Gram-positive and -negative bacteria has enabled the identification of homologous efflux proteins in the genomes of these bacteria. A BLAST analysis of LmrS against the GenBank database revealed the presence of efflux proteins with considerable sequence similarity in the genomes of, mainly, Gram-positive bacteria. Multiple sequence alignment of LmrS with homologous proteins from S. epidermidis , Bacillus subtilis , Lactobacillus plantarum , and L. monocytogenes identified conserved motifs characteristic of efflux proteins with 14 TMS. A translocase consensus motif (motif A) was identified in the loop between TMS-2 and TMS-3 (Fig. 4 ). This sequence, GVMIPLTAYLMDR, does not fully comply with the consensus sequence GxLaDrxGrkxxl found in MFS 12- and 14-TMS proteins (upper- and lowercase letters are suggestive of an amino acid present in >70% and >40% of the proteins, respectively, while x is indicative of any amino acid in that position) ( 32 ). A major deviation is the presence of alanine in the place of glycine at position 69. A previous study comparing the lincomycin resistance protein of B. subtilis with other drug transporters has found this residue to be highly conserved ( 16 ). The drug extrusion motif (motif C) identified on TMS-5 has an alanine instead of glycine at position 152 (Fig. 4 ). A previous study has shown glycine-to-alanine substitutions in about 10% of the 31 antiporters analyzed ( 44 ).

The lmrS gene cloned in a multicopy plasmid conferred resistance to a number of antimicrobials. Among the antibiotics tested, there was a 16-fold increase in MIC for the oxazolidinone drug linezolid. The MIC of 31.25 is close to the resistant concentration of 32 μg/ml ( 5 ). Linezolid is important in the treatment of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA), and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. (VRE). S. aureus resistance to linezolid has been reported previously ( 43 , 46 ). Linezolid resistance in Gram-positive bacteria has been attributed to a mutation at position 2576 of 23S rRNA. A nonribosomal mechanism of resistance in Mycobacterium smegmatis has been speculated to be due to either decreased uptake or increased efflux of the drug ( 34 , 38 ). The genome sequencing of a linezolid-resistant Streptococcus strain has identified putative antibiotic efflux proteins ( 9 ). Recently, we showed that in V. cholerae O395, the efflux pump EmrD-3 is responsible for the efflux of linezolid ( 39 ). EmrD-3 is an MFS protein with 12 TMS, homologues of which are present in some members of the Enterobacteriaceae family and the Gram-positive Bacillus spp. The alignment of LmrS with EmrD-3 revealed a sequence identity of 23% over a short stretch of 165 residues corresponding to amino acid residues 29 to 195 of LmrS. Within this stretch, the residues LMLARV (motif B) were found to be highly conserved between the two efflux pumps. 

The efficient extrusion of lincomycin by the E. coli strain harboring the cloned lmrS gene clearly demonstrates that lincomycin is a substrate for LmrS. The lincomycin resistance phenotype conferred by the lmrS gene further validates the observed sequence similarity of LmrS with lincomycin resistance proteins of Gram-positive bacteria, such as LmrB of B. subtilis . We conclude that LmrS also functions to efflux kanamycin, as evidenced by a MIC of 125 μg/ml for E. coli harboring the cloned lmrS gene. In Gram-negative bacteria, efflux pumps of the RND type have been shown to confer elevated resistance to aminoglycosides. These include AcrD of E. coli ( 48 ), MexY of Pseudomonas aeruginosa ( 2 , 45 ), AmrB of Burkholderia pseudomallei ( 23 ), the AdeABC system of Acinetobacter baumannii ( 21 ), and OpmG and OpmI of P. aeruginosa ( 15 ). MFS efflux proteins responsible for aminoglycoside efflux have been identified, such as MdfA of E. coli ( 8 ) and proteins encoded by the tap and p55 genes of Mycobacterium fortuitum and Mycobacterium bovis , respectively ( 1 , 37 ). LmrS is distinct and does not share any sequence homology to these efflux proteins. Kanamycin resistance in Gram-positive bacteria is mediated by efflux proteins. In S. aureus , the plasmid (pSK1)-borne qac genes are responsible for aminoglycoside efflux ( 41 ). LmrS is distinct from these known efflux proteins, since our amino acid comparisons did not reveal significant similarity. 

An interesting feature of LmrS is its ability to extrude fusidic acid and SDS (MIC of 250 μg/ml). Fusidic acid, an inhibitor of elongation factor G, has strong activity against MRSA, with a MIC of 0.25 μg/ml ( 35 ). The fusidic acid resistance phenotype of S. aureus has been attributed to plasmid-borne genes or to chromosomal mutations ( 27 , 28 ). In E. coli , AcrD confers moderate resistance to fusidic acid ( 20 ). 

In conclusion, the results of our study suggest that the efflux protein LmrS is an important determinant of antimicrobial resistance in S. aureus . The exact role of this protein in the physiology and pathogenesis of S. aureus requires further study of the distribution of this gene among clinical isolates and the level of lmrS expression that might contribute to the extent of antimicrobial resistance. Work is in progress to inactivate lmrS in an MRSA strain and to determine the functional significance of amino acid residues in motif B responsible for the efflux of linezolid and fusidic acid by site-directed mutagenesis.
