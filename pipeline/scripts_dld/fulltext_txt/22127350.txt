                  In this study, the occurrence and diversity of integrons were evaluated in 697 isolates belonging to Enterobacteriaceae and Aeromonas spp. isolated from urban wastewaters. Screening of integrons was performed by dot blot hybridization and intI-positive strains were further characterized. The global prevalence of integrons was 3.73%. Three new gene cassettes were identified: a novel aadA variant (aadA17), a gene putatively involved in cell signaling (dcyA) and an open reading frame of unknown function interrupted by a novel insertion sequence (orfER.17::ISAs12). In total, thirteen different gene cassette arrays were detected, 4 representing novel integrons: intI1-dcyA-tniC, intI1-orfER.1.7::ISAs12-aadA13-qacEΔ1-sul1, intI1-aacA4-catB3-bla
                     OxA-10-aadA1-qacEΔ1-sul1 and intI1-catB8-aadA17-qacEΔ1-sul1. Approximately 80% of strains were resistant to at least 3 antibiotics of different classes. The presence of novel integron structures in treated effluents suggests that domestic wastewaters may favor the formation of novel combinations of gene cassettes. Moreover, the high prevalence of multiresistant strains highlights the urgent need to employ effective means of effluent disinfection to avoid dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">In this study, the occurrence and diversity of integrons were evaluated in 697 isolates belonging to <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp. isolated from urban wastewaters. Screening of integrons was performed by dot blot hybridization and <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-positive strains were further characterized. The global prevalence of integrons was 3.73%. Three new gene cassettes were identified: a novel <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> variant (<ce:italic>aadA17</ce:italic>), a gene putatively involved in cell signaling (<ce:italic>dcyA</ce:italic>) and an open reading frame of unknown function interrupted by a novel insertion sequence (<ce:italic>orfER.17::</ce:italic>IS<ce:italic>As12</ce:italic>). In total, thirteen different gene cassette arrays were detected, 4 representing novel integrons: <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>dcyA</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>tniC</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>orfER.1.7</ce:italic>::IS<ce:italic>As12</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>aadA13</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>aacA4</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>catB3</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OxA-10</ce:inf>-<ce:italic>aadA1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>catB8</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>aadA17</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>Δ<ce:italic>1</ce:italic>-<ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>.                   <ce:label>1</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">Urban wastewater treatment usually consists of a combination of physical, chemical and biological procedures to eliminate or reduce suspended solids and organic matter loads from effluents, without damage to natural environments (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib33">Pescod, 1992</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">Recycling of treated wastewater and sludge in agriculture is encouraged by European Commission directives (86/278/EEC), owing to the presence of high concentrations of nutrients that reduces the requirements for inorganic fertilizers and consequently crop production costs. However, high nutrient loads and intensive selective pressures imposed by detergents and pharmaceuticals make wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) favorable sites for horizontal gene transfer events (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib28 bib30 bib42 bib52">Moura et al., 2007, 2010; Schluter et al., 2007b; Xu et al., 2007</ce:cross-refs>). It has been shown that antibiotics (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib52">Xu et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>) and antibiotic-resistant bacteria (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>) continue to persist in disturbing amounts in treated effluents due to incomplete metabolism of excreted antibiotics. Consequently, growing concern has arisen regarding the occurrence and diversity of mobile genetic elements in wastewaters and the possible role of these environments in the dissemination of resistance and/or virulence genetic traits which may reach natural waters, soils and eventually the food chain (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib26 bib30">Martinez, 2009; Moura et al., 2010</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Integrons are genetic systems that allow bacteria to capture and express gene cassettes. They typically consist of an <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> gene encoding for an integrase that catalyzes the incorporation or excision of gene cassettes by site-specific recombination, a recombination site <ce:italic>attI</ce:italic> and one or two promoters responsible for the expression of inserted gene cassettes (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib3">Cambray et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>). Integrons were first reported in clinical isolates in the 1980s and continue to be extensively studied in clinical environments, due to their association with other mobile genetic elements and multiresistance phenotypes (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib14 bib23">Hall et al., 1999; Leverstein-van Hall et al., 2002</ce:cross-refs>). However, in the last decade special attention has been given to integrons from natural environments in order to gather information on their ecology and diversity, and to understand their role in bacterial adaptation. Evidence that stress response may trigger the expression of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib13">Guerin et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>) and gene cassettes (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib27">Michael and Labbate, 2010</ce:cross-ref>) has been recently reported, suggesting that integrons may constitute important adaptive systems in bacterial evolution.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">Integron-related sequences have been found in a wide diversity of bacterial hosts, most of them (∼30%) belonging to γ-Proteobacteria (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">Moura et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>). The wide dispersion of integrons is probably a consequence of their association with other mobile genetic elements, which not only increases the genetic traits harbored but also the mobilizing events, contributing to the spread of such elements in the environment. Their diversity and abundance may also be related to the kind of selective pressures they encompass (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib51">Wright et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">In a previous study, we investigated the prevalence of integrons among enterobacteria and aeromonads isolated from a WWTP receiving waters from a slaughterhouse (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). The presence of integrase-carrying bacteria in the slaughterhouse’s wastewaters was determined to be 35%, a considerably high value compared to other aquatic environments (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib36 bib16">Rosser and Young, 1999; Henriques et al., 2006b</ce:cross-refs>). Moreover, nearly 50% of strains were resistant to 5 or more antibiotics, once again drawing attention to the misuse of antibiotics in veterinary therapeutics and for the role of WWTP as hotspots for horizontal gene transfer.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">The aim of the present study was to evaluate the occurrence and diversity of integrons in water samples from an urban WWTP in order to assess the influence of the type of effluent on the prevalence of integrons and on the diversity of their gene cassettes among <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> spp.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec2" view="all">
                  <ce:label>2</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Materials and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.1" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Sampling and bacterial isolation</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">Sampling was performed in November 2007 in an activated sludge plant receiving domestic effluents, located in Ermesinde in the north of Portugal. Water samples were collected in 0.5 l autoclaved bottles at 5 points throughout the WWTP: raw water (RW), a primary decantation tank (PD), an aeration tank (AT), sludge recirculation (SR) and final effluent (FE). Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD<ce:inf loc="post">5</ce:inf>), chemical oxygen demand (COD), total suspended solids (TSS) and pH were determined by standard methods (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib5">Clescerl et al., 1998</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">Serial decimal dilutions of water samples were prepared in 0.9% NaCl and filtered through 0.45 μm-pore size cellulose sterile filters (Pall Life Sciences, MI, USA). Filters were placed onto glutamate starch phenol red (GSP) agar plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) selective for <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> and MacConkey agar for <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Duplicate sets of plates were incubated for 24 h at 30 °C and 37 °C. All individual colonies were picked from the following dilutions: 10<ce:sup loc="post">−4</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−5</ce:sup> in raw waters, 10<ce:sup loc="post">−3</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−7</ce:sup> in a primary decantation tank, 10<ce:sup loc="post">−4</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−5</ce:sup> in an aeration tank, 10<ce:sup loc="post">−6</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−8</ce:sup> in sludge recirculation and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−2</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−2</ce:sup> in final effluent, for MacConkey and GSP agar, respectively.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.2" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Integron screening and identification of integrase-positive isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">Oligonucleotides used during this study are listed in <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>. Bacterial colonies were screened for the presence of <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>intI2</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> genes by dot blot hybridization, as described previously (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). Colonies giving positive hybridization signals were further purified by continuing isolation in MacConkey and GSP agar. Molecular typing of integrase-positive isolates was carried out by REP-PCR (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib49">Versalovic et al., 1991</ce:cross-ref>). Identification of isolates representing different REP profiles was achieved by amplification and sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib22">Lane, 1991</ce:cross-ref>). Identification of <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> isolates to the species level was achieved by partial amplification (∼950 bp) and sequencing of the <ce:italic>gyrB</ce:italic> gene (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib53">Yanez et al., 2003</ce:cross-ref>). Sequence similarity searches were conducted using BLAST software (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib1">Altschul et al., 1997</ce:cross-ref>) and nucleotide sequences were aligned by CLUSTAL X (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib47">Thompson et al., 1997</ce:cross-ref>). Phylogenetic trees were constructed by the neighbor-joining method (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib37">Saitou and Nei, 1987</ce:cross-ref>) using MEGA version 4.0 (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib45">Tamura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.3" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Integron characterization and genetic location</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">Integron characterization was carried out through PCR amplification with primers targeting class 1 and class 2 integron variable regions (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib24 bib50">Levesque et al., 1995; White et al., 2001</ce:cross-refs>), as previously described (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). To determine the structure of the 3′-segment of class 1 integrons, the presence of <ce:italic>qacE</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>sul1</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>sul3</ce:italic>, IS<ce:italic>CR1</ce:italic> (orf513) and <ce:italic>tniC</ce:italic> (<ce:italic>tniR</ce:italic>) genes was also tested (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">Strains that failed to amplify integron variable regions with primers 5′CS/3′CS (class 1 integrons) and hep74/hep51 (class 2 integrons) were further subjected to amplification using different combinations of primers (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>) and the Extensor Long PCR Master Mix (ABgene, UK) according to manufacturer’s instructions.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">To determine integron location, total genomic DNA and plasmid DNA were purified, separated by electrophoresis and transferred onto positive-charged membranes, as described previously (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). Southern blots were probed using <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-labeled fragments, as described previously (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.4" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Conjugative gene transfer</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">Strains containing plasmid-borne integrons were used as donors in mating assays using rifampicin- and kanamycin-resistant <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> CV601-GFP as recipient strain (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib44">Smalla et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>). Transconjugants were selected on TSA plates containing rifampicin (100 mg/l), kanamycin (30 mg/l) and streptomycin (50 mg/l) and confirmed by REP-PCR and amplification of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> genes, as previously described (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.5" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.5</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title>Antibiotic susceptibility testing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">Integrase-positive isolates were tested for antibiotic susceptibility using the disk diffusion method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib6">CLSI, 2005</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> ATTC 29522 was used as control strain.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">The following antimicrobials were tested: ampicillin (AMP, 10 μg), aztreonam (ATM, 30 μg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 μg), cefalotin (CEF, 30 μg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 μg), chloramphenicol (CHL, 30 μg), erythromycin (ERY, 15 μg), gentamicin (GEN, 10 μg), imipenem (IPM, 10 μg), kanamycin (KAN, 30 μg), nalidixic acid (NAL, 30 μg), streptomycin (STR, 10 μg), tetracycline (TET, 30 μg) and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (STX, 25 μg) (Oxoid, Basingstoke, United Kingdom).</ce:para>

                     <ce:para id="p0085" view="all">Nucleotide sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers: FJ464567-FJ464590 (16S <ce:italic>rRNA</ce:italic> gene), FJ238489-FJ238507 (<ce:italic>gyrB</ce:italic>), FJ460175-FJ460183 and HQ170510-HQ170518 (gene cassette arrays).</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="sec3" view="all">
                  <ce:label>3</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title>Results and discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0090" view="all">The occurrence and diversity of integron-carrying bacteria in wastewaters is a matter of concern due to the possible role of these environments in the perpetuation and dissemination of harmful genetic traits in soils and aquatic systems. In this study, the prevalence of integrons was assessed in a set of bacterial isolates from an urban WWTP. We focused on two bacterial groups often associated with mobile genetic elements: aeromonads, ubiquitous in aquatic environments and enterobacteria, common inhabitants of polluted waters (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib12">Goñi-Urriza et al., 2000</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0095" view="all">A total of 697 bacterial isolates were obtained (292 on MacConkey-agar and 405 on GSP-agar) from each of the following treatment stages: RW (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 95), PD (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 193), AT (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 169), SR (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 109) and FE (<ce:italic>n</ce:italic> = 131). Physico-chemical characterization of samples collected is shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>. Final effluent concentrations were in conformity with national emission standards of water quality.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0100" view="all">As shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>, the prevalence of integrons in urban wastewaters (3.73%) was much lower than that previously reported using the same methodology in a WWTP receiving effluents from a slaughterhouse (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), suggesting that the type of effluent may exert great influence on the occurrence of integrons.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0105" view="all">Integrase genes were detected in isolates from all samples except in sludge recirculation. Prevalence of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-positive isolates ranged between 7.4% (7/95) in influents (sampling point RW) and 4.6% (6/131) in treated effluents (sampling point FE). The overall prevalence of <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic> was 3.59% (25/697), and was more abundant in raw waters. Genes encoding IntI2 were present in 0.14% (1/697), and were detected only in aeration tanks. No <ce:italic>intI3</ce:italic> genes were detected. Results showed that, although secondary treatment (sampling point AT) was efficient at reducing the organic content, it was not effective in eliminating integron-carrying bacteria from treated effluent (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). Nevertheless, the decrease in <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> prevalence in sludge recirculation suggests that longer retention times and/or lower O<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf> concentrations may contribute to the elimination of integron-carrying bacteria. These results are in accordance with those obtained in lab-scale reactors testing different digestion conditions for sewage sludge stabilization (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib10">Ghosh et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0110" view="all">Twenty-four out of 26 <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-positive isolates displayed different REP profiles and were further characterized (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). The majority of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-positive strains (∼80%) belonged to the genus <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic>, emphasizing the importance of these natural water-borne bacteria as potential vectors of dissemination of integrons and antibiotic resistance determinants.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0115" view="all">Seventeen gene cassette arrays were found among class 1 integrons, harboring 1 to 4 gene cassettes (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). In addition, three fragments of 0.15 kb generated with primers 5′CS/3′CS corresponded to empty integrons, i.e. integrons with no gene cassettes inserted. It is noteworthy that in first attempts using primers 5′CS/3′CS and conventional <ce:italic>Taq</ce:italic> polymerase, amplification of integron-associated variable regions failed in 50% of strains. Afterward, several combinations of primers were tested, most of them using different primers targeting the 3′-segment of integrons, as well as specialized DNA polymerases for long-range PCR, which led to reducing the number of uncharacterized integrons. The use of this approach should be considered, especially in non-clinical environments, in order to maximize data retrieved and to minimize bias introduced by the use of primers designed for clinical settings.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0120" view="all">In this study, and contrary to previous observations on slaughterhouse effluents (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), none of the arrays detected were common to both <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic>. Nevertheless, a higher abundance of gene cassette combinations was found than those previously reported in slaughterhouse effluents. This increased diversity of integron structures may be related to a higher diversity of bacterial hosts inhabiting these wastewaters. As observed in a previous study based on culture-independent techniques, this urban effluent was reported as having higher bacterial community diversity, contrary to slaughterhouse wastewater (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0125" view="all">The most frequent gene cassettes detected were <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic>-type genes, conferring resistance to streptomycin and spectinomycin. In fact, <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic> is the most often reported gene cassette in bacterial isolates (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://integrall.bio.ua.pt/" xlink:type="simple">http://integrall.bio.ua.pt/</ce:inter-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">Moura et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>) and regardless of the limited use of such antibiotics at present, these gene cassettes seem to persist in the environment. Other gene cassettes involved in antibiotic resistance included: <ce:italic>aacA4</ce:italic> (encoding resistance to gentamicin and tobramycin), <ce:italic>aacA4-cr</ce:italic> (resistance to amikacin, tobramycin and to fluoroquinolones), <ce:italic>dfrA</ce:italic>-type genes (resistance to trimethoprim), <ce:italic>catB</ce:italic> (resistance to chloramphenicol), <ce:italic>arr</ce:italic>3 (resistance to rifampicin), <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-1</ce:inf> (resistance to penicillins and oxacillins) and extended-spectrum beta-lactamases <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">GES-7</ce:inf>, <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-10</ce:inf> type genes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0130" view="all">New gene cassettes and integrons were detected (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/>). A novel <ce:italic>aadA</ce:italic>-variant (named <ce:italic>aadA17</ce:italic>) was detected in <ce:italic>Aeromonas media</ce:italic> ER.1.18 downstream of the <ce:italic>catB8</ce:italic> gene cassette, differing from the <ce:italic>aadA12</ce:italic> encoding protein by six amino acid substitutions: Pro(26)Ser, His(45)Tyr, Phe(33)Leu, Arg(47)Asn, Lys(51)Thr, Gln(56)Pro. Strain ER.1.18 did not exhibit resistance to chloramphenicol, although resistance to streptomycin was observed. The deduced CatB8 protein differs from those previously reported by one conserved amino acid substitution, Gln(160)His. Although Gln and His possess similar physical–chemical properties (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib8">Frappat et al., 2002</ce:cross-ref>). This substitution may affect enzyme efficiency, leading to chloramphenicol susceptibility. However, further investigation is needed to clarify this subject.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0135" view="all">In <ce:italic>Aeromonas caviae</ce:italic> strain ER1.20, a novel gene cassette was identified upstream of the <ce:italic>tniC</ce:italic> gene encoding the Tn<ce:italic>402</ce:italic>-resolvase. The gene cassette consisted of an open reading frame of 1083 bp and an <ce:italic>attC</ce:italic> site of 75 bp and was tentatively designated <ce:italic>dcyA</ce:italic> (<ce:underline>d</ce:underline>iguanylate <ce:underline>cy</ce:underline>clase <ce:underline>A</ce:underline>). The putative protein DcyA of 360 aa consists of a globin-coupled-sensor (GCS) domain at the amino terminus and a diguanylate cyclase (DGC) domain at the carboxyl terminus and showed 65% identity (BLASTP Expect = 9e<ce:sup loc="post">−135</ce:sup>) with a diguanylate cyclase from <ce:italic>Shewanella baltica</ce:italic> (accession no. ABS06187). GCS domains are predominantly present in Gram-negative α-Proteobacteria, but also in the Firmicutes and Archaea (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib9">Freitas et al., 2003</ce:cross-ref>). GCS domains detect fluctuations in intracellular concentrations of oxygen, carbon monoxide, or nitrous oxide and induce signal cascades as specific adaptive responses, which result in aerotaxis and/or gene regulation (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib46">Thijs et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). DGC domains (initially described as domains of unknown function 1, DUF1) are widely present in bacteria and are thought to constitute a general regulatory principle in bacterial growth and development (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib19">Jenal, 2004</ce:cross-ref>). GCS domains have also been implicated in cell signaling, regulation of bacterial cellular adhesiveness and biofilm formation (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib19">Jenal, 2004</ce:cross-ref>). It has been suggested that environmental stress can induce the formation of biofilms (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib31">O’Toole and Stuart, 2005</ce:cross-ref>). In addition to the induction of integrase expression by SOS response (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib13">Guerin et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>), our results suggest that gene cassettes may also be actively involved in the bacterial stress response.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0140" view="all">Novel gene cassettes possessing no putative conserved domains, and thus with unknown function, were also detected. The gene cassette <ce:italic>orfER.1.7</ce:italic> found i<ce:italic>n Aeromonas salmonicida</ce:italic> was disrupted by a novel type of insertion sequence (IS) element. <ce:italic>orfER.1.7</ce:italic> consisting of an <ce:italic>attC</ce:italic> site of 75 bp and an open reading frame of 660 bp (219 aa), showing low amino acid similarity (&lt;28%) with other protein sequences deposited in public databases. The IS was 1460 bp long and belonged to the IS<ce:italic>As1</ce:italic> family. It was designated IS<ce:italic>As12</ce:italic>, according to ISFinder nomenclature (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://www-is.biotoul.fr" xlink:type="simple">www-is.biotoul.fr</ce:inter-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib43">Siguier et al., 2006</ce:cross-ref>). IS<ce:italic>As12</ce:italic> (accession no. HQ170513) was bounded by 26 bp imperfect inverted repeats and flanked by a directly repeated 12 bp target sequence. IS<ce:italic>As12</ce:italic> contained an open reading frame of 1161 bp and is predicted to encode a putative transposase of 386 aa, sharing 94% of identity with a transposase found in <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> plasmid pKpQIL_p018 (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0145" view="all">Although antibiotic-resistant genes account for the larger majority of gene cassettes reported in bacterial isolates (<ce:inter-ref xlink:href="http://integrall.bio.ua.pt" xlink:type="simple">http://integrall.bio.ua.pt</ce:inter-ref>; <ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">Moura et al., 2009</ce:cross-ref>), especially in class 1 integrons, recent studies focusing on genetic libraries of total environmental DNA have reported that they correspond to a minor fraction of the total gene cassette pool. In fact, the majority of the gene cassettes found in those studies encode proteins with no homologs in the databases (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib7 bib20 bib30">Elsaied et al., 2007; Koenig et al., 2008; Moura et al., 2010</ce:cross-refs>). Results obtained in this study also reinforce the notion that integrons may also have an important role in other cellular processes, rather than just antibiotic resistance.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0150" view="all">Similarly to slaughterhouse effluent (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib28">Moura et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>), most integrases were located on bacterial chromosomes, although the prevalence of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> genes carried in plasmids (33.3%) was higher in domestic wastewaters. Nevertheless, <ce:italic>intI1</ce:italic> genes were transferred into <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> recipient cells at similar frequencies (10<ce:sup loc="post">−5</ce:sup> and 10<ce:sup loc="post">−6</ce:sup> transconjugants per recipient cell for <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> donors, respectively). Seven strains gave positive <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic> hybridization signals in both chromosomal and plasmid DNA; since only one gene cassette array was detected in these strains, results suggest the presence of at least two copies of those integrons.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0155" view="all">Urban effluents are known to contain high levels of antibiotics due to incomplete metabolism of excreted antibiotics (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib52">Xu et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>). Antimicrobial resistance phenotypes observed in this study are shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>. Multiresistance to 3 or more different antibiotic classes was displayed by nearly 80% (19/24) of <ce:italic>intI</ce:italic>-positive isolates, with 84.2% (16/19) of multiresistant strains belonging to <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> and 15.8% (3/19) belonging to <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic>. Although multiresistance phenotypes in clinical strains have been correlated with the presence of integrons (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib23">Leverstein-van Hall et al., 2002</ce:cross-ref>), this association has not been observed in isolates from wastewater environments (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib25">Li et al., 2010</ce:cross-ref>), suggesting that multiresistance is a common trait in wastewater bacteria even in the absence of integrons. The most frequent resistances observed were to AMP, CEF, NAL and ERY (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>). In addition to species-specific penicillinases and cephalosporinases occurring in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> species (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib12">Goñi-Urriza et al., 2000</ce:cross-ref>), resistance to AMP and CEF may also be explained by the presence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases that inactivate both penicillins and cephalosporins, such as GES-7, OXA-2 and OXA-10, detected in this study. On the other hand, macrolides such as ERY have been reported as one of the most frequently detected antibiotic at WWTP (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib52">Xu et al., 2007</ce:cross-ref>) and integrons embedded in erythromycin-resistance plasmids have been identified in municipal WWTP (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib41">Schluter et al., 2007a</ce:cross-ref>). Of particular concern is the high level of bacteria resistant to quinolones (NAL) and fluoroquinolones (CIP) observed in this study. Quinolone and fluoroquinolone resistance is usually due to DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV mutations that render antibiotics ineffective (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib4">Cattoir et al., 2008</ce:cross-ref>). Emerging mechanisms of fluoroquinolone resistance, such as <ce:italic>aacA4-cr</ce:italic> gene cassettes identified in this study in plasmid-borne integrons in <ce:italic>A. media</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Aeromonas allosaccharophila</ce:italic>, might constitute a growing concern, since resistance to these compounds is rarely reported in <ce:italic>Aeromonas</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">Janda and Abbott, 2010</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0160" view="all">In conclusion, results obtained suggest that the type of effluent affects both the prevalence and diversity of integrons in aeromonads and enterobacteria. In addition, new gene cassette arrays were found, including in the treated effluent, drawing attention to wastewaters, and in particular to domestic effluents as sources and reservoirs of novel integron structures. Moreover, the gene cassettes found, specifically <ce:italic>dcyA</ce:italic>, greatly increase current knowledge on the diversity of integron gene cassettes in Gram-negative bacteria, highlighting a putative role for integrons in biofilm formation.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0165" view="all">The high number of multiresistant isolates emphasizes the importance of wastewaters as vehicles of dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and the urgent need to employ effective means of effluent disinfection in wastewater treatment plants. Further investigation on the persistence of mobile genetic elements in wastewaters should continue in order to allow sustainable use of resources, minimizing the human impact on aquatic ecosystems and the perpetuation and dissemination of harmful genetic determinants in the environment.</ce:para>
