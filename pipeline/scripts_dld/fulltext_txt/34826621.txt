                  Objectives
                  To characterize Alcaligenes faecalis metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) AFM-2 and AFM-3 from clinical Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates NDTH10366, NDTH9845 and WTJH17.
               
                  Methods
                  Clinical isolates were whole-genome sequenced using the Illumina and Oxford Nanopore platforms. MICs of clinical isolates and transformants containing MBL genes were determined using broth microdilution methods. Kinetic parameters of purified AFM and NDM-1 were measured using a spectrophotometer. The AFM structure was modelled with SWISS-MODEL.
               
                  Results
                  NDTH10366 and NDTH9845 were extensively drug-resistant (XDR) isolates carrying bla
                     AFM-2 and multiple copies of bla
                     KPC-2, whereas WTJH17 was an XDR isolate carrying bla
                     AFM-3. The plasmid-borne bla
                     AFM-2 and bla
                     AFM-3 genes are associated with a novel ISCR element, ISCR29. AFM-2 and AFM-3, differing from AFM-1 by one amino acid substitution each, shared 86.2% and 86.6% amino acid sequence identity with NDM-1, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed the close relationship between AFM and NDM. Expression of AFM and NDM-1 under their native promoters in DH5α and PAO1 led to elevated MICs for all tested β-lactams except aztreonam. Comparable catalytic abilities were observed for AFM and NDM-1 when hydrolysing nitrocefin, cefepime, imipenem and biapenem, whereas for other tested β-lactams AFM displayed weaker enzymatic activities. Modelling AFM structure revealed a characteristic αβ/βα fold with two zinc-binding active sites.
               
                  Conclusions
                  AFM from clinical P. aeruginosa isolates demonstrated β-lactamase activity comparable to NDM-1. Co-carriage of bla
                     AFM and bla
                     KPC renders clinical P. aeruginosa isolates non-susceptible to all antipseudomonal β-lactams. The association of bla
                     AFM genes with translocatable genetic elements and plasmids highlights their concerning potential for dissemination.

                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0015">Objectives</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">To characterize <ce:italic>Alcaligenes faecalis</ce:italic> metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) AFM-2 and AFM-3 from clinical <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates NDTH10366, NDTH9845 and WTJH17.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="abssec0015" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0020">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0015" view="all">Clinical isolates were whole-genome sequenced using the Illumina and Oxford Nanopore platforms. MICs of clinical isolates and transformants containing MBL genes were determined using broth microdilution methods. Kinetic parameters of purified AFM and NDM-1 were measured using a spectrophotometer. The AFM structure was modelled with SWISS-MODEL.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec role="results" id="abssec0020" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0025">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0020" view="all">NDTH10366 and NDTH9845 were extensively drug-resistant (XDR) isolates carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> and multiple copies of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf>, whereas WTJH17 was an XDR isolate carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-3</ce:inf>. The plasmid-borne <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-3</ce:inf> genes are associated with a novel IS<ce:italic>CR</ce:italic> element, IS<ce:italic>CR29</ce:italic>. AFM-2 and AFM-3, differing from AFM-1 by one amino acid substitution each, shared 86.2% and 86.6% amino acid sequence identity with NDM-1, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed the close relationship between AFM and NDM. Expression of AFM and NDM-1 under their native promoters in DH5α and PAO1 led to elevated MICs for all tested β-lactams except aztreonam. Comparable catalytic abilities were observed for AFM and NDM-1 when hydrolysing nitrocefin, cefepime, imipenem and biapenem, whereas for other tested β-lactams AFM displayed weaker enzymatic activities. Modelling AFM structure revealed a characteristic αβ/βα fold with two zinc-binding active sites.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec role="conclusion" id="abssec0025" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0030">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0025" view="all">AFM from clinical <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates demonstrated β-lactamase activity comparable to NDM-1. Co-carriage of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC</ce:inf> renders clinical <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates non-susceptible to all antipseudomonal β-lactams. The association of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> genes with translocatable genetic elements and plasmids highlights their concerning potential for dissemination.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0040">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">Listed as a critical pathogen for which new antibiotics are urgently required [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib1" id="crosref0155">1</ce:cross-ref>], carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic> has become a major global health challenge. Metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) are by far the most common carbapenemases in carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic>, and can be further divided into subclasses B1a, B1b, B2 and B3 [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib2" id="crosref0160">2</ce:cross-ref>]. Among them, New Delhi MBL (NDM) is of particular concern because it is often carried by mobile genetic elements that also carry resistance determinants for other antibiotic classes [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib3" id="crosref0165">3</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">For much of the past decade, NDM was considered the only member of MBL subclass B1b. In 2018 another subclass B1b MBL was recognized in an <ce:italic>Alcaligenes faecalis</ce:italic> strain and named <ce:italic>
                        <ce:underline>A</ce:underline>lcaligenes <ce:underline>f</ce:underline>aecalis</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:underline>m</ce:underline>etallo-β-lactamase-1 (AFM-1) (GenBank accession no.: MK143105). Subsequently, AFM-1 has been found in <ce:italic>Comamonas testosteroni</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>Stenotrophomonas maltophilia</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Bordetella trematum</ce:italic> (GenBank accessions: MT011984, CP049956, CP049957). However, the biochemical properties of the AFM enzyme have not been described in detail.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Besides MBLs, class A carbapenemases are increasingly being identified in carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic>, especially <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> carbapenemase (KPC). The co-occurrence of KPC and MBL in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> can inactivate all β-lactams including monobactams, ceftazidime/avibactam and imipenem/relebactam, making clinical treatment extremely difficult.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">Here, we report two extensively drug-resistant <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> (XDR-PA) isolates carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> and multiple copies of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf>, and one XDR-PA isolate carrying <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-3</ce:inf>. We characterized AFM-2 and AFM-3 by comparing their catalytic abilities and their impacts on antimicrobial susceptibility with AFM-1 and NDM-1.</ce:para>

                     <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">Because the mature AFM-1/2/3 proteins are identical, only the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">NDM-1</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>

                  <ce:section id="sec3.6" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="sectitle0125">Comparison of AFM and NDM kinetic parameters</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0140" view="all">Hydrolysing abilities of AFM and NDM-1 were tested for aztreonam and other β-lactams (<ce:cross-ref refid="tbl2" id="crosref0370">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). As predicted, both AFM and NDM-1 had no catalytic effect on aztreonam. For all tested β-lactams except nitrocefin and ertapenem, AFM exhibited higher affinities but lower turnover numbers than NDM-1, which was indicated by <ce:italic>K</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">M</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">cat</ce:inf> values. Similar catalytic efficiencies between AFM and NDM-1 were observed for nitrocefin, cefepime, imipenem and biapenem, as indicated by <ce:italic>k</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">cat</ce:inf>/<ce:italic>K</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">M</ce:inf> values. However, compared with NDM-1, AFM displayed lower catalytic efficiencies for penicillin G, ampicillin, carbenicillin, cefuroxime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, meropenem and ertapenem.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec3.7" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="sectitle0130">Structural modelling of AFM</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0145" view="all">The structure of AFM was modelled using NDM-1 as the reference homology template. The overall fold of AFM was predicted to be the classic αβ/βα sandwich, which is common to all MBLs (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4" id="crosref0375">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/>). Two zinc ions bound in the active sites of AFM, with Zn1 interacted with histidine residues 117, 119 and 186 at coordination distances of 2.07 Å, 1.88 Å and 2.04 Å, repectively, and Zn2 interacted with residues Asp121, Cys205 and His247 at coordination distances of 2.05 Å, 2.42 Å and 1.92 Å (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4" id="crosref0380">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="sec4" role="discussion" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="sectitle0135">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0150" view="all">Clinical <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> isolates NDTH10366, NDTH9845 and WTJH17 are XDR and sensitive only to colistin. Our analysis of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2/3</ce:inf> from clinical isolates and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-1</ce:inf>-bearing sequences in GenBank allowed us to characterize the contexts of AFM determinants and assess their potential for horizontal dissemination. These genes are associated with a novel IS<ce:italic>CR</ce:italic> element, IS<ce:italic>CR29</ce:italic>, which is probably responsible for their mobility. IS<ce:italic>CR</ce:italic> elements have been responsible for the dissemination of multiple resistance determinants, including NDM gene variants [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib21" id="crosref0385">21</ce:cross-ref>]. Concerningly, the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> genes in pNDTH10366, pNDTH9845 and pWTJH17 are already found in close proximity to multiple copies of IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic>, which is arguably the most important translocatable element associated with antibiotic resistance genes [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib22" id="crosref0390">22</ce:cross-ref>]. It appears that there is significant potential for IS<ce:italic>26</ce:italic> to capture <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> from its current context and contribute to its wider dissemination. This appears to have occurred in the case of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">NDM-1</ce:inf> [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib23" id="crosref0395">23</ce:cross-ref>] which, like the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> genes, is thought to have originally been mobilized by an IS<ce:italic>CR</ce:italic> element [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib24" id="crosref0400">24</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0155" view="all">pNDTH10366, pNDTH9845 and pWTJH17 are all members of the pBT2101-like megaplasmid family [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib15" id="crosref0405">15</ce:cross-ref>]. Plasmids in this family can carry diverse antimicrobial resistance genes and are widely distributed, both geographically and among <ce:italic>Pseudomonas</ce:italic> hosts in which they are stable and do not impose a measurable fitness cost [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib15" id="crosref0410">15</ce:cross-ref>]. The association of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> genes with pBT2101-like plasmids in <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> may potentiate their rapid spread within the genus, which warrants continuous surveillance.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0160" view="all">To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an MBL-producing <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> co-harbouring five copies of the <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> gene. Co-expression of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">KPC-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> in NDTH10366 and NDTH9845 conferred high-level and broad-range resistance to all antipseudomonal β-lactams, including aztreonam/avibactam. This could be explained by two factors. First, high-level expression of KPC-2 could not be totally inhibited by avibactam because of its high copy number, as previously reported [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib25" id="crosref0415">25</ce:cross-ref>]. Second, mechanisms other than β-lactamases may be involved in aztreonam resistance, such as the up-regulation of efflux pump systems [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib26" id="crosref0420">26</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0165" view="all">From our evaluation, the ability of AFM to hydrolyse some antibiotics was slightly weaker than NDM-1. This could be explained by certain amino acid differences within AFM and NDM-1. For example, residues Leu65 and Met67 in NDM-1 are changed to Met and Val in AFM, respectively. Residues Leu65 and Met67 of NDM-1 have been reported to form strong hydrophobic interactions with the phenyl ring of penicillin or ampicillin [<ce:cross-ref refid="bib27" id="crosref0425">27</ce:cross-ref>]. These substitutions could lead to the alteration of loop conformation and hydrophobicity, thus indirectly influencing the enzymatic activities of AFM.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0170" view="all">In conclusion, we have described the genetic contexts of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> MBL genes and demonstrated that their products exhibit β-lactamase activity comparable to that of NDM-1. The association of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM</ce:inf> genes with translocatable genetic elements and plasmids highlights their potential for dissemination. That <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-2</ce:inf> and <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">AFM-3</ce:inf> have been found in organisms as clinically relevant as XDR-PA is of great concern. Genomic surveillance will be required to track the spread of AFM determinants in bacterial populations globally.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
