                  Evidence for and against the theory that cell calcium is causally involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis is presented and evaluated. In particular, it is argued that: (1) arterial calcium is increased in atherosclerosis; (2) this increase in tissue calcium content is largely intracellular; (3) this increased intracellular calcium content is caused by increased plasma membrane calcium permeability; (4) the increased calcium content is causally related to atherogenesis; (5) many of the cell physiological, cell biological, biochemical, and molecular biological processes, known to function abnormally in atherosclerosis, are also known to be calcium regulated; and (6) these processes are activated or inactivated in atherosclerosis in a manner consistent with increased cell calcium. It is concluded that the calcium-atherogenesis hypothesis has the potential to unify macroscopic clinical risk factors in terms of intracellular mechanisms that are controlled by cell calcium, and that this hypothesis deserves further experimental tests.

D. PHAIR  Departments of Biomedical Engineering and Physiology, The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, Maryland, USA  Abstract - Evidence for and against the theory that cell calcium is causally involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis is presented and evaluated. In particular, it is argued that: (1) arterial calcium is Increased in atherosclerosis; (2) this increase in tissue calcium content is largely intracellular; (3) this increased intracellular calcium content is caused by increased plasma membrane calcium permeability; (4) the Increased calcium content is causally related to atherogenesis; (5) many of the cell physiological, cell biological, biochemical, and molecular biological processes, known to function abnormally in atherosclerosis, are also known to be calcium regulated; and (6) these processes are activated or inactivated in atherosclerosis in a manner consistent with Increased cell calcium. It is concluded that the calcium-atherogenesis hypothesis has the potential to unify macroscopic clinical risk factors in terms of Intracellular mechanisms that are controlled by cell calcium, and that this hypothesis deserves further experimental tests.  The plan of this review is to present the accumulated evidence supporting the hypothesis that increased cell calcium is the pathological second messenger of atherogenesis, that calcium is a regulatory signal so universal that it is capable of accounting for the myriad of aberrant cellular processes that characterize the atherosclerotic artery. Beginning with the classical evidence that calcium content is increased in atherosclerotic arteries, the review progresses to examine recent evidence that this increased calcium is actually intracellular. Next, it considers the evidence suggesting that the increased intracellular calcium is secondary to an increase in plasma membrane calcium permeability. The penultimate section considers evidence supporting the hypothesis that these changes in cell calcium are actually causal, and not merely secondary to the central disease process. Finally, I  have constructed a list of cellular processes that are potentially altered in atherosclerotic arteries, and have presented some evidence for control of these processes by cell calcium.  It is no longer possible to provide an exhaustive bibliography for this subject in a brief review; new work on this area is appearing so rapidly that this review will soon be dated. Nevertheless, it should serve as an useful introduction for investigators approaching this problem from either calcium metabolism or lipid metabolism. This is important because these two groups of investigators have only recently begun to explore the areas in which their interests overlap.  A word about interesting work that has been excluded to maintain focus: this review is concerned with arterosclerosis and not other forms of arteriosclerosis. Moreover, the focus is on arterial  275  276  CELL CALCIUM  processes not hepatic or duodenal processes although these are central issues in plasma lipoprotein metabolism and may well be controlled by cell calcium. There is an extensive literature on calcium and the arterioscleroses recently reviewed by Fleckenstein [1], but detailed examination of the calcium dependence of plasma lipoprotein assembly, secretion, uptake and processing in liver and small intestine has barely begun.  Evidence that arterial calcium is increased in atherosclerosis  Arterial calcification has long been recognized as a hallmark of the atherosclerotic lesion second in prominence only to the accumulation of cholesteryl ester and the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells that constitutes the `fibrous cap' of the lesion. Not only is calcification a universal feature of the plaque, but medial calcification of the artery is known to precede intimal plaque formation [2], consistent with its possible causal role.  Many reports have documented an increased calcium content of human atherosclerotic arteries [2j and arteries from experimental animals fed atherogenic diets [3-5]. These reports generally depend on the application of atomic absorption spectrophotometry or on low-resolution histochemical measurements. Consequently, they do not discriminate between infra- and extra-cellular calcium. This distinction had to await new methodological developments.  We recently developed a method based on kinetic analysis of 45 Ca efflux curves that allows measurement of intracellular calcium contents and transorganellar calcium fluxes in intact arterial segments [6, 7]. For the study of atherosclerotic vessels, this method is advantageous because it provides a tissue average of changes in intracellular calcium, an essential feature of any method that aims to quantify intracellular changes in a heterogeneous disease such as atherosclerosis. We applied this method to aortas from cholesterol-fed rabbits and found a 4.$-fold increase in intracellular calcium when compared to aortas from age-matched control rabbits [8]. An important inference from our result is that cytosolic free calcium is, on average, increased in cells from atherosclerotic aortas. This prediction should be tested using a calcium indicator and a population of cells isolated from these arteries.  The observed increase in intracellular calcium supports the hypothesis, advanced by several groups of investigators [3-5, 9-12] that atherogenesis is dependent upon increased calcium. In effect, calcium may act as a pathological second messenger, an idea that is also consistent with the proposal that cell calcium mediates the final common pathway of cell death [13]. Clearly these hypotheses are appropriate in a volume devoted to the `pathology of cell calcium.'  Evidence that increased cell calcium in atherosclerosis is caused by increased plasma membrane calcium permeability  Evidence that the atherosclerotic increase in arterial calcium is intracellular  It is widely believed that although this calcium accumulation occurs, it cannot be involved in intracellular regulation because it accumulates in the interstitial spaces of the artery. This belief is probably founded on observations that frank calcification of advanced atherosclerotic lesions appears to be confined to the acellular necrotic core of the lesion. However, the review cited above [1] correctly asserts that it is as yet unknown whether all this excess calcium is extracellular.  A further advantage of the tracer kinetic method is that it allowed us to test several hypotheses advanced to explain the observed increase in intracellular calcium. Interestingly, the 45 Ca efflux data were quantitatively consistent with the hypothesis that plasma membrane calcium permeability is increased in arteries from cholesterol-fed rabbits [8]. Moreover, the data were inconsistent with several alternative hypotheses such as general activation of organellar calcium uptake or inhibition of the plasma membrane calcium ATPase.  These results warrant further testing. Patch-clamp analysis of voltage-sensitive calcium  CELLULAR CALCIUM & ATHEROSCLEROSIS: A BRIEF REVIEW  277  channels in smooth muscle cells of diseased arteries would be highly desirable. Lacking this information should, however, not inhibit speculation as to mechanism. We and others have suggested that the increased permeability might be caused by a change in the cholesterol content of smooth muscle cell membranes. There is considerable evidence that the function of integral membrane proteins can be regulated by their lipid environment. The reported ability of alterations in erythrocyte membrane cholesterol to control the entry of extracellular calcium [14] provides experimental support for this proposal. It might be argued that inhibition by cholesterol of diphosphatidate-mediated transmembrane calcium movements [15] is contrary to our tracer result. However, it is likely that the mechanism involves slow calcium channels rather than phospholipids acting as calcium ionophores (vide infra).  Evidence that arterial cell calcium plays a causal role in atherogenesis  If intracellular calcium is a link in the causal chain of atherogenic events then perturbations of cellular calcium metabolism should result in corresponding perturbations of arterial lipid metabolism. Two lines of evidence suggest that this is the case, but other reports militate against this position.  Evidence supporting a causal role for calcium  If increased cellular calcium is atherogenic, it should be possible to ameliorate or prevent atherosclerosis using compounds that prevent calcium transport from the extracellular to the intracellular environment. There are now innumerable reports in the world's literature demonstrating that this is the case. Both inorganic and organic calcium antagonists have been used and an excellent review of this literature is available [16]. As spec examples, the pioneering work of Krarnsch and his colleagues [17, 18] deserves specific mention. With the advent of the dihydropyridines and other organic calcium antagonists, highly specific tests of the calcium-atherogenesis theory became possible. Representative compounds from each of the three  major classes of organic calcium channel blockers have been reported to block atherogenesis. For example, nifedipine [19], diltiazem [20), and verapamil [21] have been reported to reduce significantly arterial cholesteryl ester deposition in cholesterol-fed animals. These agents have been effective without reducing arterial pressure or blood lipids, and since each of the three major classes has been shown effective other side effects of the drugs are probably not responsible for their antiatherogenic actions. While there are negative reports (vide infra), the antiatherogenic properties of these compounds are cited as an additional reason for choosing them to treat hypertension [22].  If increased cell calcium causes the sequelae associated with atherogenesis, then it should be possible to induce these characteristic features of the disease by artificially increasing cell calcium. We are unaware of such experimental tests in the literature, but we have a preliminary result that appears to confirm this prediction. Segments of rabbit thoracic aorta, 2 cm long, were divided in half longitudinally. One half was incubated for 18 hr at 37 in a gassed, bicarbonate-buffered, calcium containing physiological salt solution containing 1 pM calcium-magnesium ionophore, A23187. The other half served as a paired control and was incubated in the same manner, but without the ionophore. Arteries from five rabbits were treated in this way and then analyzed for cholesterol and cholesteryl ester content. While free cholesterol content was not significantly increased, cholesteryl ester content was increased 2.1-fold. (P <0.005, paired West, n = 5) [23]. Since accumulation of cholesteryl ester is the hallmark of atherosclerosis, at least one of the many cellular events associated with atherogenesis can be initiated by artificially increasing cellular calcium. This result, though preliminary, is as predicted by the calciumatherosclerosis hypothesis.  Evidence against a causal role for calcium  Majority opinion is occasionally an inadequate guide to scientific truth. Consequently, it should be emphasized that while most attempts to prevent experimental athexosclerosis using organic calcium antagonists have been successful, several negative  278  CELL CALCIUM  reports have been published as well. For example, both nicardipine and diltiazem were found to be ineffective [24] in a rabbit model of atherosclerosis similar to the ones described above. Even nifedipine has been reported to fail [25].  Another fording that cannot be reconciled with a causal role for calcium is the apparent inability of nifedipine or verapamil to prevent arterial cholesterol deposition in a genetic model of atherosclerosis, the Watanabe heritable hyperlipidemic (WHHL) rabbit [26, 27]. The objections raised by these reports are straight forward; they simply do not confirm previous findings. But another class of objections has also been raised.  This second group of reports agrees that calcium antagonists do prevent cholesterol deposition, but argues that this has nothing to do with blockage of calcium entry. For example, isradipine has been shown to block secretion of extracellular matrix proteins (another characteristic feature of atherosclerotic lesions, assessed as acid precipitable I4C-proline secretion) from cultured smooth muscle cells, but this effect is also observed for fibroblasts and cells of macrophage and hepatic origin. The authors assert that these alternate cell types do not have voltage-sensitive calcium channels, and infer that isradipine must inhibit matrix secretion by some mechanism other than blocking calcium entry [28]. This is an important result that presents a serious challenge to advocates of a causal role for calcium in atherogenesis, but it is significant that at least one fibroblast-like cell line has recently been shown to express voltage-sensitive calcium channels [29]. If this channel is more ubiquitous than previously supposed, then the objection raised by [28] could be overruled.  Another report that attributes the antiatherogenic activity of dihydropyridines to side-effects of the drugs is [30]. These investigators demonstrated that both nifedipine and the calcium channel agonist, BAY K8644, blocked cholesteryl ester accumulation (as measured by 3H-oleate incorporation) in alveolar macrophages incubated with 0-very low density lipoprotein (O-VLDL). Since the same effect was observed whether calcium channels were blocked or held open, the conclusion was unavoidable; the ability of calcium entry blockers to prevent  cholesteryl ester deposition was 'dissociable from calcium entry blockade.' This result is obviously inconsistent with a causal role for calcium in atherogenesis. Although BAY K8644 is a racemic mixture of antagonist and agonist stereoisomers, this provides little help. The smallest concentration used in these experiments was 100 nM, and although pure agonist activity would probably best be observed at 1 nM or 10 nM, this reviewer is unaware of reports citing antagonist activity at 100 nM BAY K8644.  Finally, it may be argued that calcium is too ubiquitous a second messenger to be the mediator of a specific disease. This is difficult to rebut, but it may be significant that so many cellular processes are deranged in an atheroma and that so many of them can be shown to depend on cell calcium. Compilation of some of these processes is the subject of the next section.  Arterial cell processes involved in atherogenesis and evidence for their regulation by cell calcium  Speclftc cellular processes in the arterlal wall  Plasma lipoprotein processing by smooth muscle cells and macrophages  Function of the low density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor is known to be calcium dependent, but this reviewer is unaware of reported experiments that would clearly distinguish between dependence on extracellular versus intracellular calcium. This distinction is potentially important because if increased cytosolic calcium accelerates receptor-mediated endocytosis of LDL then the well-documented down-regulation of the LDL receptor could be countered by calcium activation of the remaining receptors. Against this notion is the report that changes in intracellular calcium cause, `...little or no change in endocytosis...' of transferrin and insulin in a human cell line [31].  One report [32] that tends to support dependence on extracellular calcium found that verapamil enhances LDL uptake by aortic cells in culture. This was seen as consistent with reported antiatherogenic actions of Ca antagonists '...by promoting transfer of LDL cholesteryl ester from  CELLULAR CALCIUM Bt ATHEROSCLEROSIS: A BRIEF REVIEW  279  the aortic interstitium to a catabolic compartment.' Other investigators have reached similar conclusions using representative compounds from several classes of organic calcium antagonists, and have also used A23187 as an antagonist of LDL receptor activity [331. It is apparent from these reports that lysosomal degradation is enhanced by calcium antagonists, but it appears to this reviewer that these actions would enhance rather than retard cholesteryl ester deposition in the arterial wall. Perhaps, as suggested in [33), the stimulation of receptor activity is unrelated to the blockage of calcium entry. In support of this, the authors note that relatively large doses of verapamil are required, and that dihydropyridines and flunarizine are ineffective. In contrast, another group has reported that calcium antagonists inhibited degradation of internalized LDL [341 and thus blocked the delivery of free cholesterol to the feedback regulatory mechanisms for cholesterol synthesis and LDL receptor synthesis. Rather than being antiatherogenic, these effects enhance the accumulation of LDL in cells. Again, however, verapamil and diltiazern were used at relatively high concentrations (5Q-100 pM) and nifedipine had no effect.  Cholesterol esterification  Accumulation of cholesteryl esters (principally cholesteryl oleate) is a major feature of the atherosclerotic plaque. The reaction that produces these esters (from free cholesterol and activated fatty acids, fatty acyl CoA) is catalyzed by acylcoenzymeA : cholesterol O-acyl transferase (ACAT) (EC 2.3.1.26). An outstanding review of the role of this enzyme in cholesterol metabolism is available [351. These authors saw the central question as:  '...whether the increased ACAT activity (in atherosclerotic lesions) is a cause of the atherosclerotic process or is simply the result of an increase in the availability of the substrate, cholesterol.'  Their conclusion was that:  'The available evidence suggests that the higher ACAT activity in atherosclerosis is a secondary event and merely reflects an increase in the availability of cholesterol (and acyl CoA) in the arterial wall.'  The ACAT reaction takes place on microsomal  membranes, and the distribution of the enzyme is reported to be similar to that of RNA [361 implying that ACAT is localized in rough endoplasmic reticulum The cholesterol ester product separates from membrane and accumulates in cytosol as lipid droplets.  While ACAT activity is controlled in many tissues by free cholesterol and acylCoA availability, it has recently been reported that verapamil decreases ACAT-mediated cholesterol esterification in intact cells but not in cell homogenates [371 suggesting control by calcium influx and emphasizing the need for intact cells when examining calcium regulated systems. Combining this with the conclusion that ACAT is regulated by mass action (that is, availability of its substrates) suggests that we look for calcium control of substrate availability.  Acyl CoA synthetase (EC 6.2.1.3) is the enzyme catalyzing activation of free fatty acids and making them available for esterifrcation to cholesterol. Since the rate limiting step in cholesteryl ester accumulation may be acylCoA formation rather than ACAT, this enzyme is a candidate for control by calcium that might explain the verapamil result [371 cited above. This reviewer knows of no data that would rule out this possibility, but since acylCoA synthetase activity in atherosclerotic aorta is not different from activity in normal aorta, this is probably not a fruitful line of investigation for tests of the calcium-atherosclerosis hypothesis.  On the other hand, acylCoA hydrolase (EC 3.1.2.2), the enzyme catalyzing the reverse reaction, is (in human platelets) known to be inhibited by calcium. If this calcium control mechanism is also present in aorta, it could control the concentration of acylCoA available for further metabolism while at the same time accounting for the observed [3514096 decrease in acylCoA hydrolase activity in atherosclerotic aorta. Against this idea is that the substrate preference of the hydrolase is pahriitoyl > stearoyl > myristoyl > oleoyl, suggesting that buildup of oleoylCoA is not a major function of this enzyme.  Cholesterol ester hydrolase (CEH) (EC 3.1.1.13) has been reported to catalyze cholesterol ester synthesis in some tissues, but the prevailing view is that, in vivo, this enzyme catalyzes cholesterol ester  280  CELL CALCIUM  hydrolysis. There is a report that this process is accelerated in lipid-loaded rabbit arterial smooth muscle cells treated with nifedipine, but the predominant action was on lysosomal acid CEH rather than on cytosolic or neutral CEH [38]. Nevertheless, the small but statistically significant effect on the cytosolic enzyme is consistent with the antiatherogenic activity of nifedipine. Of course, some mechanism of reverse cholesterol transport will still have to be activated to result in a net reduction of cell cholesterol.  Growth factor responses and cell proliferation These are prominent features of the atherosclerotic lesion [39], and it is widely believed that increased cytosolic free calcium is the mediator of growth factor responses. Our own work [40] suggests that intracellular calcium release (from endoplasmic reticulum or `calciosome' [41]) may contribute to the induction of both competence and progression events by some, but not all, polypeptide growth factors. Consequently, the calcium-atherogenesis hypothesis becomes indistinguishable from growth factor based theories when examined at the intracellular level. Both can account for the increased DNA synthesis and possibly for the clonal character of the atheroma.  Recruitment of monocytes  The debate as to whether macrophages or smooth muscle cells are the progenitors of atherosclerotic foam cells has swept through many oscillations in its long history. Today the predominant view is that monocyte-macrophages account for most foam cells. If this is true, atherogenesis requires a mechanism by which monocytes are recruited to the developing lesion. An intriguing hypothesis purporting to explain this phenomenon is that lyso-phosphatidyl choline serves as a chemotactic factor for monocytes [42]. This compound is thought to be formed upon oxidation of LDL by a phospholipase A2 activity [43]. While it is not clear whether this activity is intracellular or extracellular, it is perhaps significant that this enzyme is widely believed to be controlled by calcium. Indeed, this is the mechanism by which prostaglandin production is thought to be stimulated by increased cytosolic calcium in a variety of physiological circumstances. If lyso-phosphatidyl  choline is produced by calcium replete arterial cells and released into the extracellular space, it is likely that the recruitment of monocytes could be reduced, and atherogenesis slowed, by calcium antagonists. This possibility warrants an experimental test.  Endothelial permeation (transcytosis)  Cholesterol delivered to an atheroma is carried on plasma LDL, a molecule too large to pass through capillary pores or between vascular endothelial cells. Consequently, it is thought that LDL gains access to the arterial interstitium by endothelial endocytosis and subsequent exocytosis on the abluminal endothelial cell surface. Endothelial permeation by peroxidase (MW 40 kD) has been shown to be inhibited by flunarizine [44], but the authors concluded that the drug blocks intercellular movement. Whether it also blocks transcellular movement could not be determined from their data  De novo cholesterol synthesis  It is generally agreed that the cholesterol in atheromatous lesions is supplied from plasma, but it is also known that atherosclerotic blood vessels can synthesize cholesterol from acetyl (not acyl) CoA. This pathway is widely referred to as de novo cholesterol synthesis, and its rate-limiting step is thought to be hydroxymethylglutaryl CoA reductase (HMGCoA reductase) (EC 1.1.1.34 and 1.1.1.88). Tremendous research and clinical interest has recently developed in an inhibitor of this enzyme (meviuoln) that is seen as a potentially effective therapeutic compound for atherosclerosis. From this reviewer's point of view, however, it is potentially just as significant that this compound has been reported to block (slow) calcium channels in cultured cardiac cells with great potency [45]. If this action can be demonstrated in arterial cells as well, mevinolin may be found to be an ideal combination of cholesterol synthesis blocker and calcium antagonist. This, however, is a highly complex and interactive portion of the metabolic map and quantitative models will undoubtedly be necessary to sort out the interlocking control systems. Our own preliminary kinetic analysis of amino acid pulse-chase experiments in hepatocytes suggests that transport through the Golgi complex is slow enough that significant protein accumulation can occur there. Thus, acceleration of inter-Golgi transport could be responsible for additional protein delivery to secretory vesicles, especially if translation in the RER is accelerated as well.  Transcription and translation  Calcium depletion has recently been shown to impair protein synthesis, but transcription (the synthesis of mRNA) was ruled out as the regulatory site and initiation of translation rather than elongation was identified as the site of calcium action [46]. These investigators also conclude that ER calcium rather than cytosolic calcium is what regulates initiation. While this result suggests yet another site where calcium could regulate cell function, it requires (surprisingly) that initiation takes place after the ribosome is attached to the RER, and it implies that synthesis of proteins on free ribosomms is not regulatable by calcium. Consequently, only secretory and integral membrane proteins would be affected. Experimental tests of these predictions are clearly warranted.  Exocytosis of secretory proteins  Cell calcium has long been associated with the processes of apposition, fusion, and discharge that together constitute exocytosis [49]. Moreover, calmodulin binding compounds such as phenothiazines block secretion in several cell types. There are, however, more recent reports of Ca-independent secretion [50]. Differences between the control of secretory granule release and secretory vesicle (the vehicle carrying secretory proteins from the trans Golgi to the plasma membrane) release may well exist. But it would not be surprising to find that secretory vesicles turn over faster when cytosolic calcium is increased. If this were the case, a partial explanation of increased matrix protein secretion could be constructed based on the calcium-atherogenesis hypothesis.  Intracellular vesicular trafficking  All secretory and integral membrane proteins must pass through the secretory pathway of arterial cells in order to reach their destinations in the membrane or the extracellular space. These proteins, synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, must be processed by the Golgi complex - a series of functionally distinct organelles - before being packaged in secretory vesicles for transport to the cell surface. It has been demonstrated [47, 48] that calcium is essential for protein transit through the Golgi cisternae of myeloma cells and fibroblasts. If this is true in arterial cells, increased cell calcium might increase the fluxes of secretory and membrane proteins through the secretory pathway and provide a partial explanation for the excessive collagen and elastin secretion that is characteristic of atherosclerosis. This has long been true of arterial calcium metabolism and atherosclerosis. Frequently, the barricades between such fields are dismantled by a single compelling result. The possible relationship between vascular calcium metabolism and atherosclerosis was adumbrated by the reported ability of inorganic blockers of cellular Ca uptake to reduce atherogenesis. These early results were dramatically supported by reports of the ability of three different classes of organic Ca channel blockers to prevent development of atherosclerosis in cholesterol fed rabbits.  282  CELL CALCIUM  These findings, combined with others cited in the body of this paper, suggest a working hypothesis for cell Ca as the final common pathway of atherogenesis. A reasonable circumstantial case can be presented for each of the major clinical risk factors. Hypercholesterolemia, for example, may increase cellular calcium as a consequence of altered plasma membrane calcium channel function. There is evidence that channel function is sensitive to the cholesterol composition of the channel's microenvironment [14], and there is a precedent for sensitivity of other calcium transport systems (Na-Ca exchange) to other lipids in cardiac sarcolemma [51]. The mechanisms by which these sensitivities are established have been recently reviewed [52].  Hypertension, another major risk factor for atherosclerosis, is characterized by and (many investigators believe) is caused by abnormal arteriolar smooth muscle function and increased cytosolic calcium concentration causing an increased total peripheral resistance. Recently, it has been reported that the cytosolic calcium concentration of blood platelets correlates with blood pressure [53] and that a serum factor from hypertensioe patients causes increased cytosolic calcium in platelets [54]. If extrapolation to vascular smooth muscle is appropriate, one might speculate, based on the calcium-atherosclerosis hypothesis, that hypertension may be a risk factor for atherosclerosis precisely because this change in membrane function occurs in large vessels as well as small ones.  Diabetes has long been recognized as an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis. Recent work [55] demonstrates, using standard patch-clamp techniques, that insulin is a potent calcium antagonist in rat skeletal muscle myoballs. This suggests that hypoinsulinemia or insulin 'resistance' could result in excessive calcium uptake by at least some cell types. If this were true of aortic cells, mechanistic unification of this risk factor with the others would become possible.  Experimental evidence supporting these ideas is far from complete, but the possibility is so compelling that reports such as [56] suggesting (based on clinical, angiographic, and electrocardiographic data in 212 consecutive  patients with ischemic heart disease) that a single disease process leads fast to vasospasm and later to atherosclerotic plaque development are no longer surprising.  Once cytosolic and/or organellar calcium is increased, the myriad of calcium-regulated cellular processes is activated in concert. As indicated in the body of this review, a strong circumstantial case can be made for calcium regulation of many intracellular processes, and the direction of change caused by increased calcium is very often consistent with what is observed in atherosclerosis. The calcium-atherosclerosis hypothesis thus has the potential to unify macroscopic risk factors in terms of molecular biological and cell biological processes that are controlled by cell calcium.  As one of several contributions to a volume on the pathology of cell calcium, this brief review testes to the close interaction between arterial calcium metabolism and arterial lipid metabolism especially at the level of intracellular processes. Burgeoning activity in this research area suggests that integrative biology -the assembly of molecular biological and cell biological results into an experimentally testable, mechanistic hypothesis that explains large scale phenomena - is in its ascendancy.  The working hypothesis assembled in this review is at once simple and dramatic. It asserts that cell calcium is the pathological second messenger of atherogenesis, and that diverse risk factors for the development of atherosclerosis have one thing in common - they all promote intracellular calcium accumulation. Of course, a simple hypothesis is rarely correct in all its predictions, and there are, already in the literature, serious objections to its validity. But the potential for unification of many lines of investigation warrants the further investment that will be required to completely test this intriguing hypothesis. Ongoing clinical trials [57] and mechanistic investigations at the level of basic science will surely make rapid progress in this surprisingly active area of scientific inquiry. In particular, it is argued that: (1) arterial calcium is increased in atherosclerosis; (2) this increase in tissue calcium content is largely intracellular; (3) this increased intracellular calcium content is caused by increased plasma membrane calcium permeability; (4) the increased calcium content is causally related to atherogenesis; (5) many of the cell physiological, cell biological, biochemical, and molecular biological processes, known to function abnormally in atherosclerosis, are also known to be calcium regulated; and (6) these processes are activated or inactivated in atherosclerosis in a manner consistent with increased cell calcium. It is concluded that the calcium-atherogenesis hypothesis has the potential to unify macroscopic clinical risk factors in terms of intracellular mechanisms that are controlled by cell calcium, and that this hypothesis deserves further experimental tests.
