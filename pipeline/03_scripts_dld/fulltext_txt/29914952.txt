Colistin is a last-line drug for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. We previously reported four plasmid-mediated colistin resistance ( mcr ) gene-negative colistin-resistant Escherichia coli clinical isolates, including the major pathogenic and fluoroquinolone-resistant strains O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx (isolates SRE34 and SRE44; MIC for colistin = 16 mg/liter), non-x (SME296; MIC = 8 mg/liter), and O18-ST416 (SME222; MIC = 4 mg/liter). 

KEYWORDS: antimicrobial resistance, bacterial infection, PmrAB, lipid A, Escherichia coli 

ABSTRACT 

Colistin is a last-line drug for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. We previously reported four plasmid-mediated colistin resistance ( mcr ) gene-negative colistin-resistant Escherichia coli clinical isolates, including the major pathogenic and fluoroquinolone-resistant strains O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx (isolates SRE34 and SRE44; MIC for colistin = 16 mg/liter), non-x (SME296; MIC = 8 mg/liter), and O18-ST416 (SME222; MIC = 4 mg/liter). In this study, we investigated the colistin resistance mechanism and identified novel amino acid substitutions or deletions in the PmrAB two-component system that activates eptA (encoding a phosphoethanolamine transferase) and arnT (encoding an undecaprenyl phosphate-alpha-4-amino-4-deoxy- l -arabinose arabinosyl transferase) in all colistin-resistant isolates. SRE34 possessed deletion Δ27–45 (LISVFWLWHESTEQIQLFE) in PmrB, SRE44 possessed substitution L105P in PmrA, and both SME222 and SME296 included substitution G206D in PmrB. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry revealed that lipid A is modified with phosphoethanolamine in all four isolates. Deletion of pmrAB decreased colistin MICs to 0.5 mg/liter and lowered eptA and arnT expression. Chromosomal replacement of mutated pmrA or pmrB in colistin-susceptible O25b:H4-ST131 strain SME98 (colistin MIC = 0.5 mg/liter) increased the colistin MIC to that of the respective parent colistin-resistant isolate. In addition, SME98 mutants in which pmrAB was replaced with mutated pmrAB showed no significant differences in bacterial growth and competition culture from the parent strain, except for the mutant with L105P in PmrA, whose growth was significantly suppressed in the presence of the parent strain. In conclusion, some O25b:H4-ST131 strains appear to acquire colistin resistance via phosphoethanolamine modification of lipid A through amino acid changes in PmrAB, and the amino acid changes in PmrB do not influence bacterial growth. 

INTRODUCTION 

In extraintestinal infections caused by Escherichia coli , such as urinary tract infections and septicemia, a high prevalence of fluoroquinolone resistance and extended-spectrum β-lactamase production has been observed worldwide, including in Japan ( 1 , 2 ). This has been mainly attributed to the spread of specific international high-risk clones O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx and non-x ( 2 , 3 ). Some multidrug-resistant strains have acquired resistance to carbapenem and/or aminoglycoside drugs, including amikacin ( 4 – 6 ), emphasizing the difficulties faced by clinicians when choosing an appropriate antimicrobial therapy. 

Colistin (polymyxin E) is a polycationic peptide antimicrobial agent that is an important last-line drug for a variety of Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli ( 7 ). However, recent studies report the worldwide spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance ( mcr ) genes ( mcr-1 , - 2 , - 3 , - 4 , and - 5 ), which encode phosphoethanolamine (PEtN) transferases, especially in livestock-derived Enterobacteriaceae , due to the use of colistin as a growth promoter and anti-infective drug ( 8 – 12 ). In some countries, Enterobacteriaceae possessing mcr-1 have been isolated from human clinical samples, although the prevalence in humans is still lower than in livestock ( 8 , 13 ). Additionally, chromosomal colistin and polymyxin B resistance mechanisms have been reported for Enterobacteriaceae ( 14 ). In E. coli , this mechanism is mediated by activation of genes encoding two-component systems, phoPQ and pmrAB ( 14 , 15 ). Mutations in pmrAB activate genes, such as eptA (a synonym of pmrC ) and arnT , that are involved in modifying lipid A with PEtN and 4-amino-4-deoxy- l -arabinose ( l -Ara4N), respectively. 

In our previous study, we reported the isolation from clinical specimens of colistin-resistant E. coli O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx and non-x isolates lacking the mcr-1 and mcr-2 genes (2.2% of all ST131 isolates) ( 16 ). This indicated the presence of a colistin-resistant population in the ST131 lineage. Expansion and colonization of ST131 have been observed in both community and clinical settings ( 2 , 3 ), and the rapid spread of colistin-resistant ST131 is a major concern for clinicians faced with treating the extensively multidrug-resistant strain. Therefore, a better understanding of colistin resistance in ST131 could prove valuable for treatment and prevention. Interestingly, the patients from whom colistin-resistant E. coli strains were isolated in our previous study ( 16 ) had no history of colistin or polymyxin B therapy. This indicates the possibility that these E. coli isolates can colonize in patients in the absence of colistin selection pressure and without loss of bacterial fitness due to the acquisition of colistin resistance. However, detailed investigations have not yet been performed. 

In this study, we investigated the colistin resistance mechanism in E. coli isolates by determining the molecular structure of the lipid A region of lipopolysaccharides (LPSs), investigating eptA and arnT gene expression, and probing the role of novel mutations in pmrAB to elucidate the colistin resistance mechanism(s) independent of mcr genes in E. coli , including an international high-risk clone, ST131. In addition, we evaluated the influence of the pmrAB mutations on bacterial growth to determine the reason for isolation of the colistin-resistant E. coli in the absence of colistin selection pressure in clinical setting. 

RESULTS 

Detection of mcr genes and mutations in phoPQ and pmrAB in colistin-resistant E. coli isolates. 

In our previous study, we showed that the four colistin-resistant isolates do not possess mcr-1 and - 2 ( 16 ). In the present study, we further examined the newly discovered mcr genes and found that mcr-3 , - 4 , and - 5 are also not harbored among the isolates. 

We determined the full nucleotide sequence of phoP , phoQ , pmrA , and pmrB from the four colistin-resistant E. coli isolates and a colistin-susceptible ST131 isolate (SME98). The sequences were compared with those of E. coli strain K-12 substrain MG1655. We identified several nucleotide mutations in these genes that confer 13 amino acid alterations in both colistin-resistant and colistin-susceptible isolates ( Table 1 ). Furthermore, comparison with the colistin-susceptible SME98 revealed unique amino acid substitutions and deletions in PhoQ, PmrA, and PmrB in the four colistin-resistant isolates (shown in bold in Table 1 ). The colistin-resistant O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx (isolate SRE34), which has the highest reported colistin MIC (16 mg/liter), includes a deletion of 19 amino acid residues between positions 27 and 45 in PmrB, caused by a 57-bp deletion (between positions 79 and 135) in the pmrB gene. SRE44, another isolate of colistin-resistant O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx that also has a colistin MIC of 16 mg/liter, also includes a unique L105P substitution in PmrA. Meanwhile, the O25b:H4-ST131- H 30R non-x isolate SME296, which has a colistin MIC of 8 mg/liter, includes a unique G206D substitution in PmrB. The colistin-resistant isolate SME222 (O18-ST416), which has a colistin MIC of 4 mg/liter, includes four amino acid substitutions in PhoQ, PmrA, and PmrB, compared with SME98. Among these, G206D in PmrB was also observed in SME296.

mRNA expression levels of eptA and arnT in colistin-resistant E. coli isolates. 

Expression of eptA and arnT genes in colistin-resistant E. coli isolates was compared with expression in colistin-susceptible ST131 isolates, including SME98 ( Fig. 1 ; see also Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). All colistin-resistant isolates displayed significantly increased mRNA levels for both eptA and arnT , except for no significant difference in eptA expression in SME296. Expression levels of eptA were higher in SRE34 and SRE44 (MIC = 16 mg/liter) than in SME222 and SME296 (MIC = 4 and 8 mg/liter, respectively), and there was no significant difference in arnT expression among colistin-resistant isolates.

Structural analysis of lipid A. 

We determined the structure of lipid A in colistin-resistant E. coli isolates and SME98 using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Lipid A from SME98 yielded major molecular ions at m/z 1,716 and 1,796 (attributed to mono- and diphosphoryl hexaacyl lipid A [unmodified lipid A], respectively) ( Fig. 2 ). Lipid A from SRE34 yielded a major ion at m/z 1,919 (attributed to PEtN-modified diphosphoryl hexaacyl lipid A) in addition to another major molecular ion at m/z 1,796, followed by a subdominant molecular ion at m/z 1,839 (attributed to PEtN-modified monophosphoryl hexaacyl lipid A). Lipid A from SRE44 yielded a major molecular ion at m/z 1,839, followed by two subdominant molecular ions at m/z 1,919 and m/z 1,796. Lipid A from SME222 yielded a major molecular ion at m/z 1,796, followed by m/z 1,919 and m/z 1,839. Lipid A from SME296 yielded three major molecular ions at m/z 1,796, m/z 1,839, and m/z 1,919. These results indicate that the colistin-resistant isolates contained PEtN-modified lipid A, in addition to unmodified lipid A.

Colistin susceptibility and eptA and arnT expression in pmrAB deletion mutants. 

We generated pmrAB deletion mutants by homologous recombination with pmrAB and a kanamycin resistance cassette. All pmrAB deletion mutants derived from the four colistin-resistant E. coli isolates exhibited decreased colistin MIC values (0.5 mg/liter), comparable with the MIC of the colistin-susceptible isolate SME98 (Fig. S2). In contrast, the MIC of the pmrAB deletion mutant derived from SME98 (0.5 mg/liter) was not altered. Expression of eptA and arnT in all pmrAB deletion mutants was decreased compared with that in the parent isolates (less than 0.01-fold) (Fig. S2). These results indicate that pmrAB in colistin-resistant isolates contributed to decreasing colistin susceptibility by increasing eptA and arnT expression. 

pmrAB competition assays of colistin-resistant E. coli isolates. 

Transformation of an expression vector harboring pmrAB and the putative native promoter region of SME98 (pHSGpmrAB) into SME98 did not alter the colistin MIC (Fig. S3). Transformation of pHSGpmrAB into the four colistin-resistant isolates decreased the colistin MIC to 0.5 mg/liter, whereas transformation of the empty vector (pHSG576) into the colistin-resistant isolates and SME98 did not alter the colistin MIC relative to those of the respective parent isolates. Expressions of both eptA and arnT were significantly decreased in most of the pHSGpmrAB transformants relative to the pHSG576 transformants (0.09- to 0.51-fold differences from the pHSG576 transformants [Fig. S3]). These results indicate that native pmrAB derived from colistin-susceptible isolate SME98 competed functionally with mutated pmrAB in colistin-resistant isolates. 

Colistin susceptibility, eptA and arnT expression, and lipid A structure in pmrAB replacement mutants. 

We generated three SME98 mutants in which pmrAB was replaced with mutated pmrAB ( Table 2 ). The SME98 mutants, named SME98PORTpmrB34 and SME98PORTpmrA105, include the same deletion in pmrB as found in SRE34 (Δ27–45) and the same mutation in pmrA as found in SRE44 (L105P), respectively. Another SME98 mutant, named SME98PORTpmrB206, includes the mutation in pmrB that confers substitution G206D of SME222 and SME296 ( Table 1 ). All three mutants displayed increased colistin and polymyxin B MICs compared with those of SME98 ( Table 2 ). Furthermore, eptA and arnT expressions in SME98PORTpmrB34, SME98PORTpmrA105, and SME98PORTpmrB105 were significantly increased compared with those in SME98 ( Fig. 3 ). MALDI-TOF MS analysis detected different molecular ions in the three pmrAB replacement mutants compared with SME98 (Fig. S4), with a molecular ion at m/z 1,839 that was attributed to PEtN-modified hexaacyl lipid A.

Growth curves were determined for the SME98 mutants in which pmrAB was replaced with mutated pmrAB generated using pORTMAGE and SME98. Bacterial growth of pmrAB replacement SME98 mutants was identical to that of SME98 ( Fig. 4A ).

DISCUSSION 

Recent studies report the worldwide spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes mcr-1 to mcr-5 in Enterobacteriaceae , especially in E. coli ( 8 – 12 ). In contrast, we found that colistin-resistant E. coli isolates derived from human clinical specimens, three O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx/non-x isolates (SRE34, SRE44, and SME296) and one O18-ST416 isolate (SME222), did not possess any mcr genes. 

The four mcr -negative colistin-resistant isolates in this study displayed higher eptA and/or arnT mRNA expression levels than colistin-susceptible ST131 isolates and exhibited PEtN modification of lipid A, whereas l -Ara4N-modified lipid A was not detected ( Fig. 1 and 2 ). Previous studies on lipid A structure in colistin-resistant E. coli analyzed only laboratory strains, and both l -Ara4N and PEtN modifications were observed in some cases ( 15 , 17 , 18 ). The apparent discrepancy with our current results could be because mutants generated by genetic engineering of E. coli laboratory strains and/or the lipid A modifications may depend on the culture conditions ( 15 , 17 , 18 ). To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to reveal the lipid A structure of mcr -negative colistin-resistant ST131 E. coli clinical isolates. This result suggests that the PEtN modification pathway mediated by the enzyme encoded by eptA affects colistin resistance in E. coli clinical isolates, although the mRNA expression levels were significantly lower than those of arnT ( Fig. 1 ). 

Alteration of pmrAB and competitive expression of native pmrAB in colistin-resistant isolates indicated that mutating pmrAB contributed to colistin resistance (Fig. S1 and S2). We found a deletion of amino acid residues in PmrB (47 to 45) or unique amino acid substitutions in PmrA (L105P) or PmrB (G206D) in colistin-resistant ST131. Another colistin-resistant isolate, SME222 (O18-ST416), includes four amino acid substitutions in PhoQ, PmrA, and PmrB compared with SME98. However, the amino acid substitutions (R6H in PhoQ, and I128N and G144S in PmrA) found in SME222 were reported to not contribute to colistin resistance ( 19 ), unlike the G206D substitution in PmrB. 

To examine the contribution of these amino acid alterations in PmrAB to colistin resistance, we performed gene replacement of pmrAB . Recently, a precise and portable genome engineering method was reported using a dominant negative mutant protein of the methyl-directed mismatch repair system-driven vector pORTMAGE ( 20 ). This method allows efficient modification of chromosomal DNA without any off-target mutations ( 20 ). We deduced that this powerful tool could prove effective for assessing the direct contribution of mutated pmrAB to colistin resistance. We generated three pmrAB replacement SME98 mutants using pORTMAGE and designed 90-bp oligonucleotides containing mutated regions of pmrA and pmrB . The resulting mutants displayed drastically increased colistin MICs that were similar to those of the respective colistin-resistant isolates ( Table 2 ). Furthermore, they exhibited increased eptA and arnT mRNA expression levels and modification of lipid A with PEtN ( Fig. 3 and S4). We therefore concluded that colistin resistance in the three O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx/non-x isolates and/or the O18-ST416 isolate is acquired by amino acid substitutions and deletions in PmrAB (Δ27–45 in PmrB, L105P in PmrA, or G206D in PmrB). These alterations appear to be necessary and sufficient for colistin resistance via lipid A modification with PEtN, and this is the first time such mutations have been found to contribute to colistin resistance in E. coli . 

PmrA/PmrB is known as a two-component signal transduction system that plays a role in survival in a wide variety of environments for Enterobacteriaceae ( 14 , 21 – 23 ). We found that SME98 mutants with pmrB replaced with mutant pmrB showed no significant differences in bacterial growth and competition from the parent strain ( Fig. 4 ). It appears that colistin-resistant E. coli isolates with mutations in pmrB can retain growth. This may explain the isolation of colistin-resistant E. coli from patients with no history of colistin/polymyxin B treatment. In contrast, SME98PORTpmrA105, which shares the L105P substitution in PmrA, was significantly suppressed in its growth in the presence of the parent strain. PmrA is a DNA binding response regulator that is activated by PmrB (autophosporylated histidine kinase) and directly controls the LPS-modifying genes, such as eptA , the arnBCADTEF operon, cptA (modifies PEtN to LPS core), lpxT (phosphorylates lipid A), and ugd (encoding UDP-glucose dehydrogenase) ( 14 , 22 ). PmrA also influences to synthesis of other outer membrane components, such as O antigen and peptidoglycan ( 22 , 23 ). Based on the above observations, this mechanism might be involved with the outcompeted phenotype in pmrA mutation in the presence of the competitor. 

In conclusion, the emergence and spread of colistin-resistant E. coli isolates are a major concern, especially if they acquire extensively multidrug resistance. In particular, ST131 is of great potential risk due to epidemic expansion of pathogenicity and multidrug resistance in clinical settings ( 3 ). In this study, we revealed the colistin resistance mechanism of E. coli clinical isolates, including major extraintestinal pathogenic multidrug-resistant lineages O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx and non-x, is due to novel amino acid substitutions and deletions in PmrAB that confer PEtN modification of lipid A. Verification of bacterial fitness in vivo and identification in colistin-resistant E. coli isolates should be addressed in future studies. However, careful attention should be paid to the use of colistin for the treatment of E. coli ST131 isolates because some of the isolates acquire colistin resistance via heterogeneous mutations in pmrAB without the suppression of bacterial growth. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Bacterial isolates. 

Four colistin-resistant E. coli isolates comprising SRE34 and SRE44 (O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx), SME222 (O25b:H4-ST131- H 30R non-x), and SME296 (O18-ST416) derived from clinical specimens (urine catheter, urine catheter, indwelling pericardial drain, and urine, respectively) were used in this study ( 16 ). Ten colistin-susceptible E. coli isolates (nine O25b:H4-ST131- H 30Rx or non-x isolates and E. coli ATCC 25922, with colistin MICs ranging from 0.125 to 0.5 mg/liter) were used as reference strains. SME98, a colistin-susceptible (MIC = 0.5 mg/liter) O25b:H4-ST131- H 30R non-x E. coli isolate derived from the urine of a female patient, was shown to be representative. The in vitro -derived rifampin-resistant SME98 mutant SME98RIF was selected on Mueller-Hinton agar (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) containing 32 mg/liter of rifampin by spreading SME98 at a cell density of ∼10 9 CFU/ml. 

Colistin and polymyxin B susceptibility testing. 

Colistin and polymyxin B were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industry (Osaka, Japan). Susceptibility to colistin and polymyxin B was tested using the broth microdilution method according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) ( 24 ). E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as a reference (colistin MIC = 0.25 mg/liter). A colistin MIC of >2 mg/liter was defined as resistant according to EUCAST ( 25 ). 

Detection of mcr-3 , - 4 , and - 5 and nucleotide sequencing of phoPQ and pmrA . 

Possession of mcr-3 , mcr-4 , and mcr-5 was detected by PCR as previously reported ( 10 – 12 ). Full nucleotide sequences of phoPQ and pmrAB were determined by direct DNA sequencing using primers listed in Table S1 and compared with those of E. coli strain K-12 substrain MG1655 (GenBank accession number U00096 ), which served as a reference strain. 

Real-time RT-PCR. 

Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in Mueller-Hinton II broth (cation adjusted; Becton Dickinson), and cells were grown for 1 h at 37°C with shaking at 140 rpm. RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Plus minikit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of RNA was measured spectrophotometrically using an Infinite M200 PRO instrument (Tecan, Kawasaki, Japan), and 0.5 μg was used to synthesize cDNA using ReverTra Ace reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) master mix with genomic DNA (gDNA) remover (Toyobo, Tokyo, Japan). Expression of eptA and arnT was estimated using KOD SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo) and primer pairs listed in Table S1. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial activation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Reactions were performed in a LightCycler 480 II (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Genes rrsE and gyrB served as an endogenous reference for normalizing expression levels ( 26 ). Data were calibrated against the baseline expression level of SME98, and fold change in expression was calculated using the comparative threshold cycle ( C T ) method. Data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations from three independent experiments. 

Isolation of LPS and lipid A from LPS. 

LPS was isolated by hot aqueous phenol extraction as described previously, with some modifications ( 27 ). Briefly, bacterial cells were suspended in sterile distilled water and stirred with an equal volume of 90% (vol/vol) phenol for 15 min at 70°C. After cooling, the upper aqueous layer was recovered by centrifugation (600 × g , 15 min, and 4°C) and dialyzed against water, and the collected LPS fraction was lyophilized. Lipid A was prepared from LPS fractions as described previously, with some modifications ( 28 ). Briefly, the LPS fraction was treated with 1 to 2% (vol/vol) acetic acid for 90 min at 100°C, and an equal volume of chloroform-methanol (2:1, vol/vol) was added and thoroughly stirred. After centrifugation (600 × g , 15 min, and 4°C), the lower organic layer was collected and used as the lipid A preparation. 

MALDI-TOF MS. 

The lipid A preparation dissolved in chloroform-methanol (2:1, vol/vol) was mixed with an equal amount of 10 mg/ml of 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHBA) matrix in water-methanol (7:3, vol/vol) containing 0.1% (wt/vol) trifluoroacetic acid on a target plate. After cocrystallization, MALDI-TOF MS spectra were acquired by integration of more than 2,000 shots at random in negative-ion and reflectron modes. The molecular mass was determined using Autoflex III smartbeam with FlexControl software (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA). 

Construction of pmrAB -deficient mutants. 

A series of pmrAB -deficient mutants were generated from the colistin-resistant E. coli isolates by homologous Red/ET recombination as described previously ( 29 ). The pmrAB genes were replaced with minigenes containing the kanamycin resistance cassette (Gene Bridges, Heidelberg, Germany) and 50 nucleotides corresponding to the upstream and downstream regions of pmrAB . These were amplified by PCR using primers pmrAB-RedF and pmrAB-RedR (Table S1). 

pmrAB competition assay. 

An expression vector harboring pmrAB from SME98 (named pHSGpmrAB98) was constructed using low-copy-number plasmid pHSG576 (obtained from the National BioResource Project, Mishima, Japan). Briefly, amplification of pmrAB (from −103 bp upstream to +65 bp downstream) from SME98 was performed by PCR using two pairs of primers, (i) pHSpmrAB-F1 and pHSGpmrAB-R1 and (ii) pHSG576pmrAB-F2 and pHSG576pmrAB-R2 (Table S1). EcoRI- and HindIII-digested pHSG576 was generated by inverse PCR using primers pHSG576EcoRIinvert2 and pMW219HindIIIinvert (Table S1) and digestion with DpnI. These three fragments were ligated using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly master mix (New England BioLabs Japan, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After confirmation of the sequence, pHSGpmrAB98 was electroporated into colistin-resistant isolates or SME98, and three different pHSGpmrAB98-introduced clones for each isolate were picked and subjected to determination of colistin MIC. Plasmid pHSG576 was used as a control in this assay. 

Gene replacement of mutated pmrAB into colistin-susceptible isolate SME98. 

Gene replacement of mutated pmrAB into SME98 was performed using the pORTMAGE method as previously described ( 20 ). Oligonucleotides (90 bp) for pmrA or pmrB containing the regions mutated in colistin-resistant isolates were designed with Mage Oligo Design Tool (MODEST [ http://modest.biosustain.dtu.dk ]) using the genome of E. coli ST131 strain EC958 (GenBank accession number HG941718.1 ) as a reference (Table S1). Gene replacement in the mutants was confirmed by direct DNA sequencing using primers listed in Table S1. 

Bacterial growth curve determination. 

Bacterial growth curves were determined by measuring values for optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) using an Infinite M200 PRO multimode microplate reader (Tecan). SME98 and pmrAB replacement mutants were grown in 0.5 ml of tryptic soy broth (Becton Dickinson) overnight at 37°C, and 1 × 10 5 CFU/ml bacteria were subsequently cultured in 0.1 ml of Mueller-Hinton II broth in a 96-well plate at 37°C with shaking at 140 rpm. OD 600 values were measured every 10 min for 16 h. 

Bacterial competition assay. 

Bacterial competition assays were performed using rifampin-resistant SME98 (SME98RIF) as a competitor for SME98 and its pmrAB replacement mutants. Cells were inoculated at a concentration of 1 × 10 5 CFU/ml into 1 ml of Mueller-Hinton II broth containing an equal number of SME98RIF cells and incubated at 37°C. After 72 h, 100 μl of serial dilutions of cell suspensions containing 0.85% NaCl was seeded on plain and rifampin (32 mg/liter)-containing Mueller-Hinton agar plates. After overnight incubation at 37°C, colonies were counted. 

Statistical analysis. 

Statistical significance was determined using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.
