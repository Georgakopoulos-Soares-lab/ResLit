                     Acinetobacter baumannii is now one of the most frequently encountered nosocomial pathogens in intensive therapy units, and is renowned for being difficult to treat because of resistance to most antibiotics. Carbapenems are the remaining drugs of choice in many centres, but carbapenem resistance is now emerging in strains worldwide. Two subgroups of carbapenem-hydrolysing β-lactamases, which differ in their amino-acid homology, have been found in some resistant strains. This report describes the emergence and characterisation of a novel carbapenemase (OXA-51) in genetically distinct carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii strains from Argentina. Enzyme kinetics and inhibitor studies were performed spectrophotometrically with purified β-lactamase. Amplification of the gene was achieved with a two-step PCR method employing arbitrary partially degenerate and gene-specific primers. Transfer of imipenem resistance was attempted with the use of broth and membrane filter methods. Attempts to produce plasmid-cured variants were made in ethidium bromide curing experiments. OXA-51 was identified in two clones of A. baumannii, and was found to have &lt; 63% amino-acid identity with subgroups 1 and 2. Enzyme kinetic studies confirmed that OXA-51 was a molecular class D enzyme with carbapenemase activity, and that it displayed the highest affinity for imipenem (Km
                      value 11 μm). Sequence analysis of the gene identified distinct differences within conserved class D motifs when compared with subgroups 1 and 2. Attempts to transfer imipenem resistance and to determine a plasmid location for the gene failed. OXA-51 is the first of a new subgroup of carbapenemases to emerge in multiresistant clinical isolates of A. baumannii.

                  <ce:simple-para id="spara90" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> is now one of the most frequently encountered nosocomial pathogens in intensive therapy units, and is renowned for being difficult to treat because of resistance to most antibiotics. Carbapenems are the remaining drugs of choice in many centres, but carbapenem resistance is now emerging in strains worldwide. Two subgroups of carbapenem-hydrolysing β-lactamases, which differ in their amino-acid homology, have been found in some resistant strains. This report describes the emergence and characterisation of a novel carbapenemase (OXA-51) in genetically distinct carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> strains from Argentina. Enzyme kinetics and inhibitor studies were performed spectrophotometrically with purified β-lactamase. Amplification of the gene was achieved with a two-step PCR method employing arbitrary partially degenerate and gene-specific primers. Transfer of imipenem resistance was attempted with the use of broth and membrane filter methods. Attempts to produce plasmid-cured variants were made in ethidium bromide curing experiments. OXA-51 was identified in two clones of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>, and was found to have &lt; 63% amino-acid identity with subgroups 1 and 2. Enzyme kinetic studies confirmed that OXA-51 was a molecular class D enzyme with carbapenemase activity, and that it displayed the highest affinity for imipenem (<ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic> value 11 μ<ce:small-caps>m</ce:small-caps>). Sequence analysis of the gene identified distinct differences within conserved class D motifs when compared with subgroups 1 and 2. Attempts to transfer imipenem resistance and to determine a plasmid location for the gene failed. OXA-51 is the first of a new subgroup of carbapenemases to emerge in multiresistant clinical isolates of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="cesec10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle30">INTRODUCTION</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="para10" view="all">The incidence and spread of resistant bacteria within hospitals are now of global concern. While much attention has been focused on methicillin-resistant <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic>, the incidence of the Gram-negative organism <ce:italic>Acinetobacter baumannii</ce:italic> as a causal agent of infection has increased steadily [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref210" refid="bib1">1</ce:cross-ref>]. Acinetobacters are renowned for their ability to survive in the environment in dry conditions for prolonged periods, and environmental contamination represents an important reservoir for their dissemination [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref220" refid="bib2">2</ce:cross-ref>]. <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> is the species isolated most frequently, and is now recognised as an important cause of infection, particularly in compromised patients with such risk factors as mechanical ventilation, surgery and prolonged stays in the intensive therapy unit (ITU) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib1 bib3">1,3</ce:cross-refs>]. In many hospitals, <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> is now one of the most commonly encountered Gram-negative pathogens in the ITU [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib4 bib5">4,5</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para20" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> has the ability to rapidly acquire resistance to most antibiotics [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref230" refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>], and the worldwide emergence of multiresistant strains that are also resistant to the carbapenems, a class of antibiotic that has been the mainstay of therapy for infections caused by resistant strains, has been a worrying trend. Consequently, the use of these antibiotics has become seriously compromised [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240" refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>]. Major outbreaks of carbapenem-resistant strains have been reported in many centres worldwide [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib8 bib9 bib10 bib11 bib12 bib13">8–13</ce:cross-refs>], and pan-drug resistance is now emerging [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib14 bib15">14,15</ce:cross-refs>]. β-Lactamase-mediated carbapenem resistance is being reported with increasing frequency in clinical strains, and several Ambler class B metallo-β-lactamases of the IMP and VIM families have been identified, predominantly in isolates from parts of Asia, southern Europe [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib16 bib17 bib18 bib19">16–19</ce:cross-refs>] and the UK [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib20 bib21">20,21</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para30" view="all">An additional unexpected development has been the discovery worldwide of molecular class D β-lactamases in clinical isolates that have acquired the ability to hydrolyse carbapenems. These OXA-type enzymes are normally found in bacteria such as <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic>, and are not usually associated with carbapenem resistance [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref250" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>]. So far, two subgroups of OXA-type carbapenemases have been identified in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>. The first such enzyme, OXA-23, was identified in an isolate from a patient in Edinburgh, and along with OXA-27, isolated in Singapore, comprises subgroup 1, both sharing 99% amino-acid identity [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs70" refid="bib23 bib24 bib25">23–25</ce:cross-refs>]. The sequence of another carbapenemase, OXA-49 (China), has been reported (GenBank/EMBL accession number AY288523), but the specific details have yet to be published. However, based on amino-acid identity, it also belongs to subgroup 1. Subgroup 2 comprises OXA-24, −25, −26 and −40, which were identified in isolates from Spain, Belgium, France and Portugal, and which differ by one or two amino-acids [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs80" refid="bib25 bib26 bib27">25–27</ce:cross-refs>]. Subgroups 1 and 2 share 60% identity, and a characteristic of both is the presence of a phenylalanine (Phe) residue instead of a tyrosine (Tyr) in the conserved class D Y–G–N motif of the protein at DBL position 144 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref260" refid="bib28">28</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="para40" view="all">A previous report described the presence of carbapenemase activity in multiresistant <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> isolates from Buenos Aires, Argentina [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref270" refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>]. The present study demonstrates that these isolates contain a novel class D carbapenemase, which represents a new group of carbapenemases in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic>.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>

                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle40">MATERIALS AND METHODS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec30" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle50">Bacterial isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para50" view="all">Six isolates of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> were included in this study, all of which were collected originally between October 1993 and November 1994 from an outbreak involving carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> in three hospitals in Buenos Aires, Argentina. The presence of carbapenemase activity in these isolates, and the results of typing by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), have been reported previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref280" refid="bib30">30</ce:cross-ref>]. Four isolates (779, 780, 781 and 782) originated from a small teaching hospital. Isolates 786 and 788 originated from a second teaching hospital and a geriatric hospital, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref290" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec40" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle60">Antimicrobial susceptibility testing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para60" view="all">Antibiotics and inhibitors were obtained as follows: ampicillin, benzylpenicillin, cefotaxime, cefuroxime, cephaloridine, cloxacillin, oxacillin, EDTA and NaCl from Sigma (Poole, UK); ciprofloxacin from Bayer (Newbury, UK); ceftazidime from GlaxoWellcome (Uxbridge, UK); imipenem from Merck Sharp and Dohme (Haddesdon, UK); meropenem from Zeneca Pharmaceuticals (Macclesfield, UK); rifampicin from 1Aventis Pharma (West Malling, UK); gentamicin from David Bull Laboratories (Warwick, UK); clavulanic acid and nitrocephin from GlaxoSmithKline (Collegeville, PA, USA); and sulbactam from Pfizer (Sandwich, UK). MICs were determined according to the guidelines of the Working Party on Antibiotic Sensitivity Testing [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs90" refid="bib31 bib32">31,32</ce:cross-refs>] on IsoSensitest agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK). Agar plates containing serially diluted antibiotics were inoculated with a final inoculum of 10<ce:sup loc="post">4</ce:sup> CFU/spot. The recommended NCTC strains were included as controls [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref300" refid="bib31">31</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec50" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle70">Preparation and analysis of β-lactamase extracts</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para70" view="all">Cell cultures were grown in nutrient broth overnight at 37°C with shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and cellfree lysates were prepared by sonication. β-Lactamases were identified by isoelectric focusing (IEF) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref310" refid="bib33">33</ce:cross-ref>], with β-lactamase activity being detected by an overlay of filter paper soaked in nitrocephin (50 mg/L). Known β-lactamases were included as controls.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec60" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle80">Transfer of resistance and curing experiments</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para80" view="all">Transfer of resistance from representative strain 788 to the rifampicin-resistant recipient <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> sp. BD413 was attempted by the broth [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref320" refid="bib34">34</ce:cross-ref>] and membrane filter [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref330" refid="bib35">35</ce:cross-ref>] methods at 25°C and 37°C, with selection of transconjugants on nutrient agar plates containing imipenem (4 mg/L) and rifampicin (50 mg/L). To determine whether imipenem resistance was plasmid-encoded, curing experiments were performed by growing cultures overnight at 37°C in serial dilutions of ethidium bromide in nutrient broth. Cured variants were selected on IsoSensitest agar plates with and without imipenem (4 mg/L).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec70" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle90">β-Lactamase purification and determination of molecular mass</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para90" view="all">Partial purification of OXA-51 from representative strain 788 was performed on a MonoQ anion exchange column by Fast 2Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) (Amersham Biotech, Little Chalfont, UK) with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Protein was eluted in a 1 mM NaCl gradient. Fractions were assayed with nitrocephin, and the presence of specific β-lactamases in positive fractions was determined with IEF. Pooled fractions were applied to an FPLC Superdex 75 Hiload 20/60 gel filtration column (flow rate of 1.5 mL/min). Fractions containing β-lactamase were concentrated with a VectaSpin Micro centrifuge tube filter (Whatman, Maidstone, UK). β-Lactamase molecular mass was determined by sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a 4–15% linear gradient Tris-HCl gel (Bio-Rad, Hemel Hempstead, UK). Renaturation of β-lactamase activity was achieved with Triton X-100 1% v/v at 37°C. Activity was detected by overlaying the gel with nitrocephin (50 mg/L).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec80" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle100">Enzyme kinetics studies</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para100" view="all">Purified β-lactamase from strain 788 was assayed, with the use of a double-beam UV/V Lambda 2 spectrophotometer (Perkin-Elmer, Beaconsfield, UK), in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0, at 37°C for 5 min. <ce:italic>V<ce:inf loc="post">max</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> and <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> values were determined by linear regression analysis of Lineweaver–Burk plots of initial velocity data obtained at different substrate concentrations [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref340" refid="bib36">36</ce:cross-ref>]. Inhibition assays (ID<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> values) were performed by incubating β-lactamase with inhibitor concentrations for 10 min at 37°C. Nitrocephin (100 μM) was added as the reporter substrate, and the initial rates of hydrolysis were determined.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec90" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle110">PCR analysis</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para110" view="all">DNA was extracted from strains by boiling one to three colonies in 100 μL of sterile ultrapure water for 10 min and centrifuging briefly. A 2-μL volume of this crude DNA extract was used for each PCR. Reactions were carried out in 50-μL volumes containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.8), 10 mM KCl, 10 mM (NH<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>)<ce:inf loc="post">2</ce:inf>SO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, 2 mM MgSO<ce:inf loc="post">4</ce:inf>, 0.8 mM dNTPs and 1.25 U of <ce:italic>Pfu</ce:italic> DNA Polymerase (Promega, Southampton, UK). Amplifications were performed on a Techne Px2 gradient thermal cycler (Thermo Hybaid, Middlesex, UK). The primers employed in this study are listed in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref350" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/> and were used at a concentration of 10 pmol/μL unless otherwise stated.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec100" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle120">Amplification with subgroup 1- and subgroup 2-specific primers</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Specific primers for <ce:italic>bla<ce:inf loc="post">OXA-23</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> (subgroup 1) were used under the following conditions: one cycle of 94°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 35°C for 30 s with a gradient of 15°C, and 72°C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Primers for <ce:italic>bla<ce:inf loc="post">OXA-24</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> (subgroup 2) were used under the following conditions: one cycle of 94°C for 4 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 50°C for 1 min and 72°C for 2 min, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. A 498-bp fragment of OXA-51 was amplified with the use of OXA degenerate primers (both at 50 pmol/μL), under the following conditions: one cycle of 94°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 55°C for 1 min and 72°C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 2 min.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="cesec110" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle130">Amplification of the OXA-51 gene by a two-step PCR method</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para130" view="all">A modification of a two-step PCR method described by Sørensen <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib37">37</ce:cross-ref>] was used to amplify the 5'-and 3'-ends of <ce:italic>bla<ce:inf loc="post">OXA-51</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic>. This comprised an initial amplification with an arbitrary partially degenerate primer with a fixed 3'-end and an OXA-51-specific internal primer from the known 498-bp fragment. A second PCR with 2-μL aliquots of purified amplicons was performed with a non-degenerate primer derived from the arbitrary primer, together with a nested OXA-51-specific primer. Primers were used at a concentration of 25 pmol/μL, with the exception of degenerate arbitrary primers (75 pmol/μL). PCRs were run with the following conditions: one cycle of 94°C for 4 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 1.2 min, 40°C with a gradient of 15°C for 1.2 min, and 72°C for 3 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Products (10 μL) were analysed by electrophoresis on agarose 1.5% w/v gels and stained with ethidium bromide. Amplicons were purified directly (Qiagen, Crawley, UK), or bands were excised from a low-melting-temperature agarose 1.5% w/v gel and purified (Qiagen). Amplicons from three separate PCRs were sequenced in both directions on an ABI Prism automated sequencer (Perkin-Elmer). Protein sequences were aligned with the use of hierarchical clustering [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib38">38</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>

                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle140">PCR analysis with class 1 integron primers</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para140" view="all">Class 1 integron-specific primers were used under the following conditions: 30 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 55°C for 1 min and 72°C for 3 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> strain J53 carrying pR388, and strain J53 carrying pR483, were included as positive and negative controls for PCR analysis.</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="cesec130" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle150">RESULTS</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="cesec140" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle160">Bacterial isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para150" view="all">Two distinct clusters were identified by PFGE analysis of bacterial isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>). Cluster A comprised four isolates (779, 780, 781 and 782) with 90–97% similarity. The remaining two isolates (786 and 788) comprised cluster E; they had identical PFGE patterns (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:section id="cesec150" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle170">MICs and IEF analysis of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> clinical isolates</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para160" view="all">With the exception of three isolates (779, 780 and 781) that were sensitive to gentamicin, resistance was detected in all isolates to all antibiotics tested, including imipenem (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="tbl3">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl3"/>). MIC values at or above the recommended breakpoint value were found for meropenem.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para170" view="all">A chromosomal cephalosporinase, which focused at pI &gt; 8 (data not shown), was detected by IEF analysis in all isolates. A band that co-focused with a TEM-1 β-lactamase control was also present in isolate 786. An unknown β-lactamase band (pI 7.0) was identified in all isolates. This band did not co-focus with the control β-lactamases and was not present in the crude extract from an imipenem-sensitive strain included as a clinical control.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec160" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle180">Purification and biochemical properties of the β-lactamase of pI 7.0</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para180" view="all">A representative isolate (788) was chosen for further investigation of the pI 7.0 β-lactamase. The molecular mass of the purified β-lactamase was estimated to be 35.5 kDa. Kinetic studies were performed with purified β-lactamase. Ampicillin was the best substrate, indicating that the enzyme was predominantly a penicillinase (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl4"/>). None of the cephalosporins was hydrolysed, with the exception of cephaloridine (10% relative <ce:italic>V<ce:inf loc="post">max</ce:inf>
                           </ce:italic>). Slow hydrolysis of imipenem was detected, and the enzyme had the highest affinity for this substrate, as indicated by a low <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                           </ce:italic> value (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). No hydrolysis of meropenem was observed, but hydrolysis of both oxacillin and cloxacillin confirmed that this was an OXA-type enzyme.</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para190" view="all">For determination of inhibition of β-lactamase activity, ID<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> values of inhibitors were calculated by preincubating a range of concentrations with the enzyme and measuring the initial rate of hydrolysis of a reporter substrate. This demonstrated inhibition of enzyme activity by sulbactam and chloride ions (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). Although not inhibitors <ce:italic>per se</ce:italic>, both carbapenems were included in the inhibitor studies to confirm whether the β-lactamase was able to bind them via its active site. Imipenem and meropenem produced 50% enzyme inhibition at 2.5 μM and 0.004 μM, respectively (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="tbl4">Table 4</ce:cross-ref>). Enzyme activity was inhibited weakly by clavulanate. Neither EDTA nor cloxacillin were efficient enzyme inhibitors. These results indicated that the pI 7.0 β-lactamase was an OXA-type class D carbapenemase.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec170" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle190">Transfer of imipenem resistance</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para200" view="all">Broth and membrane filter matings failed to demonstrate transfer of resistance in representative strain 788. In addition, PCRs with class 1 integron-specific primers were unsuccessful, and ethidium bromide curing experiments failed to detect cured variants.</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                     <ce:section id="cesec180" view="all">
                        <ce:section-title id="cestitle200">PCR analysis</ce:section-title>
                        <ce:para id="para210" view="all">PCR with primers specific for the β-lactamaseencoding genes of OXA subgroups 1 and 2 failed to amplify the carbapenemase gene in strains 779 (representing PFGE pattern A) and 788 (representing PFGE pattern E). However, degenerate primers designed from conserved amino-acid regions within the genes encoding both subgroup carbapenemases amplified an internal 498-bp gene fragment from both strains. Specific primers from within this fragment were paired with partially degenerate arbitrary primers to amplify the remaining 5’ and 3’ gene regions. Sequencing of PCR products from both clones identified a gene that comprised an 825-bp open reading frame encoding a 274-amino-acid protein (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                           <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/>).</ce:para>
                        <ce:para id="para220" view="all">Significant differences were identified when the amino-acid sequence was compared with those of subgroups 1 and 2, confirming that the pI 7.0 carbapenemase was novel (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). This enzyme has subsequently been designated OXA-51 and shares 56% and 61–62% identity with subgroups 1 and 2, respectively. The sequence contained the conserved serine active site motif S–T–F–K (DBL numbering 70–73). The K–T–G triad (DBL numbering 216–218) was replaced by K–S–G (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). Importantly, OXA-51 differed from both subgroups in the sequence of two other conserved class D motifs. There was a Val → Ile change in the S–X–V triad (X denoting any amino acid) (DBL numbering 118–120), and Tyr of the Y–G–N motif (DBL numbering 144–146) was retained (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>). When OXA-51 was compared with noncarbapenem-hydrolysing class D enzymes, the highest similarity was found with OXA-10 (PSE-2) (33%), OXA-5 and OXA-11 (both 32%).</ce:para>
                     </ce:section>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="cesec190" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title id="cestitle210">DISCUSSION</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="para230" view="all">This study identified the emergence of a novel class D carbapenemase in genetically unrelated multiresistant clinical isolates of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> from Argentina. Class D β-lactamases belong to group 2d of the Bush functional classification scheme, and are characterised by their ability to hydrolyse the isoxazolyl penicillins and methicillin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>]. Although they occur predominantly in <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic>, they have been detected in other Gram-negative bacteria [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para240" view="all">The kinetic properties of OXA-51 were not typical of those of group 2d β-lactamases, in that only slow hydrolysis of oxacillin and cloxacillin was demonstrated. Indeed, the <ce:italic>V<ce:inf loc="post">max</ce:inf>/K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> ratio for oxacillin was over two-fold lower than that observed for cephaloridine. With a few exceptions, oxacillinases of this group hydrolyse these β-lactams far more effectively [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>]. Among the carbapenem-hydrolysing oxacillinases characterised so far in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic>, OXA-27 from subgroup 1 also demonstrated weak activity against oxacillin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib25">25</ce:cross-ref>], while OXA-24 from subgroup 2 failed completely to hydrolyse oxacillin, cloxacillin and methicillin [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref530" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>]. None of the cephalosporins was hydrolysed by OXA-51, with the exception of cephaloridine, which was a weaker substrate than ampicillin. This was a similar profile to that of the other characterised class D carbapenemases, although most have demonstrated slow hydrolysis of some cephalosporins [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs100" refid="bib24 bib25 bib26 bib27">24–27</ce:cross-refs>]. Slow hydrolysis of imipenem by OXA-51 was detected, but not of meropenem, and this was also demonstrated with OXA-23 and OXA-40 [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs110" refid="bib27 bib40">27,40</ce:cross-refs>]. In contrast, the other class D carbapenemases were reported to hydrolyse both substrates, albeit slowly [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs120" refid="bib25 bib26">25,26</ce:cross-refs>]. Importantly, however, OXA-51 had the highest affinity for imipenem, as demonstrated by the lowest <ce:italic>K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic> value.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para250" view="all">Inhibition of OXA-51 activity was detected with NaCl (a class D inhibitor), and this was also reported for OXA-24 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref540" refid="bib26">26</ce:cross-ref>]. Inhibition was also detected with the class A inhibitor clavulanic acid, which also inhibits most class D enzymes [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref550" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>]. With the exception of OXA-23 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref560" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>] and OXA-40 [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref570" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>], the ID<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> value detected for OXA-51 with this inhibitor was higher than the values observed for the other class D carbapenemases in subgroups 1 and 2. Despite the lack of meropenem hydrolysis by OXA-51, low ID<ce:inf loc="post">50</ce:inf> values were determined for imipenem and meropenem, indicating high enzyme affinity for both carbapenems. Failure of the metal ion chelator EDTA to inhibit the activity of OXA-51 confirmed that it was not a metallo-β-lactamase, all of which are characterised by the requirement for a zinc ion at their active site [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref580" refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para260" view="all">Significant amino-acid differences were identified when the sequence of OXA-51 was compared to those of subgroups 1 and 2. In particular, changes were observed within conserved structural motifs that are important for the functioning of the enzyme active site [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs130" refid="bib39 bib41">39,41</ce:cross-refs>]. OXA-51 had a Thr → Ser change in the K–T–G motif (DBL numbering 216–218), as seen in the subgroup 2 carbapenemases [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs140" refid="bib25 bib26 bib27">25–27</ce:cross-refs>], but not in subgroup 1 enzymes [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs150" refid="bib24 bib25">24,25</ce:cross-refs>]. A unique Val → Ile change in the S–X–V motif, that is not present in the other class D carbapenemases sequenced, was identified. Importantly, OXA-51 also retains the Y–G–N motif (DBL 144–146), in contrast to the carbapenemases of subgroups 1 and 2, in which Tyr of this motif is replaced by Phe [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs160" refid="bib24 bib25 bib26 bib27">24–27</ce:cross-refs>]. Consequently, OXA-51 is the first class D carbapenemase to be found in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> that lacks this substitution. This finding contradicts the general opinion that substitution of Tyr with Phe in these carbapenemases confers hydrolytic activity against the carbapenems [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs170" refid="bib24 bib26">24,26</ce:cross-refs>]. Site-directed mutagenesis experiments with OXA-40 have indicated that this residue change is not solely responsible for carbapenem-hydrolysing activity as originally thought, but rather, that it confers overall weak hydrolytic activity [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref590" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>]. In terms of structure–function relationships, further research is required to determine the significance of these changes.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para270" view="all">OXA-51 is the first class D carbapenemase to be sequenced in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> clinical isolates from Argentina. The biochemical properties of another carbapenemase from a single isolate in Buenos Aires have been reported [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref600" refid="bib42">42</ce:cross-ref>]. Some differences were observed in their kinetics, in that oxacillin was a better substrate for this enzyme than for OXA-51, but it was less active against imipenem in terms of <ce:italic>V<ce:inf loc="post">max</ce:inf>/K<ce:inf loc="post">m</ce:inf>
                        </ce:italic>. The gene encoding this β-lactamase has never been sequenced, and it remains to be determined whether it is related to OXA-51.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para280" view="all">The presence of OXA-51 in different clones of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> obtained from three hospitals within Buenos Aires indicated that horizontal transfer of the encoding gene had occurred. However, attempts to transfer imipenem resistance by conjugation failed, although conjugation in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. is notoriously difficult to achieve [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref610" refid="bib43">43</ce:cross-ref>]. In addition, PCR with class 1 integron primers proved unsuccessful, and imipenem-sensitive variants were not detected in curing experiments. With the exception of OXA-23, which has been shown to be plasmid-encoded [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref620" refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>], the class D carbapenemases described so far in <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> are reported to be chromosomally located [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs180" refid="bib25 bib26 bib27">25–27</ce:cross-refs>]. In addition, none has been associated with mobile elements such as integrons, which would facilitate their transfer. This is unusual, since most of the conventional class D β-lactamase genes in Enterobacteriaceae and <ce:italic>Pseudomonas</ce:italic> are on large transferable plasmids, often within the gene cassettes of integrons [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref630" refid="bib22">22</ce:cross-ref>]. However, OXA-23 has also been found in several clonally related strains of <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic>, although the presence of this carbapenemase was not sufficient in itself to cause an increase in the MIC of imipenem above the breakpoint for these strains [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref640" refid="bib45">45</ce:cross-ref>]. OXA-40 has been discovered in two clones of <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref650" refid="bib11">11</ce:cross-ref>], even though its encoding gene has been reported to be chromosomally located, and analysis of the flanking sequences has provided no evidence to suggest that it is associated with an integron structure [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref660" refid="bib27">27</ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para290" view="all">The origin of these unusual carbapenemases remains unclear. They may have always been present in a number of <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> strains, perhaps as a resistance mechanism against antibiotic-producing soil microorganisms. Imipenem is a derivative of thienamycin, a natural product of <ce:italic>Streptomyces cattleya</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref670" refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>]. OXA-23 was discovered in an imipenem-resistant clinical isolate from a patient in 1985, before imipenem was in use [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref680" refid="bib23">23</ce:cross-ref>]. In addition, a region upstream from the gene encoding this carbapenemase was found to have 90% homology with a sequence previously identified in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> strains isolated from sludge [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref690" refid="bib24">24</ce:cross-ref>]. Alternatively, related genes may have spread into <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> spp. from unknown source organisms on plasmids that have subsequently become integrated into the chromosome. A plasmid-encoded class D carbapenemase (OXA-48) that has recently been discovered in a carbapenem-resistant <ce:italic>Klebsiella pneumoniae</ce:italic> strain in Turkey [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref700" refid="bib47">47</ce:cross-ref>] shares 92% amino-acid identity with a novel class D carbapenemase (OXA-54) found in an environmental reference strain of <ce:italic>Shewanella oneidensis</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref710" refid="bib48">48</ce:cross-ref>]. In addition, a clinical isolate of <ce:italic>Shewanella algae</ce:italic> has been found to harbour a chromosome-encoded class D β-lactamase which hydrolyses carbapenems, albeit slowly [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref720" refid="bib49">49</ce:cross-ref>]. These findings shed some light on the potential reservoirs of class D carbapenemases. Comparison of OXA-48 and OXA-54 with OXA-51 identified amino-acid similarities of only 32% and 33%, respectively. Importantly, however, as with OXA-51, the Y–G–N motif is conserved in OXA-48, OXA-54 and OXA-55 [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs190" refid="bib47 bib48 bib49">47–49</ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="para300" view="all">Whatever their origin, the incidence of class D carbapenemases in <ce:italic>Acinetobacter</ce:italic> is increasing and is now of major clinical concern. Within the last year, OXA-23 has been reported in genetically related carbapenem-resistant strains from Brazil [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref730" refid="bib50">50</ce:cross-ref>], in an <ce:italic>A. junii</ce:italic> strain from China (GenBank/EMBL accession number AY554200) and in two <ce:italic>A. baumannii</ce:italic> clones from England [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref740" refid="bib51">51</ce:cross-ref>], some 11 years after it was first reported. Importantly, these multiresistant strains are contributing to the deaths of patients, as neither complex combination therapy nor treatment with polymyxin B (which has been shown to have in-vitro activity against some multiresistant strains) is guaranteed to succeed [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs200" refid="bib10 bib50 bib52">10,50,52</ce:cross-refs>]. From the results of the present study, we conclude that OXA-51 is the first of a new subgroup of class D carbapenemases to emerge in <ce:italic>A. baumannii.</ce:italic> It can be anticipated that the incidence of carbapenem-resistant strains will continue to increase, which will have a severe impact on future therapeutic choices for infections caused by this pathogen.</ce:para>
