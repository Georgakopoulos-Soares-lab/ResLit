                  Abstract
                  In 2007, a significant increase in acquired ampC genes in Enterobacteriaceae from 0.06% in 1999 to 1.3% was observed. Proteus mirabilis showed the highest prevalence (0.95%) and CMY-2 was the most prevalent AmpC enzyme (66.7%). Other enzymes such as CMY-4, DHA-1, ACC-1, and three new enzymes called CMY-25, CMY-27 and CMY-40 were detected. Seven out of the 117 isolates (6%) also produced an extended-spectrum β-lactamase. As acquired AmpC enzymes are likely to become a serious public health issue worldwide, close surveillance is necessary to curb their spread.

            <ce:abstract id="ceab10" view="all" class="author"><ce:abstract-sec id="ceabs10" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="cestitle10">Abstract</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="spara30" view="all">In 2007, a significant increase in acquired <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic> genes in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> from 0.06% in 1999 to 1.3% was observed. <ce:italic>Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic> showed the highest prevalence (0.95%) and CMY-2 was the most prevalent AmpC enzyme (66.7%). Other enzymes such as CMY-4, DHA-1, ACC-1, and three new enzymes called CMY-25, CMY-27 and CMY-40 were detected. Seven out of the 117 isolates (6%) also produced an extended-spectrum β-lactamase. As acquired AmpC enzymes are likely to become a serious public health issue worldwide, close surveillance is necessary to curb their spread.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

               <ce:para id="para10" view="all">Acquired AmpCs appeared in the late 1980s and have been detected mainly in isolates of <ce:italic>Klebsiella</ce:italic> spp., <ce:italic>Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Salmonella</ce:italic> spp. although they have also been identified in other species including natural AmpC producers [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref360" refid="bib1">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">1</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. These enzymes confer resistance to most β-lactams – including cephamycins – with the exception of cefepime and carbapenems [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref370" refid="bib2">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para20" view="all">Most acquired <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic>s derive from chromosomal <ce:italic>amp</ce:italic>C genes of the family <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> (<ce:italic>Citrobacter freundii, Enterobacter</ce:italic> spp., <ce:italic>Morganella morganii</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>Hafnia alvei</ce:italic>) whereas the origin of others remains unknown. Isolates harbouring acquired <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic>s are usually multi-resistant [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs10" refid="bib3 bib4 bib5 bib6">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3–6</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>], limiting the therapeutic options even further. In this context, we aimed to determine the prevalence of acquired AmpCs in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> isolates lacking inducible chromosomal <ce:italic>amp</ce:italic>C genes at a Spanish hospital from January 1999 to December 2007.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para30" view="all">Isolates were obtained from routine cultures at the Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau (Barcelona, Spain). When there were multiple isolates from a patient within a 30-day period, only one was considered for analysis. Isolates were identified using standard methods [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref380" refid="bib7">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">7</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. The disc diffusion susceptibility test was performed according to Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref390" refid="bib8">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">8</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>], using commercially available discs (Bio-Rad, Marnes La Coquette, France). The production of Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) was studied using the double-disc synergy test and confirmed when necessary by Etest ESBL (AB Biodisk, Solna, Sweden) [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs20" refid="bib3 bib9">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3,9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para40" view="all">As some acquired AmpCs do not confer resistance to cefoxitin, the strains selected for this study were those showing intermediate resistance or resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, cefotaxime or ceftazidime according to CLSI breakpoints [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref400" refid="bib8">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">8</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>], and negative results for ESBL production. Isolates which screened positive for ESBL production and showed intermediate susceptibility or resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic or cefoxitin were also included [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref410" refid="bib10">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">10</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. Acquired AmpCs were characterized using the previously described multiplex PCR [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref420" refid="bib2">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. Amplicons were purified and sequenced as described previously [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref430" refid="bib9">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. PCR-positive isolates were tested using a double-disc synergy test based on the utilization of cloxacillin (500 µg) as inhibitor of AmpC enzymes (except <ce:italic>Escherichia coli</ce:italic> strains). All PCR-positive isolates were also tested for the presence of scattered colonies in the inhibition halo of cefoxitin, cefotaxime, ceftazidime and aztreonam [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref440" refid="bib10">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">10</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para50" view="all">Among the 27 119 isolates of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> lacking inducible chromosomal AmpC β-lactamases, 437 isolates were studied as putative acquired AmpC producers. We obtained amplicons in 117: 75 <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>, 20 <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic>, 16 <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic>, four <ce:italic>K. oxytoca</ce:italic> and two <ce:italic>S. enterica</ce:italic>. The remaining 320 isolates were ESBL producers, hyperproducers of chromosomal AmpC enzymes (<ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic>), or hyperproducers of class A enzymes (<ce:italic>Klebsiella</ce:italic> spp.). Moreover, other non-enzymatic resistance mechanisms such as altered permeability may also have been present in these isolates. A few of the 117 isolates included in this study have been described previously [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs30" refid="bib7 bib9 bib10">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">7,9,10</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para60" view="all">The 117 isolates were recovered from urine (66.7%), fluids and tissue (14.5%), blood (10.3%), respiratory tract (3.4%) or other samples (5.1%). Most samples were from ambulatory patients (64.1%).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para70" view="all">The overall prevalence of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> carrying acquired <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic>s was 0.43%, rising from 0.06% (1999) to 1.3% (2007). This significant increase (p &lt;0.001; contingency tablechi-square test was used for evaluation; spss V15 software; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), which occurred mainly in the last 3 years, could have been as a result of the emergence of <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic>-producing DHA (16 out of 40 in 2007) and the increase of CMY-2-producing <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic>. The highest prevalence was found in <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> (0.95%), as in a recent survey in Polish hospitals where acquired AmpCs were observed exclusively in <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> (20.5%) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref450" refid="bib11">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">11</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. Other studies found no acquired-AmpC-producing <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref460" refid="bib5">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">5</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>] or found it at a lower rate (0.75%) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref470" refid="bib12">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">12</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. It is of note that in our setting, <ce:italic>P. mirabilis</ce:italic> is acquiring different types of β-lactamases, including AmpCs [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref480" refid="bib9">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para80" view="all">CMY-2 has a worldwide distribution. In our study, it was the predominant enzyme (66.7%), followed by DHA-1 (25.6%). DHA-1 was mainly associated with <ce:italic>Klebsiella</ce:italic> spp. and was the only acquired AmpC detected in <ce:italic>K. oxytoca.</ce:italic> Less commonly found enzymes were ACC-1, CMY-4, CMY-25, CMY-27 and CMY-40. The last three are reported here for first time (their amino acid substitutions and the corresponding susceptibility test results are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240s" refid="tbl1">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl1"/>). Seven (6%) out of the 117 acquired-AmpC-producing isolates also produced an ESBL (<ce:cross-ref id="cecref240d" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                  <ce:float-anchor refid="tbl2"/>). The prevalence and type of acquired AmpCs differs depending upon the geographical area, the species studied and the period of study [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs40" refid="bib4 bib11 bib12 bib13 bib14">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">4,11–14</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>], possibly as a result of the selection criteria used. For this reason, it is difficult to compare the prevalence of acquired AmpCs between studies.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para90" view="all">Resistance of the AmpC-producers to non-β-lactam antibiotics was high. Isolates showed resistance to nalidixic acid (74.4%), ciprofloxacin (51.3%), tetracycline (67.5%), chloramphenicol (43.6%), sulphonamides (61.5%), trimethoprim (43.6%) and aminoglycosides such as streptomycin (52.1%), kanamycin (43.6%), gentamicin (36.7%) and tobramycin (34.2%).</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para100" view="all">The cloxacillin test was positive for all analysed isolates. Using the disc diffusion method, 86.3% of isolates showed an inhibition halo to third-generation cephalosporins (13–33 mm) and aztreonam (13–43 mm). Most of these (91.1%) showed scattered colonies near the edge of the inhibition zones. Both these phenotypic tests are useful to detect the presence of acquired AmpCs in <ce:italic>Enterobacteriaceae</ce:italic> lacking inducible chromosomal AmpC. Nevertheless, as previously reported [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref490" refid="bib15">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">15</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>], the cloxacillin test does not allow differentiation between chromosomal and acquired AmpC enzymes. PCR is the still the most reliable test in these cases.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para110" view="all">The clonal diffusion of these enzymes was analysed by clinical and molecular epidemiology. Enterobacterial repetitive intergenic consensus (ERIC)-PCR was used as a first approach for <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> strains [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs50" refid="bib2 bib9">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">2,9</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>]. Those that showed ERIC patterns with &gt;80% homology were then analysed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref500" refid="bib3">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">3</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>]. PFGE was also used to study the clonal relationship of the remaining isolates. Results are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cecref240a" refid="tbl2">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para120" view="all">Acquired-AmpC-producing organisms are likely to remain undetected in many clinical laboratories as there is a lack of standardized phenotypic methods [<ce:cross-refs id="cecrefs60" refid="bib10 bib16">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">10,16</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-refs>]. In a multi-centre Spanish study, only 53.2% of 57 laboratories were able to detect <ce:italic>E. coli</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>K. pneumoniae</ce:italic> producing acquired AmpC [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref510" refid="bib17">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">17</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>].</ce:para>
               <ce:para id="para130" view="all">There are very few reports from Europe regarding the epidemiology of acquired-AmpCs over a period of several years [<ce:cross-ref id="cecref520" refid="bib4">
                     <ce:sup loc="post">4</ce:sup>
                  </ce:cross-ref>], and to date, this is the first from Spain. Our findings support the view that the prevalence and diversity of acquired <ce:italic>ampC</ce:italic> genes is increasing. Knowledge about the prevalence and diffusion of this emergent resistance may be helpful to establish preventive measures that will curb their spread.</ce:para>
