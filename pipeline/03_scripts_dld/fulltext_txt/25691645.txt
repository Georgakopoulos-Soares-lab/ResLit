Abstract 

We describe the genetic background of bla TEM-4 and the complete sequence of pRYC11:: bla TEM-4 , a mosaic plasmid that is highly similar to pKpQIL-like variants, predominant among TEM-4 producers in a Spanish hospital (1990 to 2004), which belong to Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli high-risk clones responsible for the current spread of different antibiotic resistance genes. Predominant populations of plasmids and host adapted clonal lineages seem to have greatly contributed to the spread of resistance to extended-spectrum cephalosporins. 

TEXT 

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) of the TEM family have frequently been identified among human and animal isolates of Enterobacteriaceae since 1983. Very few bla TEM genes ( bla TEM-3 , bla TEM-10 , bla TEM-12 , bla TEM-21 , bla TEM-24 , bla TEM-26 , and bla TEM-52 ) have successfully been spread ( 1 – 5 ), but the genetic context has only been characterized for three of them (e.g., bla TEM-3, bla TEM-24, and bla TEM-52 ) ( 6 – 8 ). These bla TEM genes are part of the Tn 3 elements, namely, Tn 3 ( bla TEM-1a ), Tn 2 ( bla TEM-1b ), and Tn 1 /Tn 801 ( bla TEM-2 ), which are located on highly transmissible plasmids ( 8 , 9 ). Tn 1 is the only one identified in species other than Enterobacteriaceae , which suggests different gene exchange networks for each Tn 3 variant ( 10 ). 

TEM-4 producers were reported first in France in 1986 and later in Spain, Poland, and Tunisia ( 5 , 11 – 18 ). Both strains and plasmids of Klebsiella pneumoniae carrying bla TEM-4 have caused nosocomial and community outbreaks, some of them being persistently recovered in hospitals from different countries ( 12 , 17 , 19 ). Follow-up studies of ESBL producers at the institutional, national, and international levels recorded a sudden decrease in the rates of TEM producers, with the last detection documented between 2007 and 2008 in Spain, Poland, and France ( 17 , 18 , 20 , 21 ). The apparent extinction of TEM-4 producers encouraged us to explore the genetic context of bla TEM-4 in isolates recovered at Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal (Madrid, Spain) for 14 years in order to further understand shifts associated with the epidemiology and transmission of genes coding for class A beta-lactamases ( 3 , 22 ). 

Forty-three TEM-4–producing Enterobacteriaceae isolates (31 K. pneumoniae , 11 Escherichia coli , and 1 Citrobacter freundii ) were analyzed (1990 to 2004). Clonal relatedness of the isolates was established by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) and multilocus sequence typing (MLST; http://mlst.warwick.ac.uk/mlst/ ; http://www.pasteur.fr/recherche/genopole/PF8/mlst/Kpneumoniae.html ). As a result, the 31 K. pneumoniae isolates were grouped into 7 PFGE types and 5 sequence types (STs), ST37, ST13, ST14, ST533, and ST29, and the 11 E. coli isolates were grouped into 5 PFGE types and 3 STs (ST131, ST127, and ST117) ( Table 1 ). Hybridization of I-CeuI– or S1-digested genomic DNA with a bla TEM-4 probe allowed identification of this gene on transferable plasmids of various sizes (50 to 120 kb, conjugation frequency ranging from 10 −6 to 10 −1 ). Comparison of HpaI-digested plasmid DNA restriction patterns and sequences of replicases encoding genes, obtained by PCR and further sequencing ( 23 , 24 ), resulted in the detection of five plasmids denominated pRYC10 (80 kb), pRYC11 (94 kb), pRYC12 (50 kb), pRYC13 (70 kb), and pRYC14 (120 kb), some of which were in different strains ( Table 1 ). The plasmids pRYC10, -12, -13, and -14 were nontypeable by the screening method initially used ( 23 ), but amplification and sequencing of a larger fragment of the replication region (rep) ( 25 ) revealed a 99% identity between reps of pRYC13 and pRYC14 and IncI1 plasmids pCVM29188_101, pH519-88, pV404, pTF2, and pSal8934a (GenBank accession numbers CP001121 , KJ484630 , LM651376 , KJ563250 , and JF274993 , respectively). pRYC11, the predominant plasmid in the study (67% of the isolates), was further sequenced by using the 454 GS-FLX system (Sistemas Genómicos, Valencia, Spain) ( 26 ). Sequences were assembled primarily with Newbler (454 GS assembler), gene prediction was done with Glimmer3, and open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using BLASTX v2.2.20 (E-value cutoff of e-15, similarity of 70%). The contigs were annotated by RAST (Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology; http://www.nmpdr.org/FIG/wiki/view.cgi/FIG/RapidAnnotationServer ) ( 27 ) and the Artemis comparison tool ( http://www.sanger.ac.uk/resources/software/act/ ). pRYC11 (European Nucleotide Archive accession number LK391770 ) is a 94,893-bp plasmid with 100 ORFs, 67 of which encode proteins involved in replication, conjugative transfer, maintenance, antibiotic resistance, mobile elements, and restriction-modification systems ( Fig. 1 ; see also Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). A large part of pRYC11 (56% coverage, corresponding to the conjugation module and part of the replication region) showed the highest identity (96%) to the IncFII k2 plasmids designated pKpQIL variants, which are widespread among ST258 K. pneumoniae strains (GenBank accession numbers NC_014016 , KJ146689 , KJ146687 , and KJ146688 ). The transfer region shows the highest identity (up to 99%) with pKpQIL-like plasmids, although pRYC11 contains insertions between traU and traW (a hypothetical protein) and traA and traM (a copy of an IS Kpn20 ) ( Fig. 1 ). Sequences associated with DNA reparation (SOS, umuC / umuD ), stable maintenance ( parA / parB , stbA / stbB ), and restriction-modification systems (EcoRII methylase/EcoRII endonuclease, ardA / ardB ) also showed high homology (95% to 98% identity) to those of pKpQIL-like plasmids and pKPN3 (GenBank accession number CP000648 ). However, pRYC11 contains two replicons scarcely represented in databases: a rep FII k9 , reported only on not fully sequenced CTX-M-15–encoding plasmids in Malaysia (2010 to 2011) ( www.pubmlst.org/plasmid/ ) and a Rep-3-like family initiator replication protein similar to that of different multidrug resistance plasmids of K. pneumoniae isolates distributed worldwide (GenBank accession numbers CP009116 , HF545434 , FJ876826 , and NC_019389 ).

The arrows contained in the black outer ring represent the coding sequences (CDSs) in pRYC11. The three inner rings symbolize the sequences shared with plasmids pKPN3 (purple; accession number NC_009649 ), pKDO1 (green; accession number NC_019389 ), and pKpQIL (blue; accession number NC_014016 ). The small diagram on the upper right is an amplified scheme of the variable region of pRYC11, where yellow represents the antimicrobial resistance genes and gray represents genes encoding transposition functions. 

The 10,553-bp variable region of pRYC11 was flanked by an IS 26 and a truncated IS EclI -like and comprises (i) a ΔTn 1 ( bla TEM-4 ); (ii) a fragment of 4,252 bp also found in the environmental plasmid pHHV35 (GenBank accession number NC_019218 ) that includes the aacC2 gene (encoding gentamicin resistance), a hypothetical protein, a truncated sigma factor, and an IS Kpn12 copy; and (iii) ΔTn 5393 (Δ strA-strB , streptomycin resistance). The ΔTn 1 ( bla TEM-4 ) sequence was similar to that of other unrelated plasmids from our institution, Poland, or the United Kingdom (GenBank accession numbers HQ659758 and AM261760 ). Similar nucleotide polymorphisms of promoters and transposon sequences of ΔTn 1 ( bla TEM-4 ) and Tn 1 ( bla TEM-2 ) suggest a common origin, as described for other bla TEM ESBLs ( 5 , 8 , 28 ). Note that Tn 1 ( bla TEM-4 ) was found on K. pneumoniae (ST13 and ST37) and E. coli (ST131, ST117, and ST127) high-risk clones, all emerging in the 21st century as major drivers of ESBL, acquired AmpC, and carbapenemase spread ( 29 – 34 ). These results suggest the existence of highly adapted populations of K. pneumoniae and E. coli able to acquire antimicrobial resistance genes since at least the early 1990s. The sudden decrease of TEM-4 producers in all countries where it had been reported for years ( 17 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 35 , 36 ) parallels the shift in the epidemiology of beta-lactamases that involves the emergence of CTX-M, KPC (class A), and NDM (class B) and the drop in TEM-ESBL producers ( 22 ). Comparative genomics studies recently demonstrated the existence of intraclonal variants with enhanced epidemicity that can successfully spread at the global level as H 30 ST131 E. coli or ST258/ST11 K. pneumoniae within clonal group (CG) 258 ( 37 – 39 ). The lack of sequences from all STs detected in this study, with the exception of the E. coli ST131 isolate that was identified as an uncommon variant ( 20 , 37 ; our unpublished results) precludes any conclusion in this regard. 

Knowledge about K. pneumoniae plasmids has remained extremely scarce until recently, when the worldwide increase in carbapenemase-producing strains has sped up the number of sequencing projects of plasmids carrying bla KPC and bla NDM genes ( ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/Plasmids/ ; ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/Bacteria/ ). pRYC11 was one of the few fully characterized plasmids recovered in the 1990s. Its similarity to contemporary IncFII k (pKpQIL-like) variants suggests the existence of plasmid populations prone to acquire antibiotic resistance genes, similar to those occurring with the IncFII plasmids of E. coli ( 40 – 44 ). Both IncFII plasmids from E. coli and InFII k plasmids from Klebsiella can be transferred between clones of the same and different species and therefore have the potential to facilitate the spread of any antibiotic resistance gene. 

Our study documents that in former decades, the bla TEM-4 gene was located on the same plasmid and high-risk lineages of K. pneumoniae and E. coli that are currently involved in the spread of diverse antibiotic resistance genes. This observation supports the concept of the high-risk plasmids and clones and their relevance in the spread of antibiotic resistance ( 45 ), and it highlights the need to understand the success of particular resistance genes in bacterial populations exposed to dynamic landscapes that influence their fixation, extinction, and reemergence ( 46 ). Further investigations to distinguish between clonal sublineages and plasmid variants with enhanced epidemicity will be relevant in explaining differences in the expansion and eventual extinction of certain antibiotic resistance genes.
