                  Using poly-(ethylene glycol)s of different molecular weights, we probe the channels formed in planar lipid bilayers by epsilon toxin secreted by the anaerobic bacterium Clostridium perfringens. We find that the pore is highly asymmetric. The cutoff size of polymers entering the pore through its opening from the cis side, the side of toxin addition, is ∼500 Da, whereas the cutoff size for the polymers entering from the trans side is ∼2300 Da. Comparing these characteristic molecular weights with those reported earlier for OmpF porin and the α-Hemolysin channel, we estimate the radii of cis and trans openings as 0.4 nm and 1.0 nm, respectively. The simplest geometry corresponding to these findings is that of a truncated cone. The asymmetry of the pore is also confirmed by measurements of the reversal potential at oppositely directed salt gradients. The moderate anionic selectivity of the channel is salted-out more efficiently when the salt concentration is higher at the trans side of the pore.

            <ce:abstract class="author"><ce:abstract-sec>
                  <ce:simple-para id="abspara0010" view="all">Using poly-(ethylene glycol)s of different molecular weights, we probe the channels formed in planar lipid bilayers by epsilon toxin secreted by the anaerobic bacterium <ce:italic>Clostridium perfringens.</ce:italic> We find that the pore is highly asymmetric. The cutoff size of polymers entering the pore through its opening from the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> side, the side of toxin addition, is ∼500 Da, whereas the cutoff size for the polymers entering from the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side is ∼2300 Da. Comparing these characteristic molecular weights with those reported earlier for OmpF porin and the <ce:italic>α</ce:italic>-Hemolysin channel, we estimate the radii of <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> openings as 0.4 nm and 1.0 nm, respectively. The simplest geometry corresponding to these findings is that of a truncated cone. The asymmetry of the pore is also confirmed by measurements of the reversal potential at oppositely directed salt gradients. The moderate anionic selectivity of the channel is salted-out more efficiently when the salt concentration is higher at the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side of the pore.</ce:simple-para>

               <ce:section id="sec1" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">Epsilon toxin (ETX) (for review, see (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib1 bib2 bib3">1–3</ce:cross-refs>)) is the major virulence factor secreted by Gram-positive, spore-forming anaerobic bacteria <ce:italic>Clostridium perfringens</ce:italic> types B and D (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib4">4</ce:cross-ref>). ETX is responsible for a rapidly fatal enterotoxaemia in herbivores when their gastrointestinal tracts are colonized by this bacterium leading to in situ toxin production (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib1 bib5 bib6">1,5,6</ce:cross-refs>). ETX is secreted in a poorly active form called prototoxin (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib7">7</ce:cross-ref>) and is activated into a highly potent toxin by proteolytic removal of 11 or 13 N-terminal and 29 C-terminal amino acid residues (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>). The <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-sheet-rich, ∼31 kDa ETX shows 20–27% sequence homology with the Mtx2 and Mtx3 toxins of <ce:italic>Bacillus sphaericus</ce:italic> and with c53 protein of <ce:italic>Bacillus thuringiensis</ce:italic> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib3">3</ce:cross-ref>). The ETX structure (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>) has a similarity to the large lobe of aerolysin, a 100-fold less potent (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib8">8</ce:cross-ref>) pore-forming protein produced by the Gram-negative pathogen <ce:italic>Aeromonas hydrophila</ce:italic>. ETX consists of three structural domains: i), N-terminal domain I, which may be involved in receptor binding; ii), domain II, which is thought to contain a transmembrane stem involved in pore formation and probably also participates in oligomer formation; iii), C-terminal domain III, which likely helps to mediate ETX insertion into membranes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">ETX activation triggers its oligomerization in the synaptosomal membrane within the detergent-insoluble microdomains (lipid rafts) of MDCK cells (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib10 bib11">10,11</ce:cross-refs>). ETX was reported to form aerolysinlike (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib9">9</ce:cross-ref>) <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-barrel heptameric (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib10 bib11 bib12">10–12</ce:cross-refs>) transmembrane pores that increase cell permeability to small molecules and ions (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib1 bib13 bib14 bib15">1,13–15</ce:cross-refs>). In vitro, only a few cell lines such as MDCK, mouse kidney cells, and human renal leiomyoblastoma G-402 cells were found to retain susceptibility to ETX (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib12 bib14">12,14</ce:cross-refs>) due to the presence of specific ETX-binding receptors. No receptors are needed for ETX incorporation into artificial lipid bilayers (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib16 bib17">16,17</ce:cross-refs>) or liposomes (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>). In bilayer lipid membranes, ETX was reported to form wide, slightly anion-selective general diffusion pores with a single-channel conductance in the range of 440–640 pS in 1 M KCl (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib16 bib17">16,17</ce:cross-refs>). Based on the structural and functional similarities with oligomeric aerolysin of <ce:italic>Aeromonas hydrophila</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>α</ce:italic>-Hemolysin of <ce:italic>Staphylococcus aureus</ce:italic>, ETX was supposed to be permeable to solutes up to a molecular mass of at least 1 kDa (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>). Petit et al. (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>) estimated the diameter of the transmembrane ETX channel to be ∼2 nm based on experiments on competitive entry of propidium iodide and osmotic uncharged compounds (i.e., glucose, sucrose, poly(ethylene) glycol (PEG) 1000, PEG 3350, and PEG 6000) with different hydrodynamic radii into MDCK cells. However, the authors pointed out that the data were not highly reliable because of PEG 1000 cytotoxicity (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">Activated ETX is one of the most potent bacterial toxins after botulinum and tetanus neurotoxins (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib2 bib19">2,19</ce:cross-refs>); an estimated lethal human dose is 7 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>g via the intravenous route (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib6">6</ce:cross-ref>). Due to ETX's high potency and lethality, it has been classified as a CDC category B agent. Driven by the idea of designing a small-molecule blocker of the ETX pore similarly to the cyclodextrin-based potent inhibitors discovered earlier for the anthrax PA<ce:inf loc="post">63</ce:inf> (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>) and <ce:italic>α</ce:italic>-Hemolysin (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib21">21</ce:cross-ref>) pores, we start with the study of the pore's physical properties.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">First, we use the polymer partitioning approach under conditions of PEG asymmetrical addition to access the pore's functional shape and size (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib22 bib23 bib24 bib25 bib26">22–26</ce:cross-refs>). This approach explores the ability of polymers to reduce channel conductance in a molecular-weight-dependent way (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib27 bib28 bib29 bib30 bib31 bib32 bib33 bib34 bib35 bib36 bib37 bib38">27–38</ce:cross-refs>), while reducing solution conductivity based only on their monomer concentration (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib39">39</ce:cross-ref>). We find that PEGs' partitioning is highly asymmetric. The <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> opening of the ETX pore allows the penetration of much larger polymer molecules than its <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> opening. The partitioning data are suggestive of an asymmetrical, e.g., conical shape of the pore with the tentative radii of the openings of 0.4 nm and 1.0 nm on the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> sides, respectively.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">Second, we explore the ionic selectivity of the pore by measuring reversal potentials in the oppositely directed gradients of potassium chloride aqueous solutions. As it was shown previously (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>), such measurements allow one to judge upon the charge distribution along the channel axis. Interestingly, we find that the asymmetry of the reversal potential in the salt gradient is opposite to what is reported for the conical nanopores with a uniformly spread surface charge (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib41 bib42">41,42</ce:cross-refs>). In the case of the ETX pore, the selectivity is salted-out more easily from the wide <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> opening of the channel. This suggests that the residues carrying the positive charge responsible for the anionic selectivity of the ETX pore (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib16 bib17">16,17</ce:cross-refs>) are not localized at its <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> opening but are shifted toward the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side.</ce:para>

               <ce:section role="materials-methods" id="sec2" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Material and Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.1" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Reagents</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">ETX was obtained from Dr. Bruce A. McClane, University of Pittsburgh and activated by incubation with trypsin as described (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib18">18</ce:cross-ref>) with some modifications. An aliquot of the <ce:italic>ɛ</ce:italic>-prototoxin was treated with trypsin (8 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>g trypsin/1 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>g toxin) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Next, an equal volume of a trypsin inhibitor bead suspension was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for additional 30 min. The mixture then was centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 RPM and the pellet/beads were discarded. The activated ETX was checked by protein gel electrophoresis, then aliquoted and stored at −80°C for future experiments.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">The following chemical reagents were used: KCl, KOH, and HCl; MES; “purum” hexadecane (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland); diphytanoyl phosphatidylcholine (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL); pentane (Burdick and Jackson, Muskegon, MI), and agarose (Bethesda Research Laboratory, Gaithersburg, MD). The poly(ethylene glycol)s (PEGs) of different molecular weights: diethylene glycol 106.12, PEG 200, PEG 300, PEG 400, PEG 600, PEG 1000, PEG 1500, PEG 2000, PEG 3400, PEG 4600, PEG 8000, PEG 20,000, and PEG 35,000 were purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO) and used in a 15% w/w concentration. The polymers were added to a 1 M KCl water solution. Doubly distilled and deionized water was used to prepare the solutions. All solutions were purified by filtration through a 0.45-<ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>m filter.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="sec2.2" view="all">
                     <ce:section-title>Channel reconstitution</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">To form solvent-free planar lipid bilayers with the lipid monolayer opposition technique (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib43">43</ce:cross-ref>), we used a 5 mg/mL stock solution of diphytanoyl phosphatidylcholine in pentane. Bilayers were formed on a 60-<ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>m-diameter aperture in a 15-<ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>m-thick Teflon film that separated two compartments as described elsewhere (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib44">44</ce:cross-ref>). The 1 M aqueous solutions of KCl were buffered by 5 mM MES at pH 6. All measurements were performed on single ETX channels at room temperature (23 ± 0.5)°C. Channels were formed by adding 0.2–0.5 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>L of ETX 0.15 mg/mL stock solution to the 1.5 mL aqueous phase in the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> half of the chamber while stirring. Under this protocol, ETX insertion was always directional, as judged by channel conductance asymmetry in the applied transmembrane voltage. For asymmetrical experiments, PEGs of varied molecular weights were added from one side of the membrane, while the impermeant PEG 8000 was on the other side. The electrical potential difference across the lipid bilayer was applied with a pair of Ag-AgCl electrodes in 2 M KCl, 1.5% agarose bridges. The applied potential is defined as positive if it is higher at the side of protein addition.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">Conductance and selectivity measurements were performed using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Foster City, CA) in the voltage-clamp mode. Signals were filtered by a low-pass 8-pole Butterworth filter (Model 9002; Frequency Devices, Haverhill, MA) at 15 kHz, and directly saved into the computer memory with a sampling frequency of 50 kHz. The reversal potentials were obtained as described (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib40 bib45">40,45</ce:cross-refs>).</ce:para>

               <ce:section id="sec3" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title>Results and Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">A typical ionic current record illustrating spontaneous events of channel formation by ETX is given in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig1"/> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>. At 100 mV of applied transmembrane voltage, the formation of the first channel is seen as a current jump of ∼48 pA, followed by the second channel of approximately equal conductance. The conductance value, 480 pS, is in good agreement with earlier reported ETX conductance (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib16">16</ce:cross-ref>). The variation of conductance from channel to channel was typical for that of some of <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-barrel channels (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib46">46</ce:cross-ref>). At very high voltages (exceeding 150 mV), we also observed voltage-dependent closures (data not shown) reported for other <ce:italic>β</ce:italic>-barrel channels (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib47 bib48">47,48</ce:cross-refs>) such as anthrax's PA<ce:inf loc="post">63</ce:inf> channel (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib49">49</ce:cross-ref>), OmpF (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib50">50</ce:cross-ref>), <ce:italic>α</ce:italic>-Hemolysin (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib51">51</ce:cross-ref>), and VDAC (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib52">52</ce:cross-ref>). The current through the open channels is noisy, displaying switching between well-resolved conductance substates. The characteristic time of this switching is in the range of several seconds as is illustrated by the inset in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic> and the power spectral density of current fluctuations in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>. The slopes of the parts of the spectra below 3 Hz are close to −2, suggesting that they represent the tails of low-frequency Lorentzians. We never observed this kind of noise in the absence of the ETX channel's main conductance while the occurrence of these substates significantly varied from channel to channel.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">Addition of PEG to the membrane-bathing aqueous solution of KCl changed channel conductance in a polymer-weight-dependent manner. The records of ionic current taken at 0.1-ms resolution are shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig2"/> for PEGs of different molecular weights in comparison with the record in polymer-free solution. The upper set (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>) shows the effect of symmetrical polymer addition when PEGs are added to both sides of the membrane in equal concentration. It is seen that small polymers, such as PEG 300, decrease the average conductance of the channel without any significant fluctuations at this time resolution. Larger polymers, PEG 1500 and PEG 2000, decrease the average conductance and produce vigorous current fluctuations. Still larger PEG 8000 does not produce significant fluctuations and slightly increases the average current. The current track in the absence of PEG (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>left</ce:italic>) lacks the characteristic switching between conductance substates seen in the inset of <ce:cross-ref refid="fig1">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref> for the reason that here we present very short, 20-ms fragments of the recordings.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">
                     <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic> shows the consequences of asymmetrical polymer addition when PEGs of different molecular weights are added to the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> side of the membrane, while the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side contains PEG 8000 in the same 15% concentration to (partially) offset the osmotic gradient across the membrane. It is seen that the PEG effect in this case is quite different from the effect of symmetrical addition shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>. The conductance of the channel is significantly less sensitive to polymer addition compared with the symmetrical case. Finally, <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>C</ce:italic> demonstrates that the asymmetrical polymer addition, wherein PEGs of varying molecular weights are added from the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side and PEG 8000 is at the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> side, gives results similar to those obtained for the symmetrical conditions of <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>. The increase in channel conductance in the presence of excluded polymers (e.g., <ce:italic>rightmost trace</ce:italic> in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>) above that of the control in the absence of polymer (<ce:italic>leftmost trace</ce:italic>) is partially related to the PEG-induced increase in salt activity (see discussion in (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>)). A similar effect was reported for other channels (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib23 bib31 bib37">23,31,37</ce:cross-refs>). However, for reasons that are not clear at the time of this writing, the increase in the ETX channel conductance is ∼10% higher than that expected based on salt activity increase (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">The effect of polymer is voltage-dependent (see also (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib27 bib37 bib53 bib54">27,37,53,54</ce:cross-refs>)). <ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig3"/> illustrates this point by showing the average conductance of the channel at symmetric application of polymers as a function of transmembrane voltage in comparison with the channel conductance in polymer-free solution (<ce:italic>open circles</ce:italic>). It is seen that while the relative effect of small and large PEGs, i.e., polymer-induced decrease or increase in conductance, is not significantly changed by the applied voltage, the action of PEG of intermediate sizes can reverse in sign. Indeed, both PEG 1500 and PEG 2000 decrease the averaged channel conductance at −100 mV but increase it at +100 mV. As this happens under symmetrical conditions in which both the concentration and molecular weight of applied polymers are the same on both sides of the membrane, the observed asymmetry must come from the asymmetric structure of the ETX channel either via field-sign-dependent interactions of polymers with the channel or voltage dependence of the channel structure itself.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">To escape these additional difficulties in the interpretation of polymer partitioning experiments, we have chosen to apply quantitative analysis only to equilibrium data. We interpolated data points similar to those illustrated in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig3">Fig. 3</ce:cross-ref> to zero applied voltage and plotted the results as functions of polymer molecular weight. <ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig4"/> shows three sets of measurements. Open circles give the average channel conductance for symmetrical polymer addition (see <ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic>), triangles stand for the results obtained in the asymmetrical set of experiments with PEGs of varied molecular weight at the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side of the membrane (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>C</ce:italic>), and open boxes for the opposite asymmetrical addition (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig2">Fig. 2</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>). It is seen that all three sets are different, with the symmetrical PEG application producing the most profound change in the average channel conductance.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">While the qualitative description of polymer partitioning leading to the conductance change shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref> is relatively simple, its quantitative description is still elusive. Qualitatively speaking, in the case of symmetrical (<ce:italic>open circles</ce:italic>) and <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> (<ce:italic>solid triangles</ce:italic>) PEG application, a characteristic molecular weight of ∼2000 Da separates the regime of penetration of smaller PEGs from the regime of exclusion of larger PEGs. Small polymers enter the channel and reduce its ionic conductance. Large polymers stay away from the channel pore and increase its conductance above that in PEG-free solution because the presence of PEG increases KCl activity (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib29">29</ce:cross-ref>). In the case of <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> addition (<ce:italic>open boxes</ce:italic>), the regime of exclusion shifts toward smaller polymers, suggesting that the size of the channel opening on this side is significantly smaller than on <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side.</ce:para>

The characteristic corner frequency allows one to obtain the relaxation time of polymer exchange according to <ce:italic>τ</ce:italic> = 1/2<ce:italic>πf<ce:inf loc="post">c</ce:inf>
                     </ce:italic>. This time for PEG 2000 as a function of transmembrane voltage is shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>B</ce:italic>. It changes from ∼50 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>s at −200 mV to ∼15 <ce:italic>μ</ce:italic>s at +200 mV. This relaxation time is orders-of-magnitude larger than the time expected for freely diffusing particles in the channel with a length of several nanometers (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib56">56</ce:cross-ref>) suggesting strong attractive interactions between the polymer and the channel pore.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0110" view="all">The power spectral density of current noise shown in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>A</ce:italic> is supposed to be voltage-dependent even if the fluctuations in channel conductance that cause this noise are voltage-independent. In the system under study, the conductance fluctuations depend on the applied voltage: the spectral density is sign-sensitive and its corner frequency changes with voltage. Thus, the dynamics of polymers in the channel that produce the conductance reduction are voltage-dependent. This is made clear by <ce:cross-ref refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>C</ce:italic> showing the normalized low-frequency spectral density, 〈<ce:italic>S</ce:italic>(<ce:italic>f</ce:italic>)〉/〈<ce:italic>i</ce:italic>〉<ce:sup loc="post">2</ce:sup>. For a linear system with voltage-independent conductance fluctuations, this quantity should be constant in voltage. The data for PEG 1500, PEG 2000, and PEG 8000 plotted in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig5">Fig. 5</ce:cross-ref> 
                     <ce:italic>C</ce:italic> display an order-of-magnitude change of the normalized spectral densities as functions of voltage.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0115" view="all">The dependence of PEG-induced fluctuations in the form of the spectral density 〈<ce:italic>S</ce:italic>(<ce:italic>f</ce:italic>)〉 on the PEG molecular weight is given in <ce:cross-ref refid="fig6">Fig. 6</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig6"/> for all polymer weights used in the study. It shows a pronounced maximum for PEG 2000 (see also (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib57">57</ce:cross-ref>)). Qualitative interpretation of this result is rather straightforward. Larger polymers produce larger effects on channel conductance with each visit to the channel pore and their exchange between the channel and the bulk is slower than for smaller polymers. Therefore, the low-frequency conductance noise increases with polymer size. However, for polymers that are too large to partition into the pore, the probability of entering the pore is reduced, thus reducing not only their effect on the average channel conductance but also on its fluctuations.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0120" view="all">Finally, we probed channel ionic selectivity using the opposite salt concentration gradients. As was shown previously (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib40">40</ce:cross-ref>), these measurements provide information about charge distribution in the channel. <ce:cross-ref refid="fig7">Fig. 7</ce:cross-ref>
                     <ce:float-anchor refid="fig7"/> shows the reversal potential of the ETX channel obtained in two sets of experiments. In one of them, represented by solid circles, the concentration of the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> side membrane-bathing solution was kept constant and equal to 0.1 M KCl, while the concentration on the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side was varied from 0.01 M KCl to 3 M KCl. In the other, represented by open boxes, the protocol was inversed: the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side was the side with the constant salt concentration. It is seen that the reversal potential is sensitive to the direction of the salt gradient. The higher concentration on the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side salts-out channel selectivity more efficiently.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0125" view="all">This observation suggests structural asymmetry and can be explained by the asymmetry of the ETX channel found in polymer-partitioning experiments (<ce:cross-ref refid="fig4">Fig. 4</ce:cross-ref>). Indeed, if the charged residues that are responsible for the channel's ionic selectivity are located in the depth of the pore, close to the channel center for example, and the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> opening of the pore is wider, the residues are more accessible for salt screening from the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side. The salt concentration near the charged residues is expected to be higher if it is higher at the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> side than at the inversed concentration arrangement. Interestingly, the effect of the gradient inversion found here with the ETX channel is opposite of that reported for synthetic conical pores, where the more efficient salting-out happens when the higher salt concentration is applied at the narrow opening of the pore (<ce:cross-refs refid="bib41 bib42">41,42</ce:cross-refs>). This happens because in synthetic pores the uniformly spread surface charge creates an effective selectivity filter at the narrowest part of the pore. Such a selectivity filter is screened more effectively when directly exposed to the higher salt concentration.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0130" view="all">Although we suggest a conical shape for the ETX pore, our findings are also compatible with more complex geometries wherein, for example, one of the openings of a cylindrical channel is partially blocked by protruding peptide loops. More fanciful geometries of the pore (e.g., irregular funnel-, bottle-, or mushroom-like) are also realistic. The proposed geometry of a circular truncated cone is just the simplest asymmetric shape that can be described by three parameters only: the length of the pore and the two radii of its openings.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0135" view="all">To conclude, our PEG partitioning and ion selectivity studies reveal a pronounced asymmetry of the <ce:italic>Clostridium perfringens</ce:italic> ETX channel with the estimated <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> opening of 0.4-nm radius being much narrower than the <ce:italic>trans</ce:italic> opening of 1-nm radius, wherein the positively charged residues, producing a moderate anionic selectivity, are hidden inside the pore. These structural features are important in our search for the prospective small-molecule compounds that would block the ETX pore similarly to anthrax's PA<ce:inf loc="post">63</ce:inf> pore inhibition by positively charged cyclodextrins derivatives (<ce:cross-ref refid="bib20">20</ce:cross-ref>). Using an analogy with other bacterial toxins, we assume that ETX incorporation into the lipid bilayers mimics its incorporation occurring in vivo. According to our observations, ETX added to the <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic> side of the membrane inserts strictly unidirectionally, so that the narrow <ce:italic>cis</ce:italic>-opening of the channel would correspond to the extracellular side of the membrane. However, it is worth noting here that the role of the specific membrane receptors for ETX binding in most of the situations in vivo is not clear in this respect. Additional studies may need to be designed to reveal the pore orientation in the membranes of target cells.</ce:para>
