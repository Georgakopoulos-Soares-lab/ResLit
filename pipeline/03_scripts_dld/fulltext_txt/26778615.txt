                  Background
                  
                     Achromobacter spp. are emerging pathogens in Cystic Fibrosis (CF) patients. Recent studies proposed Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) scheme and a species-level identification method by nrdA sequencing for this genus. Epidemiological data are needed to assess the species and/or the sequence types (STs) involved and their potential role in CF patients lung function degradation. The aims of this study were i) to describe the distribution of the different species of Achromobacter in our CF centre ii) to detect potential STs more involved in chronic colonisations iii) to detect a potential local or worldwide predominance of some STs among clinical and environmental isolates.
               
                  Methods
                  All the isolates (477) collected in our CF centre from 2007 to 2014 among the 177 patients attending the centre were identified using nrdA sequencing. MLST analysis was performed for 37 clinical and 14 environmental isolates.
               
                  Results
                  A total of 47 out of 177 patients presented positive culture(s) with Achromobacter spp., representing 12.7% of the patients of the centre each year. Eleven species were detected, A. xylosoxidans being the most prevalent species (27 patients). Only A. xylosoxidans (&gt;80%) and A. insuavis were involved in chronic colonisation (6.7%). MLST analysis revealed a wide diversity among the isolates (36 STs for 51 isolates). Nevertheless, one third of the isolates belonged to STs previously detected in clinical isolates from other countries.
               
                  Conclusions
                  This study is a first approach in understanding the global epidemiology of Achromobacter species in CF. These results confirm the high prevalence of the species A. xylosoxidans among CF patients, reveal the worldwide distribution of some STs and point out the potential role of environmental sources of contamination. More studies are needed to search for relationships between species and/or ST and pathogenicity.

                  <ce:section-title id="st0010">Background</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="sp0085" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. are emerging pathogens in Cystic Fibrosis (CF) patients. Recent studies proposed Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) scheme and a species-level identification method by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing for this genus. Epidemiological data are needed to assess the species and/or the sequence types (STs) involved and their potential role in CF patients lung function degradation. The aims of this study were i) to describe the distribution of the different species of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> in our CF centre ii) to detect potential STs more involved in chronic colonisations iii) to detect a potential local or worldwide predominance of some STs among clinical and environmental isolates.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="as0010" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="st0015">Methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="sp0090" view="all">All the isolates (477) collected in our CF centre from 2007 to 2014 among the 177 patients attending the centre were identified using <ce:italic>nrd</ce:italic>A sequencing. MLST analysis was performed for 37 clinical and 14 environmental isolates.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="as0015" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="st0020">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="sp0095" view="all">A total of 47 out of 177 patients presented positive culture(s) with <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp., representing 12.7% of the patients of the centre each year. Eleven species were detected, <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> being the most prevalent species (27 patients). Only <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> (&gt;<ce:hsp sp="0.10"/>80%) and <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> were involved in chronic colonisation (6.7%). MLST analysis revealed a wide diversity among the isolates (36 STs for 51 isolates). Nevertheless, one third of the isolates belonged to STs previously detected in clinical isolates from other countries.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec><ce:abstract-sec id="as0020" view="all">
                  <ce:section-title id="st0025">Conclusions</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:simple-para id="sp0100" view="all">This study is a first approach in understanding the global epidemiology of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> species in CF. These results confirm the high prevalence of the species <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> among CF patients, reveal the worldwide distribution of some STs and point out the potential role of environmental sources of contamination. More studies are needed to search for relationships between species and/or ST and pathogenicity.</ce:simple-para>
               </ce:abstract-sec></ce:abstract>

                  <ce:label>1</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="st0035">Introduction</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0005" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. are recognised as emerging pathogens in Cystic Fibrosis (CF) patients. The prevalence of the patients colonised increased during the last decade. Recently several studies using Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) based methods described the complexity within <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> genus and led to the characterisation of new species <ce:cross-refs id="cf0015" refid="bb0005 bb0010 bb0015">[1–3]</ce:cross-refs>. To date, a total of 15 species and 6 genogroups belong to the genus <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-refs id="cf0020" refid="bb0015 bb0020 bb0025">[3–5]</ce:cross-refs>. The MLST scheme developed by Spilker <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> is based on 7 housekeeping genes sequencing. In 2012, Spilker <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> showed that sequencing 765<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>bp of the single housekeeping gene <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> allows the identification of the isolates to the species-level <ce:cross-ref id="cf0025" refid="bb0030">[6]</ce:cross-ref>. Before the description of the new species, <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. were mostly referred as <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> in the studies because conventional methods (biochemical tests, <ce:italic>rrs</ce:italic> sequencing, Mass Spectrometry) are not powerful enough to differentiate the several species <ce:cross-refs id="cf0030" refid="bb0010 bb0015 bb0035 bb0040">[2,3,7,8]</ce:cross-refs>. Consequently, the epidemiology and clinical impact of these novel species in CF patients are not known yet and their innate antimicrobial resistance remains to be established. Some <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> genes variants seem to be constitutive in some species such as <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-114</ce:inf> in <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-refs id="cf0035" refid="bb0045 bb0050 bb0055 bb0060">[9–12]</ce:cross-refs>. According to the results of the few studies available <ce:cross-refs id="cf0040" refid="bb0010 bb0030 bb0065 bb0070 bb0075">[2,6,13–15]</ce:cross-refs>, <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> seems to be the most prevalent species among these patients but it has to be confirmed in other CF centres. Therefore, it seems necessary to correctly identify by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing all the isolates detected in CF patients sputum to better understand the role of the different species in chronic colonisation or in a potential lung function degradation.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0010" view="all">To go even farther in this approach, genotyping of the isolates by MLST might allow detecting an eventual worldwide clonal spread within the most prevalent species. Indeed, MLST analysis offers the major advantage of allowing easy interlaboratory comparisons because it provides numerical data <ce:cross-ref id="cf0045" refid="bb0080">[16]</ce:cross-ref>. For <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp., a database including mostly CF patients isolates is available online (<ce:inter-ref id="ir0005" xlink:href="http://pubmlst.org" xlink:type="simple">http://pubmlst.org</ce:inter-ref>/achromobacter/). To date, very few studies performed MLST analysis of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> isolates from limited number of CF patients <ce:cross-refs id="cf0050" refid="bb0065 bb0070 bb0075 bb0085">[13–15,17]</ce:cross-refs>. Moreover, there is no study reporting MLST analysis comparing clinical and environmental <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> isolates.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0015" view="all">In former works, we described the epidemiology of <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> colonisation in our CF centre in Dijon (Burgundy, France) and detected environmental isolates of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> in Burgundy <ce:cross-refs id="cf0055" refid="bb0045 bb0050">[9,10]</ce:cross-refs>. These isolates were identified by conventional methods and we showed by Pulsed-Field Gel Electrophoresis (PFGE) that some environmental and clinical isolates shared common genotypes.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0020" view="all">The aims of the present study were:<ce:list id="l0005">
                        <ce:list-item id="o0005">
                           <ce:label>i.</ce:label>
                           <ce:para id="p0025" view="all">To identify to the species-level all the isolates of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> of the CF patients affiliated with our centre from 2007 to 2014 by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing</ce:para>
                        </ce:list-item>
                        <ce:list-item id="o0010">
                           <ce:label>ii.</ce:label>
                           <ce:para id="p0030" view="all">To perform the genotypic analysis by MLST of all the clinical isolates belonging to the most prevalent species in our centre, especially those involved in chronic colonisation and to detect a potential worldwide dissemination of clonal isolates.</ce:para>
                        </ce:list-item>
                        <ce:list-item id="o0015">
                           <ce:label>iii.</ce:label>
                           <ce:para id="p0035" view="all">To perform the genotypic analysis by MLST of <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> environmental isolates to detect potential specific genotypes shared between environmental and clinical samples</ce:para>
                        </ce:list-item>
                     </ce:list>
                  </ce:para>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="s0010" view="all">
                  <ce:label>2</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="st0040">Materials and methods</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="s0015" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0045">Patients</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0040" view="all">Currently 122 patients (paediatrics 60% and adults 40%) receive care in the CF Centre of Dijon's Hospital: the CRCM (Centre de Ressources et de Compétences de la Mucoviscidose). In this study, we focused on the patients harbouring at least one positive sputum culture with <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. from January 1<ce:sup loc="post">st</ce:sup> 2007 to December 31<ce:sup loc="post">st</ce:sup> 2014. We used the term “colonisation” because we did not include any clinical data. Patients were considered as chronically colonised according to the criteria defined by Pereira <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic>: “when at least three positive cultures in 1<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>year were obtained, with a minimum 1-month interval between them, for at least 2<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>years” <ce:cross-ref id="cf0060" refid="bb0090">[18]</ce:cross-ref>. The colonisation was referred to as “sporadic” when less than 4 positive cultures were obtained during the period of the study.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0020" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0050">Isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0045" view="all">Since 1995 we have constituted a collection of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. isolates which includes all the isolates (1110) recovered from CF patients sputum and environmental isolates (80). For all these isolates, the genus level identification involved conventional methods API 20NE (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France) and either whole <ce:italic>rrs</ce:italic> gene sequencing <ce:cross-ref id="cf0065" refid="bb0095">[19]</ce:cross-ref> or Matrix-Assisted Laser-Desorption/Ionization Time-Of-Flight mass spectrometry (MALDI Biotyper, Bruker). All the isolates were submitted to genotypic analysis by PFGE and interpreted as already described <ce:cross-refs id="cf0070" refid="bb0050 bb0100">[10,20]</ce:cross-refs>.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0050" view="all">In the present study, we included all 477 isolates of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. detected in our CF centre between 2007 and 2014. Moreover, 14 environmental isolates previously identified as <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing (as described below) were included in the study for comparison with clinical isolates (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0075" refid="t0005">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="t0005"/>). These isolates were chosen because they originated from various local samples: Dijon's hospital departments (ENV-H), domestic (ENV-D) or natural environmental water (ENV-W) sites <ce:cross-ref id="cf0080" refid="bb0045">[9]</ce:cross-ref> and were genotypically distinct (as determined by PFGE).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0025" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0055">Species-level identification by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0055" view="all">It was considered that the isolates belonging to the same PFGE profile belonged to the same species. Therefore, species identification was performed for one isolate of each pulsotype detected from each patient by sequencing 765<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>bp of the <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> gene with primers nrdA-P1/nrdA-P2 <ce:cross-ref id="cf0085" refid="bb0015">[3]</ce:cross-ref>. PCR and sequencing were performed as previously described <ce:cross-ref id="cf0090" refid="bb0050">[10]</ce:cross-ref>. The sequences obtained were analysed in the <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> PubMLST database <ce:cross-ref id="cf0095" refid="bb0105">[21]</ce:cross-ref>. For 17 isolates novel alleles were detected and submitted to Theodore Spilker who categorised the isolates by using his database <ce:cross-ref id="cf0100" refid="bb0030">[6]</ce:cross-ref> as known species or as putative new species.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0030" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0060">Multilocus Sequence Typing analysis and construction of a phylogenetic tree</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0060" view="all">MLST analysis was performed on one isolate (for each patient) of each PFGE pattern identified for the species <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> and on the 14 <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> environmental strains. The isolates were submitted to MLST analysis as described <ce:cross-ref id="cf0105" refid="bb0015">[3]</ce:cross-ref>. After sequencing the 7 housekeeping genes (<ce:italic>nusA</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>rpoB</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>eno</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>gltB</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>lepA</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>nuoL</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic>), the <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> PubMLST database was used for analysis and for assignment of new Sequence Types (STs) numbers.</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0065" view="all">The concatenation of the 7 sequences was performed (total of 2249<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>bp) for each isolate and for the type strain of each of the <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> species available in the database. A dendrogram was generated from the concatenated sequences using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replications (MEGA 6.06).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0035" view="all">
                     <ce:label>2.5</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0065">Detection and sequencing of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> genes</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0070" view="all">For one clinical isolate of each PFGE pattern and for the 14 environmental <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> the presence of a <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> gene was searched by PCR. We used 3 primer pairs for the detection: OXA-114A/OXA-114B <ce:cross-ref id="cf0110" refid="bb0055">[11]</ce:cross-ref>, AXXAF/AXXAR <ce:cross-ref id="cf0115" refid="bb0050">[10]</ce:cross-ref> or EntF/AXXAR <ce:cross-ref id="cf0120" refid="bb0110">[22]</ce:cross-ref>. Positive PCR products were sequenced as already described <ce:cross-ref id="cf0125" refid="bb0050">[10]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>

                  <ce:label>3</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="st0070">Results</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:section id="s0045" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.1</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0075">Patients and isolates</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0075" view="all">From 2007 to 2014, 177 patients received care in our CF centre. Among them, 172 had bacterial cultures performed and <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> isolates were detected in the sputum of 47 patients (26.6%) for a total of 477 isolates. On average, each year 111 patients had sputum analysis performed and 12.7% of them had positive culture with <ce:italic>Achromobacter.</ce:italic> With our criteria an average of 6.9% of the patients were chronically colonised (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0130" refid="t0010">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="t0010"/>).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0080" view="all">PFGE revealed 56 distinct pulsotypes among these isolates. Five pulsotypes were shared between 12 patients. In particular pulsotype AXX42 was detected from the isolates of 4 patients (2 chronically colonised siblings patients and 2 sporadically colonised patients). The characteristics of the isolates are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cf0135" refid="t0005">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="s0050" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.2</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0080">Identification of the isolates by <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0085" view="all">A total of 63 clinical isolates were submitted to <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> sequencing for identification. The results are shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cf0140" refid="t0005">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>. The most prevalent species was <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> (28) followed by <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> (9), <ce:italic>A. aegrifaciens</ce:italic> (7), <ce:italic>A. spiritinus</ce:italic> (undergoing reclassification, Pr. Vandamme personnal communication) (7), <ce:italic>A. animicus</ce:italic> (5), <ce:italic>A. insolitus</ce:italic> (2), <ce:italic>A. mucicolens</ce:italic> (1) and <ce:italic>A. spanius</ce:italic> (1). The last 3 isolates (isolates n°44, 61 and 63) belonged to 3 putative novel species. Their <ce:italic>nrdA</ce:italic> alleles differed by 9, 19 and 36 nucleotide substitutions from alleles 100, 152 and 150 respectively.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0055" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.3</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0085">Epidemiology at the CF centre from 2007 to 2014</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0090" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> was the most prevalent species, detected from 27 out of the 47 patients between 2007 and 2014. The annual prevalence of patients colonised by <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> varied from 7.4% to 10.4% for the centre (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0145" refid="t0010">Table 2</ce:cross-ref>). <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> was the second most prevalent species with 9 patients colonised during the period (up to 3.7% each year). For 8 patients distinct species were identified in the same or in successive samples during the period of the study (e.g. 4 different species for patient CF-12).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0095" view="all">A total of 11 chronically colonised patients were identified. They were all but two colonised by <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> (&gt; 80%). The last two were siblings (CF-27 and CF-28) colonised by <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> pulsotype AXX1. All the isolates belonging to other species were only detected in sporadic colonisations (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0150" refid="t0005">Table 1</ce:cross-ref>).</ce:para>

                  <ce:section id="s0060" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.4</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0090">
                        <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> detection and sequencing</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0100" view="all">
                        <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-114-like</ce:inf> was detected in all the isolates identified as <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans.</ce:italic> We observed a wide diversity among OXA-114 with 14 variants including the new variants OXA-114w and OXA-114x, (GenBank accession numbers <ce:inter-ref id="ir1000" xlink:href="ncbi-n:KU323970" xlink:type="simple">KU323970</ce:inter-ref> and <ce:inter-ref id="ir2000" xlink:href="ncbi-n:KU323971" xlink:type="simple">KU323971</ce:inter-ref> respectively). Concerning <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> isolates we detected <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-243-like</ce:inf> with 3 variants, the very recently described <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-455</ce:inf> (isolates n°17 and 41) and a new <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> gene showing 99% identity with <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-455</ce:inf> (isolate n°43)<ce:inf loc="post">.</ce:inf> The new enzyme differed by 3 amino acid substitutions from OXA-455 (GenBank accession number <ce:inter-ref id="ir3000" xlink:href="ncbi-n:KU323972" xlink:type="simple">KU323972</ce:inter-ref>)<ce:italic>.</ce:italic> No <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                        <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> gene was detected with the primers used in this study in any of the isolates identified as <ce:italic>A. aegrifaciens</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. insolitus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. mucicolens</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. spiritinus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. spanius</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. animicus</ce:italic> or as novel species.</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
                  <ce:section id="s0065" view="all">
                     <ce:label>3.5</ce:label>
                     <ce:section-title id="st0095">Genotyping by MLST</ce:section-title>
                     <ce:para id="p0105" view="all">The 2 most prevalent species in our centre also involved in chronic colonisation were <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>A. insuavis.</ce:italic> For these species 37 clinical isolates were submitted to MLST analysis: <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> (28) and <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> (9). Analysis of the 51 isolates (37 clinical and 14 environmental) by MLST revealed a total of 36 STs. Among these 36 STs, 25 were novel variants identified in this study (ST 275 to 299). The other 11 STs were already detected among isolates originating from other countries (details shown in <ce:cross-ref id="cf0155" refid="t0015">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="t0015"/>). The 28 <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> clinical isolates belonged to 22 STs, 7 of them being previously detected (STs 2, 3, 27, 137, 153, 175 and 207). ST 207 was the most prevalent ST (4 patients). The 9 <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> isolates belonged to 7 distinct STs, with 3 of them already detected in other countries (ST 144, 218 and 271).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0110" view="all">The 14 <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> environmental isolates belonged to 13 STs. The dendrogram showed that the environmental isolates were not clustered in a different group from the clinical ones (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0160" refid="f0005">Fig. 1</ce:cross-ref>
                        <ce:float-anchor refid="f0005"/>). Furthermore, a total of 7 STs were identified also in clinical isolates from CF patients of our centre (STs 2, 27, 175, 283, 290 and 292) or other CF or non CF (NCF) patients around the world (STs 2, 27, 175 and 184) (<ce:cross-ref id="cf0165" refid="t0015">Table 3</ce:cross-ref>). ST 175 was the most prevalent ST in our study with 5 strains isolated between 2008 and 2012 (3 clinical and 2 environmental isolates).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0115" view="all">All the isolates classified into the same PFGE pulsotype belonged to the same ST (AXX42 and ST 207; AXX2 and ST 292; AXX58 and ST 175, AXX71 and ST 137, AXX61 and ST 271; AXX1 and ST 289, AXX74 and ST 283, AXX46 and ST 27).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0120" view="all">In several cases, isolates belonging to the same ST were classified into different pulsotypes by PFGE. For instance the 5 isolates n° 14, 15, 16, 74 and 75 belonged to ST 175 and were classified into 3 distinct PFGE pulsotypes. The same observation can be done for the 2 isolates belonging to ST 2 (n° 31 and 68 with distinct pulsotypes) and the 2 isolates belonging to ST 290 (n°6 and 76 with distinct pulsotypes).</ce:para>
                     <ce:para id="p0125" view="all">Nine STs were detected in our study from chronically colonised patients (STs 3, 137, 207, 275, 278, 289, 290, 292 and 294).</ce:para>
                  </ce:section>
               </ce:section>
               <ce:section id="s0070" view="all">
                  <ce:label>4</ce:label>
                  <ce:section-title id="st0100">Discussion</ce:section-title>
                  <ce:para id="p0130" view="all">We report here the first epidemiological data about the different species of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. isolated among 177 CF patients in a French CF centre during 8<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>years (2007 to 2014).</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0135" view="all">To date, since the development of a discriminating identification method of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> isolates to the species-level, only one other similar study was performed in a CF centre (Madrid, Spain) gathering 250 patients and for a period of 10<ce:hsp sp="0.25"/>years <ce:cross-ref id="cf0170" refid="bb0065">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. In this centre, only 5.6% (14/250) of the patients had positive cultures. The prevalence of patients colonised with <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. is much higher in our centre. Indeed, over the period of the study, more than one fourth of the patients (47/177) had at least one positive culture with <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic>, with an average of 12.7% each year. Further similar studies conducted in other CF centres are necessary for comparison with our data.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0140" view="all">The results of species-level identification showed an important diversity of the species involved in CF patients lung colonisation. A total of 11 species (<ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. aegrifaciens</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. spiritinus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. insolitus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. animicus</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. mucicolens</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. spanius</ce:italic>, and 3 putative new species) were detected. As already reported in the few other similar studies <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> was the most prevalent species <ce:cross-refs id="cf0175" refid="bb0010 bb0030 bb0065 bb0075">[2,6,13,15]</ce:cross-refs>. Surprisingly, the species <ce:italic>A. ruhlandii</ce:italic> was neither detected in our centre nor in the Spanish centre whereas it was reported to be the second species involved in CF patients colonisations in the USA (23.5% of the isolates from 341 CF patients attending 86 US centres between 1998 to 2012) <ce:cross-ref id="cf0180" refid="bb0030">[6]</ce:cross-ref>. <ce:italic>A. ruhlandii</ce:italic> has also been described in Denmark where one clone (Danish epidemic strain) spread among CF patients <ce:cross-refs id="cf0185" refid="bb0010 bb0115">[2,23]</ce:cross-refs> and in UK with 3% of the isolates collected from 96 patients <ce:cross-ref id="cf0190" refid="bb0070">[14]</ce:cross-ref>. Similarly, no <ce:italic>A. dolens</ce:italic> were detected in our study whereas it accounted for 17% of the isolates in Spilker's study <ce:cross-ref id="cf1100" refid="bb0030">[6]</ce:cross-ref> and 8% of the isolates in Coward's study <ce:cross-ref id="cf0195" refid="bb0070">[14]</ce:cross-ref>. These disparities might be due to variable distribution of the species among the countries within environmental sources of contamination. In a former study, we detected reservoirs of <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> in Burgundy <ce:cross-ref id="cf0200" refid="bb0045">[9]</ce:cross-ref>. Further studies are needed to search for other species.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0145" view="all">Interestingly, for 12 out of the 47 patients, several species (up to 4) were identified in the same or in successive samples during the period of analysis. These results underline the necessity to identify each isolate in CF patients colonisations.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0150" view="all">Detection of <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA</ce:inf> genes among <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. confirmed that OXA-114 is species specific as all and only the 36 <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> isolates harboured <ce:italic>bla</ce:italic>
                     <ce:inf loc="post">OXA-114</ce:inf>. Conversely, heterogeneity was observed within the 7 <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> isolates with the detection of 2 different oxacillinases: OXA-243 and OXA-455.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0155" view="all">The rate of chronically colonised patients was high compared with the Spanish centre (6.9% versus 3.6%) but similar to those reported in a French and a Danish centre (respectively 6% out of 200 patients and 8.6% out of 151 patients) <ce:cross-ref id="cf0205" refid="bb0075">[15]</ce:cross-ref>. To date, chronic colonisations have been reported for the species <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic>, <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-refs id="cf0210" refid="bb0065 bb0075">[13,15]</ce:cross-refs>, <ce:italic>A. genogroup 7</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cf0215" refid="bb0065">[13]</ce:cross-ref>, <ce:italic>A. dolens</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>A. genogroup 17</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cf0220" refid="bb0065">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. In our centre <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> was responsible for more than 80% of the chronic colonisations. Only 2 siblings patients were chronically colonised by a clonal strain <ce:italic>A. insuavis.</ce:italic> These results suggest that all other species of our study are less likely to induce chronic colonisations. One might hypothesize that the species involved in chronic colonisation are well adapted to persist in the CF patients respiratory tract and/or more virulent as already suggested <ce:cross-ref id="cf0225" refid="bb0075">[15]</ce:cross-ref>. For instance <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> which are innately resistant to aminoglycosides <ce:cross-refs id="cf0230" refid="bb0120 bb0125">[24,25]</ce:cross-refs> might be selected by exposure to these drugs frequently used in this population against <ce:italic>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</ce:italic>. Nevertheless, it has been reported that colonisation by <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> can occur in patients who were not previously colonised by <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cf0235" refid="bb0130">[26]</ce:cross-ref>.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0160" view="all">
                     <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> and <ce:italic>A. insuavis</ce:italic> being the two species involved in chronic colonisation in our centre, we performed MLST analysis of all unrelated isolates of these species recovered from each patient. For comparison, we also included 14 environmental <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> isolates. This is the first study to include a large number of isolates analysed by MLST (51) including CF and environmental isolates. The MLST analysis revealed a wide diversity among the isolates as already reported very recently by Coward <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> 
                     <ce:cross-ref id="cf0240" refid="bb0070">[14]</ce:cross-ref>. The 37 clinical and 14 environmental isolates of our study belonged respectively to 29 and 13 STs.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0165" view="all">PFGE proved to be more discriminatory than MLST in this study. For example the five isolates belonging to ST 175 were classified into 3 pulsotypes by PFGE. Interestingly, the isolates originated from various sources and were isolated at different times: river, hospital sink (2012) and 3 CF patients (2008, 2010). These discrepancies were already described for <ce:italic>P. aeruginosa</ce:italic> or <ce:italic>Burkholderia cepacia</ce:italic> complex <ce:cross-refs id="cf0245" refid="bb0135 bb0140">[27,28]</ce:cross-refs>. Nevertheless our results are discordant with those of Barrado <ce:italic>et al.</ce:italic> who noticed that “in all cases in which a common ST was found, the PFGE patterns were also indistinguishable” <ce:cross-ref id="cf0250" refid="bb0065">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. Therefore, MLST can not replace PFGE for local epidemiological studies and especially to detect cross-contaminations between patients. However, MLST is a powerful tool that allows global epidemiological studies thanks to international databases. It is noteworthy that more than 40% of the isolates (22/51) from our study belonged to 11 STs already detected among clinical isolates from other countries: Belgium, Brazil, Spain, Thailand, United Kingdom (UK) and USA. Interestingly ST 27 identified in one CF patient (CF-7) and in one hospital sink from our study has already been detected worldwide, particularly in 2 chronically colonised patients in Spain <ce:cross-ref id="cf0255" refid="bb0065">[13]</ce:cross-ref>. ST 175, the most prevalent in our study (3 clinical and 2 environmental isolates), was also detected from one CF and one NCF patients in UK.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0170" view="all">Moreover, our study revealed that some clones are detected both in clinical and environmental samples. Indeed, half of our environmental isolates belonged to STs already detected in clinical ones in our study or worldwide. Acquisition of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> by patients remains unexplained but our results confirm that environmental reservoirs (hospital, domestic or outdoor) have to be considered as sources of contamination as we already suspected <ce:cross-ref id="cf0260" refid="bb0045">[9]</ce:cross-ref>. The main limitation of our study is the lack of obvious epidemiological links between patients and environment.</ce:para>
                  <ce:para id="p0175" view="all">We did not detect predominant STs among chronically colonised patients isolates (9 STs from 11 patients isolates). However, ST 3 was also detected in one chronically colonised patient in the Spanish centre. More studies in other centres are needed to search for eventual links between ST and persistence in the respiratory tract. Indeed, the impact of colonisation with <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> spp. on lung function degradation in CF patients is still unclear. <ce:italic>A. xylosoxidans</ce:italic> is likely the species the most involved in CF patients colonisation, but its pathogenicity remains to be confirmed. Clinical studies are needed and might help detecting potential more pathogenic species or STs associated with more virulence and determining if they should be eradicated since early colonisations. The results of these studies will help establishing guidelines for the management of <ce:italic>Achromobacter</ce:italic> infections that are not currently available.</ce:para>
               </ce:section>
